Query: Role of microRNAs and epigenetic regulators in modulating lysosomal biogenesis, acidification, and cathepsin D expression in retinal pigment epithelium under dry age-related macular degeneration conditions, including identification of miRNA targets, chromatin modifications at lysosomal gene loci, and impact on RPE autophagic flux and viability in vitro and in vivo models

MicroRNAs shape multiple nodes of lysosomal biogenesis and endolysosomal maturation in RPE under AMD-like stress. In vivo, loss of miR-211 impairs lysosomal biogenesis and degradative capacity, leading to accumulation of poorly processed photoreceptor outer segments (POS); miR-211 activation induces autophagy and rescues lysosomal anomalies by repressing Ezrin, enhancing TRPML1-dependent Ca2+ microdomains, activating calcineurin, and promoting TFEB nuclear translocation to drive lysosomal/autophagy gene expression (targets/pathway: Ezrin → TRPML1–Ca2+ → calcineurin → TFEB) (intartaglia2021theimpactof pages 8-10, intartaglia2021theimpactof pages 5-7). miR-204 loss elevates Rab22a, blocks endosomal maturation and phagolysosome activity, and causes rhodopsin/POS accumulation in vivo (target: RAB22A) (intartaglia2021theimpactof pages 8-10, intartaglia2021theimpactof pages 10-11). Additional miRNAs converge on clearance: oxidative stress via STAT3 elevates miR-25 to target ITGAV and PEDF, suppressing phagocytosis; subretinal antago‑miR‑25 restores phagocytosis and mitigates RPE degeneration in a sodium‑iodate model (targets: ITGAV, PEDF) (intartaglia2021theimpactof pages 8-10). miR‑184 inhibition upregulates Ezrin and reduces phagocytosis; miR‑410 inhibition enhances phagocytosis; miR‑30 represses Beclin1/ATG5 (autophagy initiation); miR‑24 targets CHI3L1 to activate AKT/mTOR–ERK and perturb autophagy; miR‑29 targets LAMTOR/p18 (Ragulator) linking miRNA control to lysosomal mTORC1 signaling; miR‑617 (MYO7A) affects lysosome movement; miR‑146a represses IL‑6/VEGF‑A and inactivates NF‑κB, integrating inflammatory cues with autophagy/lysosome function (intartaglia2021theimpactof pages 8-10, intartaglia2021theimpactof pages 10-11, intartaglia2021theimpactof pages 13-14).

Master transcriptional regulators TFEB/MITF directly activate lysosomal/autophagy programs, including V‑ATPase subunits (acidification machinery) and cathepsin D (CTSD), establishing a mechanistic route from upstream miRNA–signaling axes to acidification capacity and hydrolase expression (ozturk2019mechanismsofautophagy pages 62-66). TFEB nuclear entry is controlled by mTORC1 phosphorylation and by lysosomal Ca2+–calcineurin downstream of MCOLN1/TRPML1; similar regulation applies to MITF/TFE3 (ozturk2019mechanismsofautophagy pages 54-59, ozturk2019mechanismsofautophagy pages 48-54). In non‑RPE systems, MIR211 downregulates RICTOR to inhibit mTORC2–AKT–mTORC1, promoting TFEB/MITF nuclear translocation and an autophagy-amplifying loop, supporting a miRNA→mTOR→TFE/MITF→lysosome gene pathway that could operate in RPE (ozturk2019mechanismsofautophagy pages 13-15, ozturk2019mechanismsofautophagy pages 1-9, ozturk2019mechanismsofautophagy pages 156-164).

Functional impacts on RPE flux/viability are documented: miR‑211 activation restores autophagy/lysosomal biogenesis; miR‑204−/− shows phagolysosomal blockade; antago‑miR‑25 rescues phagocytosis and reduces RPE degeneration in vivo (intartaglia2021theimpactof pages 8-10). AMD RPE exhibits marked Dicer1 reduction and RPE degeneration in conditional knockouts, indicating perturbed small RNA homeostasis, though degeneration was attributed to Alu RNA toxicity rather than global miRNA loss (intartaglia2021theimpactof pages 10-11).

Evidence gaps: the excerpts do not report RPE/AMD-specific measurements of lysosomal acidification or CTSD levels/activity, nor chromatin/epigenetic modifications at lysosomal gene loci; while MITF/TFEB occupancy and targets imply control of acidification and CTSD, RPE-directed evidence is lacking (ozturk2019mechanismsofautophagy pages 62-66, intartaglia2021theimpactof pages 13-14, intartaglia2021theimpactof pages 10-11). Recommended assays to close these gaps in RPE models include LC3‑II/p62 turnover, GFP‑LC3–RFP flux reporters and GFP‑LC3/LAMP1 colocalization, LysoTracker for acidification, and MagicRed cathepsin activity, favoring genetic over prolonged pharmacologic lysosomal inhibition to avoid toxicity (kloimbock2024theroleof pages 72-75).

In RPE under dry‑AMD–like stress, specific miRNAs (notably miR‑211, miR‑204, miR‑25, miR‑184, miR‑30, miR‑24, miR‑29) rewire endolysosomal maturation and autophagic flux via defined targets and upstream signaling, with in vivo rescue data for miR‑25 and loss‑of‑function phenotypes for miR‑211/miR‑204 (intartaglia2021theimpactof pages 8-10, intartaglia2021theimpactof pages 10-11). TFEB/MITF directly regulate V‑ATPase and CTSD, linking transcriptional programs to acidification and cathepsin D, but RPE‑specific acidification, CTSD, and chromatin‑modification readouts are not provided in the excerpt (ozturk2019mechanismsofautophagy pages 62-66). Robust lysosome/flux assays can establish these missing endpoints in dry‑AMD‑relevant RPE systems (kloimbock2024theroleof pages 72-75).

In RPE, miRNAs control lysosomal biogenesis and phagolysosome function through targets such as Ezrin, RAB22A, ITGAV/PEDF, LAMTOR/p18 and CHI3L1, altering autophagic flux and, in vivo, RPE degeneration phenotypes (intartaglia2021theimpactof pages 8-10, intartaglia2021theimpactof pages 10-11). TFEB/MITF directly target V‑ATPase subunits and CTSD, providing a mechanistic route to acidification and cathepsin D, but RPE/AMD‑specific acidification, CTSD activity, and chromatin‑modification data are not available here (ozturk2019mechanismsofautophagy pages 62-66). Standardized flux and lysosome assays are suitable to fill these gaps in AMD‑relevant RPE models (kloimbock2024theroleof pages 72-75).
