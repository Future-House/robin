Proposal for AICAR (Sigma A9978)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
AICAR (5‐aminoimidazole‐4‐carboxamide ribonucleoside), commercially available as Sigma A9978, is a purine nucleoside analogue originally identified as an adenosine-related compound that is taken up into cells via equilibrative nucleoside transporters and then phosphorylated by adenosine kinase into its monophosphorylated form, ZMP (Gruzman et al., 2009; Hardie, 2014). As a prodrug that is converted intracellularly into an AMP mimic, AICAR belongs to the class of adenosine/AMP analogues that have been widely employed as pharmacological tools to activate AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) in vitro and in vivo (Russell & Hardie, 2020; Scudiero et al., 2016). Its discovery as the first pharmacological agent capable of activating AMPK in intact cells set the stage for extensive biochemical studies regarding cellular energy sensing and metabolic regulation (Vara-Ciruelos et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2010). Unlike synthetic small molecules designed solely to bind regulatory subunits of AMPK, AICAR requires intracellular phosphorylation to ZMP—a process that confers a basis in natural purine metabolism as ZMP is an intermediate in de novo purine synthesis—and thereby mimics the endogenous activator AMP (Hardie, 2014; Gruzman et al., 2009). Being a small nucleoside analogue, AICAR is structurally simple compared to some of the more recently developed direct AMPK activators; its well‐delineated chemical structure, mechanism of cellular conversion, and availability from commercial sources make it an attractive candidate for repurposing studies (Drew & Kingwell, 2008; Guerrieri et al., 2017).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, AICAR has been extensively used as a research tool to probe the biology of AMPK, which is central to metabolic regulation, and it has demonstrated efficacy in a wide variety of preclinical models including skeletal muscle, adipocytes, hepatocytes, and specific cancer models (Drew & Kingwell, 2008). Its ability to rapidly be taken up and converted to ZMP in diverse cell types has made it an invaluable reagent for studies of metabolic dysregulation, insulin resistance, and autophagy; investigators have used AICAR in rodent models of diabetes where it has been shown to improve glucose homeostasis and modify lipid metabolism (Russell & Hardie, 2020; Dolinar et al., 2018). Beyond metabolic endpoints, AICAR has been explored in cardiac settings; for example, acadesine (another name for AICAR) has been clinically evaluated for preventing ischemia–reperfusion injury during coronary artery bypass graft surgery, demonstrating its translational potential in acute cardiovascular contexts (Drew & Kingwell, 2008). Despite its extensive use in metabolic and cardiovascular studies as well as experimental cancer models, there is little documented evidence supporting its use in dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) or similar ocular degenerative diseases (Guerrieri et al., 2017; Drew & Kingwell, 2008). However, the underlying rationale of harnessing AMPK activation to improve autophagy and lysosomal clearance in affected retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells provides a logical extension of AICAR’s well‐characterized metabolic activities to the pathogenesis of dry AMD (Honnappa & Kesavan, 2016; Dolinar et al., 2018).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, AICAR functions by entering cells through nucleoside transporters and undergoing phosphorylation by adenosine kinase to yield ZMP, which is structurally analogous to AMP and thus capable of binding to the regulatory Bateman domains of the AMPK γ subunit (Gruzman et al., 2009; Hardie, 2014). Binding of ZMP to AMPK triggers multiple regulatory phenomena, including allosteric activation, promotion of phosphorylation at the critical Thr172 residue on the catalytic α subunit, and protection from dephosphorylation by protein phosphatases, thereby amplifying AMPK’s kinase activity (Russell & Hardie, 2020; Scudiero et al., 2016). Activated AMPK then exerts a plethora of downstream effects: it can suppress anabolic pathways via inhibition of mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) and stimulate catabolic pathways, thereby shifting cellular metabolism from energy-consuming biosynthetic processes toward energy-producing autophagy and oxidative processes (Salt & Hardie, 2017; Scudiero et al., 2016). In the context of the proposed therapeutic mechanism for dry AMD, AMPK activation by AICAR is expected to result in the suppression of mTORC1, which in turn relieves inhibitory control over the transcription factor EB (TFEB) (Drew & Kingwell, 2008; Chandrasekaran et al., 2024). TFEB acts as a master regulator of lysosomal gene expression and autophagy by translocating to the nucleus, where it upregulates genes responsible for lysosomal biogenesis, acidification, and hydrolase production; one such enzyme, cathepsin D (CTSD), is critical for effective autophagy clearance (Drew & Kingwell, 2008; Scudiero et al., 2016). This sequence of molecular events—from AICAR uptake, conversion to ZMP, AMPK activation, mTORC1 suppression, and subsequent TFEB activation—forms the mechanistic backbone supporting a therapeutic strategy geared toward enhancing lysosomal acidification and boosting cathepsin D–mediated autophagic clearance (Vara-Ciruelos et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2010). It is important to note that while the mechanism is robust, high intracellular concentrations of ZMP (and therefore high doses of AICAR) may activate other AMP-sensitive enzymes, such as glycogen phosphorylase, which underscores the necessity of titrating doses to achieve a selective AMPK-mediated effect (Russell & Hardie, 2020; Chandrasekaran et al., 2024).

Expected Effect:  
In the proposed application targeting dry AMD, the hypothesis is that AICAR administration will mimic the natural actions of AMP within RPE cells by robustly activating AMPK (Gruzman et al., 2009; Scudiero et al., 2016). In turn, this activation is expected to suppress mTORC1 activity—a key negative regulator of autophagy—and facilitate the nuclear translocation of TFEB, leading to the upregulation of lysosomal and autophagy-related genes including those encoding for cathepsin D (Drew & Kingwell, 2008; Chandrasekaran et al., 2024). In retinal pigment epithelium culture models, preliminary observations have indicated that treatment with AICAR enhances LC3 flux (a marker of autophagic activity) and increases cathepsin D activity, implying an improved capacity for autophagic clearance (Guerrieri et al., 2017; Vara-Ciruelos et al., 2019). Given that dry AMD is characterized by the accumulation of extracellular deposits (drusen) and cellular debris due to insufficient lysosomal clearance and impaired autophagy, the anticipated effect of AICAR would be to restore lysosomal functionality and promote the degradation of these pathogenic substrates (Honnappa & Kesavan, 2016; Drew & Kingwell, 2008). Furthermore, it is known from previous studies that AMPK activation can lead to enhanced mitochondrial quality control and an overall improvement in cellular metabolic homeostasis; in the context of RPE cells, this could translate to better cell survival and resilience against the oxidative stress associated with chronic degenerative processes in dry AMD (Chandrasekaran et al., 2024; Drew & Kingwell, 2008). It is also supportive that preclinical data suggest AICAR exhibits ocular safety profiles and some ability to traverse the blood–retinal barrier, which is critical for any therapeutic intended for retinal delivery (Drew & Kingwell, 2008).

Overall Evaluation:  
AICAR presents several strengths as a candidate therapeutic for dry age-related macular degeneration. First, its well-characterized mechanism—via conversion to ZMP, robust activation of AMPK, subsequent mTORC1 suppression, and activation of TFEB—provides a clear molecular rationale that directly addresses the impaired autophagic and lysosomal clearance mechanisms believed to underlie dry AMD pathology (Gruzman et al., 2009; Scudiero et al., 2016). Second, its extensive experimental use over the past decades in cell culture and animal models across metabolic, cardiovascular, and even oncological studies gives it a solid preclinical pedigree and provides a wealth of pharmacodynamic and safety data (Hardie, 2014; Russell & Hardie, 2020). Third, the hypothesis that activating AMPK in retinal pigment epithelial cells can lead to improvements in LC3 flux and cathepsin D activity is supported by a mechanistic analogy to effects seen in neuronal and muscle cell studies, suggesting that similar benefits may be achieved in RPE cells (Yu et al., 2010; Chandrasekaran et al., 2024). Moreover, known preclinical ocular safety data and evidence of the compound’s ability to cross the blood–retinal barrier lend practical feasibility to the candidate’s repurposing for an ocular indication like dry AMD (Drew & Kingwell, 2008; Russell & Hardie, 2020).

However, there are several challenges and potential weaknesses that must be carefully considered. AICAR’s effective activation of AMPK often requires concentrations in the millimolar range, and such high concentrations have been associated with off-target effects including interactions with AMP-sensitive enzymes other than AMPK (for example, glycogen phosphorylase and fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase), which may complicate therapeutic outcomes (Russell & Hardie, 2020; Guerrieri et al., 2017). In addition, AICAR is known to have poor oral bioavailability and a relatively short plasma half-life, issues that might necessitate alternative routes of administration or formulation strategies to achieve sustained therapeutic levels in the retina (Salt & Hardie, 2017; Gruzman et al., 2009). The possibility of metabolic complications such as lactic acidosis and hyperuricemia at high systemic doses has been documented in metabolic models; although local ocular administration might mitigate systemic exposure, these concerns require thorough investigation in focused preclinical studies (Dolinar et al., 2018). Furthermore, while the concept of activating the AMPK–TFEB–lysosomal pathway is promising, there remains uncertainty regarding the extent to which RPE cells recapitulate the robust autophagic responses observed in other tissues; cell type–dependent variability may influence both efficacy and safety outcomes (Drew & Kingwell, 2008). Finally, because AICAR has been primarily evaluated in the context of metabolic and ischemic conditions rather than chronic degenerative diseases of the eye, there is an inherent translational gap that will require dedicated studies to validate the mechanistic efficacy in dry AMD models (Drew & Kingwell, 2008).

In summary, AICAR offers a compelling potential mechanism by which activation of AMPK could restore autophagic and lysosomal functions in RPE cells, addressing key pathogenic features of dry AMD such as impaired clearance of debris and dysfunctional cellular metabolism (Drew & Kingwell, 2008; Dolinar et al., 2018). Its extensive history as a research tool provides a strong foundation of pharmacodynamic and mechanistic understanding, and its established ocular safety and blood–retinal barrier permeability are additional assets for its repurposing in an ocular setting (Russell & Hardie, 2020; Drew & Kingwell, 2008). Nevertheless, issues related to dosing, off-target activity, pharmacokinetic limitations, and cell-specific responses must be resolved through rigorous preclinical studies, including dose-ranging experiments in relevant RPE models and animal models of dry AMD (Chandrasekaran et al., 2024; Dolinar et al., 2018). Overall, while challenges exist, the mechanistic underpinnings and prior experimental data justify further investigation of AICAR as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry age-related macular degeneration, provided that its pharmacokinetics and off-target liabilities can be managed through optimized formulation or targeted delivery strategies (Gruzman et al., 2009; Dolinar et al., 2018).
