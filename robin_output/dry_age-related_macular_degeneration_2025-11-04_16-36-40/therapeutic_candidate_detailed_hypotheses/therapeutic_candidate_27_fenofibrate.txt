Proposal for Fenofibrate (Sigma F6020)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Fenofibrate is a synthetic small‐molecule drug belonging to the fibrate class of compounds, originally developed and approved for the treatment of dyslipidemia. It acts predominantly as a peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor alpha (PPARα) agonist. Fenofibrate itself is a prodrug that undergoes hepatic esterase hydrolysis to form its active metabolite, fenofibric acid, which binds to PPARα and modulates the transcription of genes involved in lipid metabolism. This drug class has historically been used for lowering serum triglycerides and increasing high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, reflecting its well‐established lipid‐modulating properties. Although fenofibrate was not initially developed for ocular indications, its mechanism as a PPARα agonist has spurred interest for repurposing in retinal diseases. In particular, the broad class of fibrates has garnered attention because PPARα is expressed in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), and its activation can regulate lipid oxidation, autophagy, and inflammatory processes—all of which are critically disturbed in dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) (Fu et al., 2019; Falcão et al., 2025).

Therapeutic History:  
Fenofibrate has a robust clinical history in the management of dyslipidemia and cardiovascular risk reduction, with extensive use in millions of patients worldwide. Its ocular benefits became apparent with large clinical trials, such as the FIELD and ACCORD Eye studies, which demonstrated that fenofibrate can reduce the progression of diabetic retinopathy and decrease the need for retinal laser therapy in diabetic patients (Lu et al., 2014; Treacy & Hurst, 2012). Although these trials primarily addressed diabetic retinal disease, subsequent preclinical and clinical reports have indicated that the retina may benefit from the lipid‐modulatory, anti‐inflammatory, and neuroprotective actions of PPARα agonists. For instance, several studies have explored fenofibrate in animal models of retinal ischemia and neovascularization, showing protective effects on the retinal vasculature and RPE (Fu et al., 2020; Qiu et al., 2017). While the majority of these ocular studies have focused on diabetic retinopathy or neovascular AMD, emerging literature has pointed to the potential of targeting PPARα signaling to mitigate RPE dysfunction and lipid accumulation, which are hallmarks of dry AMD (da Ana et al., 2023; Dou & Duerfeldt, 2020). This history of fenofibrate in retinal disease, combined with its proven clinical safety for dyslipidemia and established ocular pharmacokinetics (including studies on topical delivery), provides a compelling rationale for its repurposing in dry AMD (Tomita et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2021).

Mechanism of Action:  
Fenofibrate’s mechanism of action is rooted in its role as a PPARα agonist. Upon conversion to fenofibric acid, the compound binds to PPARα—a nuclear receptor highly expressed in several tissues, including the liver and the retinal pigment epithelium. When activated, PPARα forms heterodimers with retinoid X receptors (RXR) and binds to peroxisome proliferator response elements (PPREs) within the promoters of target genes. This binding results in the upregulation of genes critical for fatty acid uptake, β-oxidation, and lipid catabolism. Key genes regulated by PPARα include those encoding enzymes that facilitate mitochondrial and peroxisomal fatty acid oxidation, which in turn reduces intracellular lipid accumulation and promotes energy homeostasis (Fu et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2023).

In the context of the RPE and dry AMD, the hypothesis is that fenofibrate will stimulate PPARα-mediated transcriptional programs that not only enhance lipid catabolism but also intersect with cellular degradative pathways. One of the central tenets of the proposed mechanism is the coactivation of transcription factor EB (TFEB). TFEB is recognized as a master regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy through the coordinated lysosomal expression and regulation (CLEAR) network. Under normal conditions, TFEB is kept inactive in the cytosol by phosphorylation mediated by mTORC1. Under stress conditions or when lysosomal function is impaired, TFEB translocates to the nucleus to upregulate genes involved in lysosomal acidification, autophagy, and lipid metabolism. The therapeutic hypothesis posits that fenofibrate, through PPARα activation in RPE cells, will indirectly coactivate TFEB. This may occur through changes in cellular metabolism that reduce mTORC1 activity or via direct transcriptional crosstalk between PPARα target genes and the CLEAR network (Falcão et al., 2025; da Ana et al., 2023).

Moreover, TFEB activation is expected to enhance lysosomal acidification—a process necessary for optimal degradation of phagocytosed outer segment debris and drusen precursors—and upregulate lysosomal hydrolases such as cathepsin D. In AMD, RPE cells often show lysosomal dysfunction characterized by deficient cathepsin D activity and lipofuscin accumulation. By improving lysosomal function, fenofibrate may facilitate the clearance of lipid-rich drusen precursors, thereby reducing RPE stress and preventing progression of dry AMD (Falcão et al., 2025; da Ana et al., 2023). In addition to these lysosome-centric effects, fenofibrate has anti-inflammatory properties, as PPARα activation suppresses NF-κB signaling, reduces the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α and IL-1β), and alleviates oxidative stress—all of which are exacerbating factors in the pathogenesis of dry AMD (Lee et al., 2023; Treacy & Hurst, 2012). Furthermore, the modulation of the FGF21 pathway, occasionally observed with fenofibrate treatment, may contribute to neuroprotection and further support retinal homeostasis (Tomita et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2023). Taken together, the molecular interactions initiated by fenofibrate converge on restoring lipid homeostasis, enhancing autophagic flux, and mitigating inflammatory insults in RPE cells—a multifaceted approach that aligns with the pathophysiology of dry AMD (Treacy & Hurst, 2012; Lee et al., 2021).

Expected Effect:  
In the assays proposed to evaluate fenofibrate’s efficacy for dry AMD, one anticipates several measurable cellular and biochemical outcomes. First, treatment of RPE cells with fenofibrate should lead to a consistent activation of PPARα target genes, as demonstrated by upregulation of markers for fatty acid oxidation and lipid catabolism. Such molecular signatures can be quantified using gene expression analyses and reporter assays for PPRE activity (Fu et al., 2019; Tomita et al., 2021). Concurrently, it is expected that fenofibrate will enhance autophagic flux in RPE cells. This could be evident from increased LC3-II accumulation in the presence of lysosomal inhibitors and enhanced degradation of autophagic substrates, which would indicate an upregulation of the autophagic machinery (Falcão et al., 2025; da Ana et al., 2023).

A critical aspect of the proposed mechanism involves TFEB. Upon fenofibrate treatment, one should observe increased nuclear translocation of TFEB in RPE cells, which can be assessed by immunocytochemistry and subcellular fractionation. The activation of TFEB would then be expected to drive the transcriptional upregulation of genes encoding lysosomal proteins, such as lysosomal-associated membrane protein 2 (LAMP2) and various components of the V-ATPase complex, leading to enhanced lysosomal acidification. Lysotracker assays and measurements of intralysosomal pH could be deployed to confirm that fenofibrate-treated cells exhibit more acidic lysosomes (Falcão et al., 2025; da Ana et al., 2023).

Furthermore, increased cathepsin D activity is a pivotal expected outcome, serving as a surrogate marker for improved lysosomal enzyme function in degrading waste products, including drusen precursors. Biochemical assays to quantify cathepsin D activity, alongside immunostaining for its protein levels, would help to validate this aspect of the mechanism. Functionally, the cumulative effect of these molecular changes should be a reduction in the accumulation of lipofuscin and other drusen-associated lipid-protein aggregates within the RPE. This reduction could be observed using autofluorescence imaging techniques and electron microscopy to assess cytoplasmic inclusions (Falcão et al., 2025; Treacy & Hurst, 2012).

Additionally, given that fenofibrate has established anti-inflammatory properties, one might expect a decrease in the secretion or expression of inflammatory mediators in RPE cultures, as well as improved cellular resistance to oxidative stress challenges. Cytokine assays and measurements of reactive oxygen species (ROS) would serve as corroborative evidence for an anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effect. Importantly, because reduced autophagy and lysosomal dysfunction are central to dry AMD, the combined effect of enhanced lipid catabolism, improved lysosomal acidification, and increased cathepsin D activity should contribute to an overall reduction in cellular stress and preservation of RPE integrity (Falcão et al., 2025; Treacy & Hurst, 2012). Taken together, these expected effects would validate the therapeutic hypothesis and provide a strong rationale for advancing fenofibrate into further preclinical models of dry AMD.

Overall Evaluation:  
Fenofibrate emerges as a promising repurposed drug candidate for dry AMD based on several compelling lines of evidence. The drug’s long-established clinical safety profile and extensive use in dyslipidemia serve as major strengths in terms of its translational potential (Lu et al., 2014; Treacy & Hurst, 2012). Additionally, the documented success of fenofibrate in slowing the progression of diabetic retinopathy in large-scale clinical trials underscores its ability to exert beneficial effects in retinal tissues (Lu et al., 2014; Treacy & Hurst, 2012).

From a mechanistic standpoint, fenofibrate’s mode of action as a PPARα agonist is well characterized, and its ability to stimulate lipid oxidation and enhance fatty acid metabolism could directly address the lipid dysregulation observed in RPE cells in dry AMD (Fu et al., 2019; da Ana et al., 2023). Crucially, the hypothesis that fenofibrate may also coactivate TFEB, thereby promoting lysosomal biogenesis and function, positions it uniquely among therapeutic candidates. Improved lysosomal acidification and increased cathepsin D activity would facilitate the clearance of drusen precursors and reduce the cytotoxic accumulation of lipofuscin, which is a central pathogenic feature of dry AMD (Falcão et al., 2025; da Ana et al., 2023; Dou & Duerfeldt, 2020). The convergence of lipid catabolism enhancement, autophagy upregulation, and anti-inflammatory effects creates a multifaceted therapeutic profile that is particularly attractive for a complex, multifactorial disease such as dry AMD.

However, there are also notable weaknesses and challenges. Most of the current evidence supporting fenofibrate’s retinal benefits is derived from studies focusing on diabetic retinopathy or neovascular AMD rather than directly on dry AMD. Although there is supportive mechanistic rationale linking PPARα activation to improved lysosomal function in the RPE, direct evidence for fenofibrate’s efficacy in reducing drusen accumulation and alleviating RPE stress in dry AMD models remains sparse (Dou & Duerfeldt, 2020; da Ana et al., 2023). The specific involvement of TFEB activation by fenofibrate in RPE cells has not been fully elucidated in the existing literature, and further in vitro and in vivo studies are needed to validate this aspect of the mechanism. Additionally, while fenofibrate is generally safe when used systemically, there have been concerns regarding potential renal side effects, which may be particularly relevant in the elderly population that predominantly suffers from AMD (Treacy & Hurst, 2012; Tomita et al., 2021). Thus, optimizing the route of delivery—possibly via topical formulations or nanoparticle-based systems—may be necessary to ensure sufficient retinal bioavailability while minimizing systemic exposure (Huang et al., 2021; Lu et al., 2014).

Another potential limitation is the dose required to achieve sufficient PPARα activation in the RPE to modulate TFEB and lysosomal pathways effectively. Preclinical studies have sometimes required relatively high doses of fenofibrate to observe significant ocular effects, and the pharmacokinetic barriers posed by the blood-retinal barrier may further complicate translatability (Tomita et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2023). Moreover, the current body of literature underscores the need for more targeted studies to determine whether the benefits seen with fenofibrate in models of diabetic retinopathy and neovascular AMD can be replicated in models of dry AMD, where the pathogenic processes are distinct in their emphasis on lipid accumulation, oxidative stress, and RPE dysfunction (da Ana et al., 2023; Dou & Duerfeldt, 2020).

Despite these challenges, the overall evaluation remains cautiously optimistic. Fenofibrate’s mechanism of action aligns well with the pathophysiological features of dry AMD, particularly regarding the restoration of lipid homeostasis and the enhancement of lysosomal function. The dual potential for direct metabolic benefits via PPARα activation and indirect benefits through TFEB-mediated lysosomal enhancement represents an innovative therapeutic strategy that could fill a critical gap in the current treatment landscape for dry AMD (Falcão et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2019; da Ana et al., 2023; Dou & Duerfeldt, 2020; Lee et al., 2023). Additionally, the availability of various delivery platforms—including systemic administration, topical eye drops, and nanoparticle formulations—provides flexibility in addressing bioavailability challenges and minimizing adverse effects (Huang et al., 2021; Lu et al., 2014).

In conclusion, fenofibrate (Sigma F6020) possesses significant potential for repurposing as a drug candidate for dry AMD based on its well-established pharmacological profile, robust clinical safety record, and mechanistic actions that target key pathological processes in the RPE. While further preclinical studies specifically focused on dry AMD models are warranted to validate the hypothesized coactivation of TFEB and subsequent enhancement of lysosomal acidification and cathepsin D activity, the current literature provides a strong scientific basis for such investigations. The strengths of fenofibrate lie in its multifaceted mechanism—encompassing lipid catabolism, autophagy, anti-inflammatory, and possibly neuroprotective effects—which collectively offer a promising strategy to reduce drusen precursors and mitigate RPE stress. The primary weaknesses involve the need for direct evidence in dry AMD models, optimization of ocular delivery to overcome pharmacokinetic barriers, and careful monitoring of potential systemic side effects in an elderly population. Overall, fenofibrate represents a compelling and scientifically sound candidate that merits further exploration in the context of dry AMD, with the potential to significantly advance our therapeutic arsenal against this currently untreatable form of macular degeneration (Falcão et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2019; da Ana et al., 2023; Dou & Duerfeldt, 2020; Lee et al., 2023).
