Proposal for Harmine (Sigma H1392)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Harmine is a naturally occurring β-carboline alkaloid that is abundant in Peganum harmala L., and has been investigated for neuropharmacological activities including acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibition, antioxidant activity, and anti-inflammatory effects, with related β-carbolines such as harmaline frequently studied alongside it (Singh et al., 2024). As an alkaloid, harmine belongs to a class of nitrogen-containing, basic, often polycyclic natural products that have historically provided a rich source of neuroactive scaffolds and approved therapeutics across neurodegenerative diseases (NDDs), with the literature situating harmine within a broader set of “neuroprotective alkaloids” considered for NDD drug discovery (Singh et al., 2024). In preclinical pharmacology, water-soluble salt forms such as harmine hydrochloride have been used, and medicinal chemistry analogs with higher potency than the parent have been reported, supporting tractability for formulation and structure–activity optimization (Singh et al., 2024).

The mechanistic disease context relevant to dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is dominated by lysosomal and autophagy dysfunction in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), where impaired lysosomal degradation, phagosome–lysosome fusion, and autophagic flux promote lipofuscin accumulation, oxidative stress, complement/inflammasome activation, and RPE death (Boya et al., 2023; Kaarniranta et al., 2023). Master regulators of lysosomal biogenesis and degradative capacity include TFEB/TFE3, which are controlled by mTORC1, AMPK, Ca2+/calcineurin, and lysosomal signaling nodes; TFEB drives expression of the CLEAR gene network to expand lysosomal/autophagy capacity and has been repeatedly highlighted as a central therapeutic axis for restoring RPE degradative function (Boya et al., 2023). The CLEAR program directly impacts lysosomal membrane proteins such as LAMP2 and proteases like cathepsins, and TFEB activity is constrained by nutrient-sensing mTORC1 signaling on the lysosomal surface (Boya et al., 2023). Because LAMP2 deficiency impairs photoreceptor outer segment (POS) degradation and yields AMD-like phenotypes, and cathepsin D (CTSD) is the primary POS protease in RPE, TFEB-mediated enhancement of these systems is mechanistically aligned with dry AMD pathobiology (Boya et al., 2023; Si et al., 2023; Kaarniranta et al., 2013). Note, however, that the provided evidence base contains no direct data linking harmine to TFEB activation, CLEAR gene induction, or DYRK1A inhibition in RPE or neural cells; those specific connections are absent in the cited retinal/lysosome literature excerpts (Boya et al., 2023).

Therapeutic History  
No registered clinical trials were identified on ClinicalTrials.gov for the search terms “Harmine retinal pigment epithelium” or “Harmine dry age-related macular degeneration,” indicating no publicly registered clinical evaluation of harmine for dry AMD or closely related RPE indications under those exact phrases (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.). The negative registry findings were reiterated in independent queries and associated summaries, which emphasize that these are specific to the exact search terms and this registry, and do not exclude preclinical studies, trials registered elsewhere, or studies indexed under alternative nomenclature; nonetheless, they demonstrate a lack of registered clinical activity for harmine in dry AMD on this platform (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.).

Outside ophthalmology, harmine has a preclinical pharmacology history in neurodegenerative disease models, including antiparkinsonian activity comparable in some studies to levodopa, anti-cataleptogenic effects in haloperidol models at 2.5–5 mg/kg, and reduction of MPTP-induced oligokinesia, with some analogs achieving desired effects at 2 mg/kg compared with 20 mg/kg for harmine (Singh et al., 2024). Mechanistically, harmine promotes time- and dose-dependent degradation of α-synuclein species via upregulation of the PSMD1 subunit of the ubiquitin–proteasome system and enhanced PKA phosphorylation, supporting a proteostasis-modulating mechanism in CNS models (Singh et al., 2024). Additional pharmacology reported in the literature includes MAO and AChE inhibition in preclinical models, consistent with multi-target neuromodulatory activity of β-carbolines (Singh et al., 2024). The excerpts provided here do not document any prior use of harmine in ocular disease models or clinical settings for retinal disease, nor do they offer ocular pharmacokinetic/tissue exposure or ocular tolerability data (Singh et al., 2024).

Mechanism of Action  
Known pharmacology of harmine in the supplied materials centers on: (1) enhancement of proteasome-dependent clearance of aggregation-prone proteins through upregulation of the PSMD1 subunit and PKA-mediated mechanisms, leading to time- and dose-dependent degradation of α-synuclein species; and (2) inhibition of monoamine oxidase (MAO) and acetylcholinesterase (AChE), along with reported antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects in diverse preclinical systems (Singh et al., 2024). These activities position harmine as a multi-modal agent influencing proteostasis and neurotransmitter pathways in the CNS (Singh et al., 2024).

By contrast, the specific hypothesis under evaluation—that harmine will inhibit DYRK1A in RPE, increase TFEB dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation, and thereby promote CLEAR-driven lysosomal biogenesis, acidification, and CTSD activity—is not supported by direct evidence in the provided context; the retinal/lysosomal references explicitly discuss TFEB/TFE3 regulation, RPE lysosome biology, and autophagy dysfunction in AMD, but do not report DYRK1A involvement or link harmine to TFEB modulation (Boya et al., 2023). The mechanistic literature excerpts instead highlight established regulatory routes by which TFEB/TFE3 control lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy: mTORC1-dependent phosphorylation prevents TFEB nuclear import, whereas AMPK activation and lysosomal Ca2+/calcineurin signaling can promote TFEB/TFE3 dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation to induce the CLEAR program (Boya et al., 2023). In the RPE, upstream lysosomal amino-acid sensing via V-ATPase–Ragulator–Rag GTPases coordinates mTORC1 activity at the lysosomal membrane, and disruptions in these nodes (e.g., βA3/A1-crystallin deficits) impair TFEB/TFE3 nuclear translocation and CLEAR gene expression, contributing to AMD-like phenotypes (Boya et al., 2023; Sinha et al., 2016).

The RPE’s degradative workload is extraordinary—daily heterophagy of shed photoreceptor outer segments and continuous autophagy—so lysosomal membrane proteins and hydrolytic enzyme systems are critical to homeostasis: LAMP2 is a TFEB/CLEAR target whose deficiency delays POS degradation and produces AMD-like retinal changes, and CTSD is the principal lysosomal protease mediating POS/rhodopsin degradation; impaired phagosome–lysosome fusion and elevated lysosomal pH (e.g., due to A2E or lipofuscin) reduce cathepsin activity and drive lipofuscinogenesis and RPE degeneration (Boya et al., 2023; Si et al., 2023; Kaarniranta et al., 2013). Consequences of lysosomal failure include LMP with cytosolic release of cathepsins, DAMP emission, complement activation, and necroptotic cell death; interventions that stabilize lysosomal membranes (e.g., Hsp70 induction) or normalize pH have restored lysosomal function and reduced retinal degeneration in preclinical models, underscoring the therapeutic relevance of lysosome-centric strategies (Boya et al., 2023). Therapeutic interventions that restore lysosomal enzyme function (e.g., cathepsin D gene therapy) and enhance CMA or autophagy have also attenuated retinal degeneration and preserved visual function in vivo, further supporting this axis (Boya et al., 2023).

In summary, while TFEB activation and lysosomal rejuvenation are compelling, disease-relevant mechanisms for dry AMD, the materials provided do not document harmine as a DYRK1A inhibitor in RPE, or as an agent that increases TFEB nuclear translocation, CLEAR gene expression, LAMP1/2 abundance, or CTSD activity in ocular systems; these specific mechanistic links remain unsubstantiated in this evidence set (Boya et al., 2023).

Expected Effect  
If the hypothesized mechanism were correct—that harmine inhibits a TFEB-restraining kinase and thereby drives TFEB nuclear translocation and CLEAR gene activation in RPE—the expected cellular outcomes would include increased lysosomal biogenesis, improved lysosomal acidification and ion homeostasis, higher LAMP2/LAMP1 expression, augmented cathepsin content and activity (including CTSD), enhanced autophagic flux, and improved phagosome–lysosome fusion and degradation of POS, together leading to reduced lipofuscin burden, mitigation of LMP, and protection from necroptosis and chronic inflammation (Boya et al., 2023; Si et al., 2023). TFEB is the master transcriptional regulator of the CLEAR program, and its dephosphorylation/nuclear localization induces expression of genes that collectively increase lysosomal number and degradative competence; in the RPE, CLEAR-dependent targets include LAMP2, whose deficiency causes delayed POS clearance and AMD-like degeneration (Boya et al., 2023). A direct functional prediction is that promoting TFEB activity would increase lysosome number and cathepsin activity; indeed, pharmacologic Ezrin inhibition—through a distinct regulatory route—has been shown to increase lysosomal number and cathepsin activity in RPE, reduce vision loss, and thus serves as proof-of-concept that boosting lysosomal protease capacity is salutary in retinal disease models (Boya et al., 2023). Because CTSD is the primary POS protease in RPE and is inhibited by elevated lysosomal pH or phototoxic lipofuscin constituents such as A2E, a TFEB-driven program that improves acidification and enzymatic capacity would be predicted to restore POS processing and limit lipofuscin accumulation (Si et al., 2023; Kaarniranta et al., 2013; Boya et al., 2023).

Autophagy and mitophagy readouts should similarly improve: TFEB/CLEAR activation increases autophagosome formation and lysosomal fusion, restoring autophagic flux diminished with age and in AMD, and reducing SQSTM1/p62 accumulation and associated proteostatic stress (Kaarniranta et al., 2023). Enhanced lysosomal function is expected to reduce DAMP release and intracellular complement activation, attenuate inflammasome/ferroptosis cascades associated with reduced autophagy, and stabilize mitochondrial–lysosomal crosstalk via AMPK-dependent signaling and TFEB-induced biogenesis (Boya et al., 2023). Because mTORC1 hyperactivation in the RPE causes lysosomal dysfunction and AMD-like degeneration, an agent that indirectly reduces mTORC1 activity or relieves TFEB from mTORC1/AKT constraints could restore lysosomal-autophagic balance and improve RPE survival (Boya et al., 2023).

However, the materials provided include no experimental data demonstrating that harmine produces these outcomes in RPE or retina, nor do they show ocular pharmacokinetics or local tolerability; thus, in the proposed assays, any expected effects of harmine on TFEB/CLEAR, lysosomal acidification, LAMP1/2, CTSD, POS clearance, autophagic flux, or inflammatory readouts should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and require direct testing (Singh et al., 2024). In designing such assays, relevant endpoints and models include: TFEB nuclear translocation and CLEAR gene panels; Lysotracker-based pH measurements; LAMP1/LAMP2 abundance; total and active CTSD and cathepsin activity assays; live-cell phagosome–lysosome fusion kinetics with POS; A2E/lipofuscin-loaded RPE to model elevated lysosomal pH and LMP susceptibility; LC3-II turnover and SQSTM1/p62 levels to quantify autophagic flux; markers of LMP (cytosolic cathepsin activity) and necroptosis; complement activation and inflammasome markers (e.g., IL-1β) in stressed RPE—each aligned with disease-relevant mechanisms in the cited literature (Boya et al., 2023; Kaarniranta et al., 2013; Boya et al., 2023; Kaarniranta et al., 2023; Boya et al., 2023).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
- Mechanistic alignment with RPE pathobiology: The dry AMD literature strongly implicates lysosomal dysfunction, autophagy decline, impaired phagosome–lysosome fusion, and defective degradative capacity in RPE degeneration; TFEB-directed lysosomal biogenesis and restoration of lysosomal pH/ion homeostasis are repeatedly identified as rational therapeutic axes (Boya et al., 2023). CLEAR-dependent targets such as LAMP2 and cathepsins (including CTSD) are essential for POS processing and retinal homeostasis, and their failure yields AMD-like phenotypes—consistent with the proposed direction of effect (Boya et al., 2023; Si et al., 2023; Kaarniranta et al., 2013).  
- Proof-of-concept for lysosome-centric interventions: Stabilization of lysosomal membranes, normalization of lysosomal pH, enhancement of cathepsin activity, activation of CMA, and direct correction of lysosomal enzyme deficits each attenuate retinal degeneration and preserve function in preclinical models, providing a diverse set of precedents that validate the therapeutic strategy at the pathway level (Boya et al., 2023).  
- Proteostasis-modulating activity of harmine: In CNS models, harmine promotes clearance of misfolded/aggregation-prone proteins via the ubiquitin–proteasome system, upregulating PSMD1 and enhancing PKA-dependent proteasomal activity; this establishes harmine as a proteostasis-active scaffold with in vivo pharmacology, a potentially favorable feature for diseases characterized by toxic protein/lipid waste accumulation (Singh et al., 2024).  
- Formulation and medicinal chemistry tractability: A water-soluble hydrochloride salt has been used in vivo, and analogs with markedly improved potency over harmine have been reported, supporting a path for optimization if ocular exposure or target engagement proves limiting (Singh et al., 2024).

Weaknesses and Critical Gaps  
- No direct evidence for the central hypothesis: The provided evidence base lacks demonstrations that harmine inhibits DYRK1A, increases TFEB dephosphorylation/nuclear localization, induces CLEAR gene expression, or elevates LAMP1/2 and CTSD in RPE or neural cells; consequently, the core premise—DYRK1A→TFEB→CLEAR engagement by harmine in RPE—remains unsubstantiated here (Boya et al., 2023).  
- Absence of ophthalmic data: There are no ocular pharmacokinetics, retina/RPE exposure, or local ocular tolerability data for harmine in the materials provided, which constitute major translational risks for an ocular indication (Singh et al., 2024).  
- No registered clinical activity in dry AMD: Multiple ClinicalTrials.gov queries under relevant phrases returned zero trials, indicating an absence of publicly registered clinical development for harmine in RPE/dry AMD; this constrains assessment of clinical feasibility, ocular target engagement, and dose–response in humans for this indication (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.).  
- Potential systemic off-target liabilities: Harmine’s MAO and AChE inhibitory activities in preclinical models indicate multi-target pharmacology that could complicate systemic dosing for ocular indications, although the present excerpts do not provide human tolerability or safety data to quantify this risk; nonetheless, the profile reinforces the need for careful exposure–response characterization and possibly local delivery strategies (Singh et al., 2024).  
- Unclear linkage to mTORC1/AMPK axes in RPE: The TFEB pathway in RPE is tightly regulated by mTORC1, AMPK, lysosomal Ca2+/calcineurin, and V-ATPase–Ragulator signaling; the provided materials do not show harmine’s effects on these nodes in ocular systems, leaving mechanistic plausibility at the level of generic TFEB activation rather than compound-specific evidence (Boya et al., 2023).

Decision Context and Recommended Next Steps  
- Evidence-based conclusion: On the evidence provided here, harmine is not yet a validated candidate for dry AMD, because the central mechanism-of-action claims (DYRK1A inhibition leading to TFEB/CLEAR activation in RPE) are not supported by direct data in these materials, and there is no ocular PK/tolerability or registered clinical experience to de-risk translation (Boya et al., 2023; Singh et al., 2024; ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.).  
- High-priority experiments to test the hypothesis:  
  • In vitro RPE mechanistic assays: quantify TFEB nuclear translocation, TFEB/TFE3 phosphorylation status, and CLEAR gene panels after harmine exposure; measure LAMP1/LAMP2 abundance, cathepsin content and activity (CTSD), lysosomal pH (Lysotracker/LysoSensor), and phagosome–lysosome fusion kinetics in POS-fed RPE; evaluate autophagic flux via LC3-II turnover and SQSTM1/p62; determine effects under A2E/lipofuscin load and oxidative stress to model AMD-relevant stressors (Boya et al., 2023; Kaarniranta et al., 2013; Boya et al., 2023; Kaarniranta et al., 2023; Boya et al., 2023).  
  • Inflammation/complement readouts: assess DAMP release, complement activation, inflammasome markers (e.g., IL-1β), and ferroptosis regulators, given the link between reduced autophagy and inflammasome–ferroptosis activation in RPE (Boya et al., 2023).  
  • Pathway positioning: determine harmine’s impact on mTORC1 (p-S6K, p-4E-BP1), AMPK activation, and Ca2+/calcineurin signaling, to map its position relative to TFEB regulatory inputs in RPE (Boya et al., 2023).  
  • Ocular pharmacokinetics and tolerability: quantify retina/RPE exposure after systemic and local (intravitreal/subconjunctival) dosing of harmine and harmine·HCl; evaluate local ocular tolerability (inflammation, IOP, ERG, retinal histopathology); these data are entirely missing in the provided materials and are essential for translational assessment (Singh et al., 2024).  
  • Comparative benchmarking: include positive controls known to enhance lysosomal function in RPE (e.g., mTORC1 inhibitors, AMPK activators, Ezrin pathway modulators) to calibrate the magnitude of effect needed for translational relevance (Boya et al., 2023).

- Literature and registry expansion: Because ClinicalTrials.gov queries under narrow terms returned no trials, broaden searches to other registries (WHO ICTRP, EU CTR), synonyms/related classes (β-carbolines, DYRK1A inhibitors) and biomedical literature (PubMed/Embase) for any published RPE/ocular data on harmine or close analogs; this is explicitly recommended in the ClinicalTrials.gov query notes to ensure a comprehensive landscape assessment (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.).

Programmatic Risk–Benefit Assessment  
- Potential upside: If harmine or an optimized analog demonstrates robust TFEB/CLEAR activation and lysosomal rejuvenation in RPE—with measurable restoration of LAMP2 and CTSD activity, improved POS clearance, reduced lipofuscin/LMP, and downstream anti-inflammatory benefits—then it would sit squarely on a well-validated pathophysiological axis in dry AMD, akin to other successful lysosome-targeted interventions in retinal models (Boya et al., 2023).  
- Key derisking milestones: Demonstrating ocular exposure at pharmacologically relevant levels without ocular toxicity, and confirming TFEB/CLEAR pathway engagement with functional rescue of RPE degradative capacity, are gating requirements for progression (Singh et al., 2024; Boya et al., 2023).  
- Principal uncertainties: The absence of direct evidence tying harmine to TFEB activation in RPE or neural systems in the provided materials, the lack of ocular PK/tolerability data, and the multi-target CNS pharmacology (MAO/AChE inhibition) that may complicate systemic dosing strategies collectively elevate development risk (Boya et al., 2023; Singh et al., 2024).

Bottom Line  
Harmine presents as a proteostasis-active, neuropharmacologically tractable scaffold with preclinical in vivo CNS efficacy and formulation/medicinal chemistry precedent, but the specific mechanism-of-action proposed for dry AMD—DYRK1A inhibition leading to TFEB/CLEAR-mediated lysosomal rejuvenation in RPE—lacks direct support in the provided evidence base, and there is no ocular pharmacokinetic or tolerability information to enable translational planning (Singh et al., 2024; Boya et al., 2023). Given the strong independent rationale for TFEB-directed lysosomal restoration in RPE and the centrality of CTSD/LAMP2 to POS processing and retinal integrity, harmine remains an intriguing, but currently unvalidated, hypothesis-driven candidate whose advancement should be contingent on near-term generation of rigorous RPE mechanistic data and ocular PK/tolerability profiles, alongside expanded literature/registry searches for any unpublished or non-ClinicalTrials.gov evidence (Boya et al., 2023; Si et al., 2023; Boya et al., 2023; ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.).
