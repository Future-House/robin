Proposal for SH-BC-893 (v-ATPase activator; WuXi AppTec)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
SH-BC-893 is proposed as a small-molecule v-ATPase activator intended to allosterically stabilize the proton pump in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) lysosomes, thereby enhancing proton transport, reacidifying the lumen, and restoring cathepsin D (CTSD) maturation and activity to improve degradative flux relevant to dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) pathology. However, within the provided literature set there are no primary or review data naming SH-BC-893, describing its chemical class, discovery, or synthetic route, nor preclinical evaluations in ocular systems or other indications; moreover, no registered clinical trials in AMD list this agent on ClinicalTrials.gov, and no AMD trials list “v-ATPase” as an intervention term, indicating a lack of clinical development signals under those names in this registry at the time of the query (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.-a; ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.-b). In contrast, the broader therapeutic class—agents that restore lysosomal acidity or augment lysosome/autophagy capacity—has been investigated in RPE disease models using several orthogonal strategies (e.g., cAMP-elevating ligands, zinc ionophores, TFEB activation, and acidifying nanoparticles), but none of the provided sources report a direct small-molecule v-ATPase activator tested in ocular models (RPE or AMD) or any mention of SH-BC-893 by name (Ng et al., 2024, pp. 3–5, 10–12, 13–15; Choi et al., 2024, pp. 18–29, 29–36; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7; Li et al., 2025, pp. 17–20, 27–29). By mechanistic category, SH-BC-893 would fall under lysosomal function restorers that act directly on the lumenal acidification machinery; the central target, the vacuolar-type H⁺-ATPase (v-ATPase), is the multisubunit rotary proton pump that establishes lysosomal acidity required for hydrolase activity and autophagic/heterophagic flux in RPE, a post-mitotic, highly phagocytic epithelium that ingests and degrades photoreceptor outer segments daily (Kaarniranta et al., 2023, pp. 2–3; Sinha et al., 2016, pp. 1–3). The V1 sector hydrolyzes ATP and the V0 sector conducts protons across the membrane, with subunit stoichiometries and regulatory dynamics (e.g., reversible V1–V0 association) that tune organelle pH; acidic pH ~4.5–5.0 is required for optimal lysosomal enzyme activity and membrane integrity (Chen et al., 2025, pp. 2–5). While reports outside the eye document multiple regulatory levers for v-ATPase assembly/activity (kinase control, accessory complexes, isoform selection), the provided sources do not identify a validated medicinal chemistry series that positively and directly activates v-ATPase in RPE or AMD models (Chen et al., 2025, pp. 22–23; Song et al., 2020, pp. 13–14).

Therapeutic History  
There is no documented clinical or veterinary use of SH-BC-893 in AMD or related retinal degeneration in the provided corpus, and the ClinicalTrials.gov query identified no trials in AMD with SH-BC-893 as an intervention (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.-a). Likewise, no AMD trials explicitly list “v-ATPase” as the intervention in the registry result provided (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.-b). Within ocular preclinical literature, restoration of lysosomal acidity and degradative capacity has been targeted by alternative pharmacologic or biophysical means, each reinforcing the disease rationale but not testing a v-ATPase activator per se. In ABCA4-mediated Stargardt models (human iPSC-RPE from STGD1 patients and Abca4⁻/⁻ mice), lysosomal alkalinization, impaired maturation of CTSD (accumulation of pro-CTSD), and reduced CTSD activity were demonstrated; pharmacologic lysosomal reacidification with the P2Y₁₂ antagonist ticagrelor lowered lysosomal pH, rescued CTSD maturation/activity, reduced CTSD aggregates, decreased autofluorescence, and protected photoreceptors, supporting therapeutic relevance of acidification in RPE disease (Ng et al., 2024, pp. 3–5, 10–12, 13–15). In these same systems, acidification was linked to restored degradation of outer segments (OS), a core RPE function degraded in AMD-like states (Ng et al., 2024, pp. 10–12, 13–15).

Independent strategies have shown that raising intracellular zinc with ionophores reacidifies A2E-alkalinized lysosomes, restores autophagic flux, reduces intracellular A2E burden, and protects ARPE-19 against A2E-induced cytotoxicity; in vivo chloroquine-induced alkalinization produced AMD-like pathology, again underscoring the central role of lysosomal pH (Choi et al., 2024, pp. 18–29, 29–36). Nanoparticle delivery of acidic polymers (PLGA) has attenuated weak-base–induced lysosomal alkalinization and preserved CTSD activity in RPE and optic nerve astrocytes, demonstrating a delivery-based reacidification modality with high lysosomal colocalization (Li et al., 2025, pp. 17–20). Upstream enhancement of lysosomal biogenesis, acidification capacity, and hydrolase complement via TFEB activation (viral TFEB-S467A, trehalose) or AKT2 inhibition corrected CTSD/CTSL levels and improved lysosomal/autophagy markers in AMD-relevant RPE models, and rapamycin lowered A2E and lipofuscin-like material in POS-fed cells, with effects contingent on dephosphorylated, nuclear TFEB and active lysosomal enzymes (Ghosh et al., 2024, p. 6; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7). In other inherited macular disease models (Best disease), inhibition of v-ATPase with bafilomycin phenocopied OS-degradation defects, and enhancing proteolysis/autophagy with valproic acid and rapamycin reduced rhodopsin burden after POS feeding and mitigated long-term autofluorescence accumulation in disease iPSC-RPE, albeit with acknowledged pleiotropy and translational constraints (Singh et al., 2015, pp. 6–7).

Mechanistically proximal genetic models further anchor v-ATPase to RPE health: βA3/A1-crystallin localizes to lysosomes and binds the V0 a1 subunit (ATP6V0A1), regulating v-ATPase activity. Conditional loss of βA3/A1-crystallin (Cryba1) in RPE elevates lysosomal pH, reduces CTSD activity, blocks degradation of ingested OS (despite normal ingestion), impairs autophagosome clearance, and produces vacuolation and retinal dysfunction—an AMD-like phenotype; these data highlight that v-ATPase activity is both necessary for acidification and functionally rate-limiting for RPE clearance pathways (Valapala et al., 2016, pp. 1–6). However, none of these ocular studies demonstrate rescue by pharmacologic or genetic activation of v-ATPase itself; rather, they leverage signaling, transcriptional, or physicochemical routes to restore lysosomal physiology (Boya et al., 2023, pp. 3–6). Outside the eye, enforced expression of the V0 subunit ATP6V0A2 restored lysosomal acidification and conferred protection from epirubicin-induced ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes, establishing that boosting v-ATPase can reacidify lysosomes and yield tissue-protective effects in vivo, but this evidence is in cardiac models and is not ocular (Chen et al., 2025, pp. 16–17).

Mechanism of Action  
The therapeutic hypothesis centers on allosteric stabilization and activation of the v-ATPase proton pump in RPE lysosomes to lower luminal pH and normalize the acid-hydrolase environment. This is well aligned with the biochemistry of RPE lysosomes and AMD pathobiology. v-ATPase is the principal proton pump creating the lysosomal pH gradient; the V1 sector (A3B3CDE3FG3H) hydrolyzes ATP, and the V0 sector (a c₉ c′′ d e along with ATP6AP1 and ATP6AP2) translocates protons into the lumen, thereby enabling activity of acidic hydrolases and supporting autophagy and phagolysosomal degradation (Chen et al., 2025, pp. 2–5). RPE are uniquely burdened by daily phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments, and efficient lysosomal acidification is required to degrade these discs and recycle their components; impaired acidification disrupts heterophagy and autophagy, leading to lipofuscin and drusen accumulation recognized as core AMD cellular features (Kaarniranta et al., 2023, pp. 1–3; Sinha et al., 2016, pp. 1–3).

CTSD is the dominant lysosomal aspartyl protease in RPE, responsible for the bulk of rhodopsin proteolysis; its maturation (from preproenzyme to active heavy/light chains) and catalytic activity are sharply pH-dependent, with activity falling profoundly upon modest pH elevation (e.g., near pH 5.0) (Ng et al., 2024, pp. 13–15; Valapala et al., 2016, pp. 1–4). In patient-derived STGD1 iPSC-RPE and Abca4⁻/⁻ mice, lysosomal alkalinization is associated with accumulation of immature pro-CTSD, decreased mature CTSD, loss of CTSD activity, impaired OS digestion, protein aggregation (α-synuclein), and LAP/autophagy defects linked to perturbed phosphatidylethanolamine composition; pharmacologic reacidification restores CTSD maturation and proteolysis, reduces cathepsin aggregates, and improves degradative function, validating the causal sequence from pH to CTSD to flux (Ng et al., 2024, pp. 3–8, 10–15). These CTSD-centric dependencies underscore why direct v-ATPase activation could be an efficient lever: it addresses the proximal physical determinant (pH) needed for CTSD maturation and broad lysosomal hydrolase competency (Kaarniranta et al., 2023, pp. 2–3; Sinha et al., 2016, pp. 1–3).

Endogenous control of v-ATPase in RPE is exemplified by βA3/A1-crystallin, which binds ATP6V0A1 on the lysosomal membrane and appears to modulate catalytic efficiency; loss of βA3/A1-crystallin reduces v-ATPase activity, elevates pH, and diminishes CTSD activity with consequent degradative blockade and AMD-like retinal changes (Valapala et al., 2016, pp. 1–6). v-ATPase also participates in amino acid sensing and mTORC1 recruitment via the Ragulator–Rag GTPase axis; this signaling can suppress autophagy when hyperactivated, while TFEB—the master regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy genes—depends on mTORC1 dephosphorylation for nuclear entry and CLEAR-network activation (including ATP6V0A1 expression), placing v-ATPase at the hub of both acidification and signaling that set lysosomal capacity (Sinha et al., 2016, pp. 3–4; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7). In AMD donor RPE, TFEB is more phosphorylated (cytosolic/inactive), and CTSD/CTSL protein levels and activities are reduced; constitutively active TFEB upregulates CLEAR genes (e.g., CTSB, LAMP2, ATP6V0A1) and reduces POS-dependent lipofuscin-like granules, while rapamycin reduces A2E and autofluorescent granules via TFEB activation and active lysosomal enzymes, reinforcing that increasing lysosomal gene expression and function rescues RPE degradative deficits (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7).

The regulatory literature further indicates v-ATPase activity is controlled by signal-dependent assembly/disassembly, kinase phosphorylation, and accessory factors (RAVE, Rabconnectin-3, AMPK, PKA, AKT), as well as lipid and trafficking determinants; small-molecule perturbations in non-ocular contexts can modulate v-ATPase assembly or activity, though some agents cause over-acidification and cytotoxicity, emphasizing the need for precise tuning (Chen et al., 2025, pp. 22–23). None of the provided sources, however, describe SH-BC-893’s binding site, affinity, kinetics, effect on V1–V0 association, or selectivity across v-ATPase isoforms; nor do they document its effects on mitochondrial membrane potential, autophagic flux metrics, or ocular safety—these are central unknowns that would need to be established experimentally to substantiate the proposed allosteric-activation mechanism (Song et al., 2020, pp. 13–14; Chen et al., 2025, pp. 22–23).

Expected Effect  
If SH-BC-893 positively stabilizes and activates lysosomal v-ATPase in RPE, the expected sequence is: increased proton pumping → lower lysosomal pH toward physiologic (~4.5–5.0) → enhanced maturation of CTSD (and other cathepsins) → increased hydrolase activity → improved degradation of POS cargo in phagolysosomes and of autophagic substrates → reduction of lipofuscin/A2E accumulation and secondary inflammation → improved RPE homeostasis and photoreceptor support. This causal chain is strongly supported by multiple interventions that restore acidic pH or upregulate lysosomal capacity in RPE models. In STGD1 iPSC-RPE and Abca4⁻/⁻ mice, pharmacologic reacidification with ticagrelor lowered lysosomal pH, reduced pro-CTSD aggregates by roughly half, restored mature CTSD levels and enzymatic activity to near-normal, reduced autofluorescence, and protected photoreceptors—directly validating that reacidification rescues CTSD and degradative function in disease-relevant RPE (Ng et al., 2024, pp. 3–5, 10–12, 13–15). In ARPE-19, zinc ionophores reacidified A2E-alkalinized lysosomes, normalized autophagic flux (reduced p62/LC3-II accumulation), lowered A2E burden, and decreased cell death; conversely, chloroquine alkalinization generated AMD-like in vivo pathology, indicating bidirectional control and the importance of acidification for RPE viability (Choi et al., 2024, pp. 18–29, 29–36). Acidic PLGA nanoparticles attenuated tamoxifen-induced lysosomal alkalinization and preserved CTSD catalytic-site binding by BODIPY FL-Pepstatin A, indicating restored CTSD activity in RPE and optic nerve astrocytes (Li et al., 2025, pp. 17–20). Activation of TFEB or mTOR inhibition increased CLEAR genes (including ATP6V0A1), reduced A2E and lipofuscin-like granules, and restored CTSD/CTSL abundance and activity together with lysosomal markers (LAMP1), consistent with enhanced lysosomal biogenesis/function and improved degradation (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7; Ghosh et al., 2024, p. 6).

Expression of the relevant target machinery in the cell type is established: v-ATPase is the essential proton pump of lysosomes in RPE, and ATP6V0A1 (V0 a1) is a CLEAR gene upregulated by TFEB activation in these cells; CTSD and CTSL activities are reduced in AMD donor RPE, further underscoring the therapeutic importance of restoring lysosomal hydrolase function (Kaarniranta et al., 2023, pp. 2–3; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7). Genetic data in Cryba1 cKO RPE demonstrate that reduced v-ATPase activity elevates lysosomal pH and decreases CTSD activity, blocking OS degradation and autophagic clearance, thereby establishing the proximal role of v-ATPase in RPE homeostasis (Valapala et al., 2016, pp. 1–6). Together, these data imply that a direct v-ATPase activator—if selective for lysosomes in RPE and capable of sufficient exposure—should lower lysosomal pH and increase CTSD activity, with predicted improvements in OS degradation, autophagic flux, and reductions in lipofuscin-like autofluorescence in dry AMD-relevant assays (Ng et al., 2024, pp. 3–5, 10–12, 13–15; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7).

Nevertheless, two mechanistic cautions should shape experimental expectations. First, v-ATPase also couples lysosomal amino acid sensing to mTORC1 activation through Ragulator–Rag GTPases; augmenting v-ATPase function could, context-dependently, promote mTORC1 signaling, which suppresses autophagy if chronically hyperactivated, potentially counteracting benefits unless managed (Sinha et al., 2016, pp. 3–4). Second, inducing autophagy when lysosomal function is compromised can be cytotoxic; therefore, combination strategies that simultaneously stimulate autophagy and correct acidity must be balanced and staged to avoid flux–capacity mismatches (Boya et al., 2023, pp. 8–9).

Therapeutic History (comparators and absence of SH-BC-893-specific data)  
- Direct ocular evidence for positive modulation of v-ATPase is absent in the provided sources, and no study reports pharmacologic or genetic v-ATPase activation in RPE to restore lysosomal pH and CTSD function; multiple reviews explicitly note the mechanistic plausibility of targeting v-ATPase while underscoring the lack of direct activation data in dry AMD or retinal degeneration models (Boya et al., 2023, pp. 3–6; Sinha et al., 2016, pp. 3–4; Valapala et al., 2016, pp. 1–6). Outside the eye, enforced ATP6V0A2 expression restored lysosomal acidification and conferred myocardial protection in cardiomyocytes, providing precedent that increasing v-ATPase function can reacidify lysosomes in vivo (Chen et al., 2025, pp. 16–17).  
- Multiple pH-restoring or lysosome-enhancing strategies show rescue in RPE models: P2Y₁₂ antagonism (ticagrelor), adenosine receptor agonism, zinc ionophores, TFEB activation, mTOR inhibition, and acidifying nanoparticles (Ng et al., 2024, pp. 3–5, 10–12, 13–15; Choi et al., 2024, pp. 18–29, 29–36; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7; Li et al., 2025, pp. 17–20). These cross-validate the target biology (lysosomal acidity, CTSD) while highlighting that pharmacologic v-ATPase activation has not yet been documented in these systems in the provided corpus.

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
- High mechanistic plausibility in RPE: v-ATPase is the core determinant of lysosomal acidification; RPE requires robust lysosomal degradation for heterophagy and autophagy; and lysosomal alkalinization is a recurring lesion in AMD-like and Stargardt models (Kaarniranta et al., 2023, pp. 1–3; Sinha et al., 2016, pp. 1–3). Restoration of lysosomal acidity rescues CTSD maturation/activity and degradative endpoints in human patient iPSC-RPE and Abca4⁻/⁻ mice, directly linking pH correction to functional recovery (Ng et al., 2024, pp. 3–5, 10–12, 13–15).  
- CTSD-centric therapeutic rationale: CTSD is a dominant RPE protease for rhodopsin and other substrates; its activation/maturation is tightly pH dependent; AMD donor RPE shows reduced cathepsin D/L levels and activity; restoring acidity and/or CLEAR-network expression restores lysosomal enzymology and reduces toxic pigment accumulation (Ng et al., 2024, pp. 6–8, 13–15; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7).  
- Genetic path validation: βA3/A1-crystallin deficiency impairs v-ATPase activity in RPE, elevates lysosomal pH, reduces CTSD activity, blocks OS degradation and autophagosome clearance, and produces AMD-like phenotypes, placing v-ATPase activity upstream of key disease processes in the relevant cell type (Valapala et al., 2016, pp. 1–6).  
- Multiple orthogonal rescue modalities support the endpoint package: cAMP-raising P2Y₁₂ antagonism, adenosine receptor agonism, TFEB activation/mTOR inhibition, zinc ionophores, and acidic nanoparticles each restore aspects of lysosomal pH/biogenesis and degradative capacity in RPE, increasing confidence that directly enhancing proton pumping should deliver similar or stronger benefits if properly targeted (Ng et al., 2024, pp. 3–5, 10–12, 13–15; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7; Choi et al., 2024, pp. 18–29, 29–36; Li et al., 2025, pp. 17–20).

Key Risks and Gaps  
- No evidence base for SH-BC-893: There are no publications or registry entries in the provided corpus documenting the identity, synthesis, class, binding site, target engagement, potency/selectivity, ocular PK/PD, or safety of SH-BC-893; consequently, the core claims about allosteric stabilization of the V0 domain and selective lysosomal activation in RPE cannot be substantiated here (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.-a; ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.-b; Song et al., 2020, pp. 13–14).  
- No direct v-ATPase activation precedent in ocular models: Although v-ATPase’s centrality is strong, none of the provided ocular studies show pharmacologic or genetic activation of v-ATPase as a rescue mechanism; several reviews emphasize this gap and call for targeted studies (Boya et al., 2023, pp. 3–6; Sinha et al., 2016, pp. 3–4; Valapala et al., 2016, pp. 1–6).  
- Signaling cross-talk risk (mTORC1): v-ATPase participates in amino acid–dependent mTORC1 activation; enhancing v-ATPase activity could contextually promote mTORC1 signaling, which suppresses autophagy if chronically active and impairs TFEB nuclear translocation; careful titration and possibly combination with TFEB-activating strategies may be needed to avoid a counterproductive autophagy suppression (Sinha et al., 2016, pp. 3–4; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7).  
- Systemic and cell-type specificity: v-ATPase is ubiquitous; global activation could perturb non-ocular tissues (cardiac, neuronal, immune), as v-ATPase also influences endosomal pH, trafficking, and signaling; tissue-specific delivery or RPE-targeted formulations would be essential to minimize off-target effects, but no SH-BC-893 delivery data are provided here (Chen et al., 2025, pp. 22–23; Song et al., 2020, pp. 13–14).  
- Flux–capacity mismatch: Augmenting autophagy when lysosomal function is still suboptimal can be cytotoxic; conversely, rapid acidification in lysosomes loaded with lipofuscin could risk lysosomal membrane permeabilization cascades in extreme stress contexts; staging, dose selection, and markers of lysosomal membrane integrity (e.g., LMP assays) should be integrated into development (Boya et al., 2023, pp. 8–9).  
- Translational pharmacology: Related agents (e.g., rapamycin, valproic acid) show in vitro benefits but present systemic tolerability limits in vivo; although nanoparticles and ocular routes can localize exposure, there are no data for SH-BC-893 ocular dosing, distribution, or durability in this corpus (Singh et al., 2015, pp. 6–7; Li et al., 2025, pp. 17–20).

Decision Drivers for Advancement  
- Target validity and disease linkage are strong: RPE lysosomal acidification defects and reduced CTSD function are documented in AMD donor tissue and in human disease-model RPE; reacidification restores CTSD and degradative function; v-ATPase sits at the mechanistic bottleneck (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7; Ng et al., 2024, pp. 3–5, 10–12, 13–15; Kaarniranta et al., 2023, pp. 2–3).  
- Modality novelty: A direct, positive v-ATPase modulator could provide a rapid and proximal correction compared to upstream signaling or biogenesis approaches, potentially synergizing with TFEB activation or complement-modulating strategies; however, because this is an unvalidated modality in ocular models within the provided data, the risk is considerable (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7; Boya et al., 2023, pp. 3–6).  
- Feasibility signals from other systems: Enforced expression of ATP6V0A2 restored lysosomal acidity and protected cardiomyocytes in vivo, supporting the general concept that increasing v-ATPase function can correct deacidification in disease—but chemical activation and ocular translation remain to be demonstrated (Chen et al., 2025, pp. 16–17).

Program Recommendations (evidence-based)  
- Establish compound identity and primary pharmacology: Because no SH-BC-893 data are available here, first confirm chemical identity, V0-domain binding, v-ATPase activation, and selectivity across organelles and tissues using biophysical assays and lysosomal pH measurements; define concentration–response for pH and CTSD activity in human iPSC-RPE derived from AMD donors or aged controls (Chen et al., 2025, pp. 2–5; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7).  
- Demonstrate on-mechanism rescue in RPE: Replicate the acidification–CTSD–flux triad demonstrated for ticagrelor, zinc ionophores, and nanoparticles using SH-BC-893, with readouts including LysoSensor pH, CTSD maturation/activity, OS uptake/degradation kinetics, LC3/p62 flux, and autofluorescent granule burden under POS feeding; benchmark against ticagrelor or trehalose/TFEB activation arms (Ng et al., 2024, pp. 3–5, 10–12, 13–15; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7; Li et al., 2025, pp. 17–20).  
- Dissect signaling interactions: Quantify mTORC1 activity (p-S6K, p-4EBP1), TFEB localization, and CLEAR gene expression (including ATP6V0A1) to ensure that v-ATPase activation does not chronically hyperactivate mTORC1 and suppress autophagy; consider combination with TFEB-enhancing regimens if needed (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7; Sinha et al., 2016, pp. 3–4).  
- Evaluate lysosomal membrane integrity and inflammation: Track LMP markers, cytosolic cathepsins, and inflammasome readouts to ensure acidification does not destabilize overloaded lysosomes; measure complement-related inflammation and secretory autophagy outputs, given their linkage to AMD (Boya et al., 2023, pp. 3–4).  
- In vivo pharmacology in RPE-driven models: Transition to models with documented lysosomal alkalinization and lipofuscin/A2E accumulation (e.g., Abca4⁻/⁻ mice), using ocular delivery to maximize RPE exposure; endpoints: in vivo RPE/retinal imaging of autofluorescence, ERG, photoreceptor survival, and RPE ultrastructure; pharmacodynamic tissue assays for pH and CTSD (Ng et al., 2024, pp. 3–5, 10–12, 13–15).  
- Safety pharmacology: Because v-ATPase is ubiquitous and controls multiple organellar pH-dependent processes, perform local tolerability, ocular histopathology, and systemic safety with emphasis on tissues sensitive to altered endo-lysosomal pH (cardiac, kidney, immune), guided by precedents that modulation of v-ATPase assembly can have profound non-ocular effects (Chen et al., 2025, pp. 16–17, 22–23).

Conclusion  
On target biology, the rationale for lysosomal acidification in RPE as a disease-modifying strategy for dry AMD is compelling, and restoration of CTSD maturation/activity via pH correction is repeatedly shown to rescue degradative function and reduce toxic pigment accumulation in human RPE models and in vivo Stargardt systems (Ng et al., 2024, pp. 3–5, 10–12, 13–15; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7). v-ATPase is the proximal driver of lysosomal acidity, genetically and mechanistically tied to RPE degradative competence and AMD-like phenotypes (Valapala et al., 2016, pp. 1–6). However, the specific drug candidate SH-BC-893 lacks any corroborating information in the provided literature set—no chemical identity, mechanism data, ocular pharmacology, or clinical development signals—so the central claims about its discovery and performance cannot be verified here (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.-a; ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.-b). Therefore, while a v-ATPase activator remains an attractive therapeutic concept with strong biological underpinnings in RPE, SH-BC-893 should be considered unvalidated based on the sources provided, and a go/no-go decision would require de novo demonstration of selective v-ATPase activation, lysosomal reacidification, CTSD rescue, and durable functional benefit in RPE with a clear ocular safety margin (Boya et al., 2023, pp. 3–6; Sinha et al., 2016, pp. 3–4).
