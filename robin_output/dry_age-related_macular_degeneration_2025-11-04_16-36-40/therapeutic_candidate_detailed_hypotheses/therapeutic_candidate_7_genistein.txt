Proposal for Genistein (Sigma G6649)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Genistein is a naturally occurring isoflavone that was first isolated from soybeans and other leguminous plants. As a member of the phytoestrogen class, genistein possesses structural similarities to mammalian estrogens and can bind to estrogen receptors, albeit with much lower intrinsic potency than endogenous steroids. It is typically obtained from dietary sources such as soy, red clover, and other legumes, but high‐purity formulations have been developed and are commercially available (for example, Sigma G6649). Genistein belongs to a broader class of natural bioactive compounds that have been studied over the past several decades for their modulating effects on various cellular processes including antioxidant defense, differentiation, apoptosis, and proliferation. Historically, phytoestrogens like genistein have been investigated both for their hormone‐like activity and as specific inhibitors of protein tyrosine kinases. The compound has been utilized in research ranging from hormone replacement strategies in menopausal women to anticancer studies, and its ability to modulate several cell signaling pathways—including those associated with PI3K/AKT/mTOR—has attracted interest in other fields such as neurodegeneration and ocular health (Albulescu & Popovici, 2007).

Therapeutic History:  
The therapeutic history of genistein spans multiple domains. Clinically, phytoestrogens have been examined largely as potential alternatives to traditional estrogen replacement therapies for the alleviation of menopausal symptoms, with some studies reporting modest effects on vasomotor symptoms, menstrual cycle regulation, and quality‐of‐life indices (Glazier & Bowman, 2001; Albulescu & Popovici, 2007). In the veterinary and preclinical realm, high doses of phytoestrogens have been associated with reproductive toxicity in livestock such as sheep (the classical clover disease), while rodent studies have similarly documented endocrine disruptions when exposed to high concentrations (Glazier & Bowman, 2001). For bone health specifically, preclinical data have shown that ovariectomized rats fed genistein exhibit reduced rates of bone loss; this finding parallels clinical trials using the synthetic isoflavone derivative ipriflavone—which is related to the parent compounds in the isoflavone class—and has demonstrated benefits in slowing bone loss and increasing bone mineral density when given for periods of up to two years in postmenopausal women (Glazier & Bowman, 2001). Regarding ocular applications, there is emerging preclinical work to improve the solubility and corneal penetration of genistein; for example, encapsulating genistein in hyaluronic acid–modified MPEG-b-PAE polymeric micelles has been shown to improve its corneal permeability by approximately 1.5-fold compared to a simple drug solution (Li et al., 2018). Although genistein has not yet been extensively evaluated in clinical trials specifically targeting dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD), its broad modulatory effects on tyrosine kinases and downstream signaling pathways provide a rationale for repurposing it to address dry AMD pathophysiology, particularly given the significant unmet therapeutic need in this area (Glazier & Bowman, 2001).

Mechanism of Action:  
Genistein is widely recognized for its ability to inhibit protein tyrosine kinases. This inhibitory action extends to several receptors and intracellular kinases, thereby modulating key signaling cascades including the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway—a pathway that is critical for cell survival, proliferation, and metabolism. In many cell-based studies, genistein has been shown to suppress PI3K/AKT signaling, leading to downstream inhibition of mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1) activity. Since mTORC1 coordinates cellular anabolic processes and suppresses autophagy under nutrient-rich conditions, inhibiting this complex can trigger autophagic clearance mechanisms (Albulescu & Popovici, 2007). One of the pivotal regulators downstream of mTORC1 is the transcription factor EB (TFEB), which, when dephosphorylated due to mTORC1 inhibition, translocates into the nucleus and promotes the transcription of genes involved in lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy. There is evidence that genistein can modulate lysosomal metabolism and promote TFEB activation, as reported in studies highlighting the ability of flavonoids to enhance the clearance of substrate accumulations via TFEB-mediated transcriptional programs (Xu et al., 2020). In retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells, which play a crucial role in maintaining photoreceptor health through phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments (POS), enhanced autophagy and lysosomal function are essential for the degradation and removal of cellular debris, including drusen components characteristic of dry AMD (Falcão et al., 2024). Moreover, experimental reports have suggested that in RPE-like models, treatment with compounds that suppress the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis leads to increased LC3 turnover and upregulation of cathepsin D (CTSD), a lysosomal aspartic protease essential for phagolysosomal digestion (Albulescu & Popovici, 2007; Jahan et al., 2022). In this context, genistein is hypothesized to function by inhibiting upstream tyrosine kinases that feed into PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling, thereby promoting TFEB activation. TFEB then orchestrates the transcription of lysosomal genes, leading to enhanced lysosomal acidification and increased enzymatic activities, including that of cathepsin D, which is imperative for effective degradation within phagolysosomes (Falcão et al., 2024; Falcão et al., 2025).

Expected Effect:  
The central hypothesis posits that administration of genistein to RPE cells will lead to measurable inhibition of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, resulting in dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation of TFEB. This transcriptional activation is expected to upregulate a battery of lysosomal and autophagy-related genes, including those responsible for the maintenance of lysosomal acidification and the synthesis of key proteases such as cathepsin D (Falcão et al., 2024). In functional assays, an increase in LC3 turnover—a marker of enhanced autophagic flux—would be anticipated, alongside elevated levels of CTSD, providing the biochemical basis for improved phagolysosomal digestion. Consequently, in an in vitro RPE cell model subjected to a photoreceptor outer segment (POS) feeding assay, genistein treatment is expected to facilitate the clearance of POS-derived debris and reduce the accumulation of autofluorescent lipofuscin-like granules. This outcome mimics the effects observed with compounds like rapamycin, where mTOR inhibition leads to a significant, approximately 30% reduction of POS-dependent autofluorescence by improving lysosomal processing (Falcão et al., 2024). Moreover, in light of published observations demonstrating that effective activation of TFEB can restore lysosomal biogenesis and enhance cellular clearance, genistein’s dual inhibition of kinase activity and subsequent autophagy upregulation is predicted to result in a favorable impact on drusen clearance and overall RPE function in dry AMD settings (Falcão et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2020).

Overall Evaluation:  
Overall, genistein presents as an intriguing candidate for repurposing in the treatment of dry AMD, particularly given its established history as a natural product with a favorable oral safety profile and its documented ability to modulate critical signaling pathways involved in cell survival and autophagy. One of the major strengths of genistein lies in its dual mechanism: as a selective inhibitor of tyrosine kinases, it can suppress the upstream activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, and as a modulator of lysosomal metabolism, it facilitates TFEB activation. This mechanistic duality offers a targeted approach to enhance the autophagic clearance capacity of RPE cells, which is critical in preventing the accumulation of intracellular debris and drusen associated with dry AMD (Falcão et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2020). Furthermore, the existing literature on phytoestrogens indicates that compounds in this chemical class are generally well tolerated when administered orally, and formulation strategies (such as encapsulation in hyaluronic acid–modified polymeric micelles) enhance ocular bioavailability, potentially enabling effective local delivery with minimal systemic adverse effects (Li et al., 2018).

However, several challenges and weaknesses must be addressed before advancing genistein as a therapy for dry AMD. First, while there is a substantial body of evidence supporting the role of genistein in modulating PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling in non-ocular tissues, direct evidence in RPE cells and in the context of dry AMD is sparse. Most published studies in the ocular domain have focused on formulation and delivery aspects rather than on detailed mechanism-of-action studies within RPE cells. Second, the estrogenic activity of genistein, although weak, raises potential concerns regarding off-target endocrine effects, particularly during long-term administration in older populations who may be susceptible to hormone-related side effects. These aspects would require rigorous assessment in preclinical and early-phase clinical trials. Additionally, the pharmacokinetics of genistein when administered via ocular routes (either topically or systemically) remain incompletely characterized, and comprehensive data on tissue distribution, especially to the retina or RPE, is limited. Such pharmacokinetic studies are crucial to ensure that therapeutic concentrations can be achieved and maintained in the target tissues without eliciting adverse effects (Glazier & Bowman, 2001; Li et al., 2018).

In light of the above considerations, genistein is promising as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD based on its capacity to inhibit upstream tyrosine kinases and suppress the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling cascade, thereby promoting TFEB-mediated enhancement of lysosomal acidification and cathepsin D activity. Such a mechanism is directly relevant to improving phagolysosomal function in RPE cells and facilitating the clearance of drusen components—a critical unmet need in the management of dry AMD (Falcão et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2020). The dual actions of genistein offer mechanistic clarity that may translate into a therapeutic edge when compared to broad-spectrum autophagy inducers. Nonetheless, the current evidence base is still largely circumstantial, and further direct investigations are necessary to confirm that genistein induces the expected autophagic and lysosomal responses in RPE cells. Preclinical studies employing appropriate RPE models (such as POS feeding assays and chronic oxidative stress paradigms) should be prioritized to solidify the link between genistein treatment and improved clearance of lipofuscin-like deposits. Ultimately, detailed pharmacokinetic, efficacy, and safety studies—ideally complemented by early-phase clinical trials—will be required before genistein can be confidently advanced as a therapeutic modality for dry AMD (Albulescu & Popovici, 2007; Falcão et al., 2024).

In summary, genistein’s natural origin, established safety in other contexts, and its dual ability to inhibit relevant tyrosine kinase–mediated signaling and upregulate autophagy through TFEB activation collectively support its potential for repurposing in dry AMD. However, critical gaps in direct efficacy data in ocular models and a precise understanding of its tissue pharmacokinetics necessitate further investigation. This combination of promising mechanistic rationale and some supportive preclinical data argues that genistein merits further preclinical exploration in RPE models of dry AMD, with the expectation that if such studies confirm improved clearance of lipofuscin and drusen markers, subsequent early-phase clinical trials can be pursued to evaluate its therapeutic benefit in patients suffering from this blinding disease (Falcão et al., 2024; Glazier & Bowman, 2001; Li et al., 2018; Xu et al., 2020).
