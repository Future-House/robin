Proposal for Anakinra (Kineret; Sobi)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Anakinra (brand: Kineret) is a recombinant, non-glycosylated form of the human interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1Ra) that competitively binds the type I interleukin-1 receptor (IL-1RI) and prevents signaling by IL-1α and IL-1β, thereby broadly antagonizing downstream IL-1–dependent signal transduction cascades (notably NF-κB and MAPK) in responsive cells (Dabouz, 2024; Dabouz et al., 2020). IL-1Ra belongs to the class of anti-cytokine biologics that block pro-inflammatory cytokine signaling, a therapeutic strategy that has been validated across systemic autoinflammatory and autoimmune diseases; Kineret has an extensive human safety and pharmacology dossier as an approved product, with prescribing information available for systemic use (Mesquida, Drawnel, & Fauser, 2019). Within ophthalmology, IL-1 pathway blockade has been investigated in multiple preclinical ocular models and has seen clinical use for inflammatory eye diseases; routes explored include systemic administration of anakinra for uveitis and scleritis, topical IL-1 antagonists for ocular surface disease (e.g., isunakinra/EBI-005), and experimental ocular gene therapy encoding IL-1Ra (Fabiani et al., 2017). In posterior segment disease models, intravitreal recombinant IL-1Ra (anakinra) inhibited laser-induced choroidal neovascularization (CNV) in rodents in a dose-dependent fashion, establishing local ocular pharmacologic activity of IL-1R antagonism in retina/choroid (Fabiani et al., 2017; Sandhu, Lambert, Xu, & Kaplan, 2018).

Therapeutic History  
Systemic indications and ocular inflammatory experience. Anakinra has long been employed systemically in rheumatology and has been repurposed for ocular autoinflammatory and autoimmune conditions such as Behçet uveitis, Blau syndrome/CAPS phenotypes, and refractory scleritis, with reported improvements in intraocular inflammation and steroid-sparing in case series and retrospective cohorts; dosing regimens reported include 100–150 mg daily or weight-based 1–3 mg/kg daily subcutaneous dosing, with acceptable tolerability profiles dominated by mild injection-site reactions in the ocular cohorts cited (Fabiani et al., 2017; Korhonen, 2024). A practical constraint is anakinra’s short systemic half-life that necessitates daily dosing, with relapse reported upon dose reductions in some inflammatory eye disease cases—an important consideration for chronic indications (Fabiani et al., 2017; Korhonen, 2024). Topical isunakinra/IL-1Ra formulations for ocular surface disease demonstrated clinically meaningful symptom and sign improvements in randomized trials, high ocular receptor occupancy (~97%) at low doses, minimal systemic exposure (plasma below detection), and a favorable local safety signal, although these outcomes pertain to the anterior segment and cannot be extrapolated directly to the posterior segment (Fabiani et al., 2017).

Posterior segment and AMD-relevant preclinical work. Multiple animal studies substantiate that IL-1 signaling is active and targetable in the posterior segment. In a rat CNV model, intravitreal anakinra led to dose-dependent reductions in neovascular area; related IL-1β-targeted interventions prevented CNV without exacerbating photoreceptor degeneration, arguing against gross phototoxicity from IL-1 blockade in that setting (Sandhu et al., 2018; Fabiani et al., 2017). In a blue light–induced retinal degeneration model, systemic IL-1R antagonism with anakinra mitigated inflammatory readouts, reduced accumulation of subretinal macrophages, preserved photoreceptor structure, and maintained ERG function; an allosteric IL-1R modulator (rytvela) was somewhat superior, but anakinra nonetheless produced protective effects (Dabouz et al., 2020; Dabouz, 2024). Mechanistically, these studies localized IL-1β production to inflammasome-active subretinal mononuclear phagocytes (MPs) and mapped Il1r1 expression mainly to astroglia and vascular cells in mouse retina, supporting paracrine IL-1 signaling to neurovascular targets in the outer retina (Dabouz et al., 2020; Dabouz, 2024).

Human clinical evidence in dry AMD/geographic atrophy. No published human trials of anakinra for dry AMD/geographic atrophy (GA) were identified in the provided materials; a registry search string returned 18 ClinicalTrials.gov hits combining anakinra with retinal keywords, but no trial-level information (indication, route, dose, status, outcomes) was provided, and no AMD/GA trials of anakinra are documented here (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.). Systemic IL-1β blockade with canakinumab showed no effect in a diabetic retinopathy exploratory clinical context, underscoring translational uncertainty of systemic IL-1 pathway modulation for posterior segment disease (Korhonen, 2024).

Mechanistic rationale in RPE/AMD. Extensive literature supports that in AMD-relevant stress contexts the RPE and subretinal myeloid cells exhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activation, caspase-1–dependent maturation of IL-1β and IL-18, lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP) with cathepsin release, and altered autophagy/heterophagy—mechanistic substrates for chronic para-inflammation and cell death (apoptosis → pyroptosis shift) in RPE (Treanor, n.d.; Yerramothu, Vijay, & Willcox, 2018; Kaarniranta et al., 2023). IL-1β levels are elevated in AMD vitreous and plasma, and microglia/MP-derived IL-1β can drive chemokine and cytokine production by Müller cells and RPE, linking IL-1 to retinal inflammatory amplification (Nashine et al., 2022; Korhonen, 2024). The RPE itself produces IL-1Ra and uses IL-1Ra to limit immune activation: IL-1Ra suppresses necrosis-induced release of IL-6, IL-8, and MCP-1 from ARPE-19 and reduces antigen-presenting functions in RPE cocultures, indicating that IL-1 signaling is operational in RPE and that IL-1R antagonism can blunt RPE-mediated inflammatory outputs in vitro (Fabiani et al., 2017; Kaur & Singh, 2021).

Mechanism of Action  
Receptor pharmacology. IL-1β and IL-1α bind IL-1RI and recruit IL-1 receptor accessory protein (IL-1RAcP) to form a signaling complex that initiates MyD88-dependent activation of IRAKs, TRAF6, TAK1, and downstream MAPKs (JNK/p38) and NF-κB, driving expression of inflammatory mediators and cell-type–specific responses (Dabouz, 2024). Anakinra is a competitive orthosteric antagonist of IL-1RI that prevents IL-1α/β binding and thereby blocks the formation of the signaling complex with IL-1RAcP, “non-selectively” inhibiting downstream IL-1–dependent pathways across cell types expressing IL-1RI (Dabouz, 2024; Dabouz et al., 2020). In the blue-light retinal injury model, orthosteric antagonism with anakinra and allosteric modulation with rytvela each prevented IL-1–driven retinal inflammation and photoreceptor injury; mechanistically, both agents protected neuroretinal compartments that express IL-1R while not necessarily reducing IL-1β produced in macrophage conditioned media, consistent with receptor-level antagonism in responder cells (Dabouz et al., 2020; Dabouz, 2024).

Cellular sources/targets in retina. In injury models, Il1b is upregulated primarily in infiltrating subretinal mononuclear phagocytes that co-express NLRP3 and caspase-1, whereas Il1r1 expression is detected predominantly in astrocytes, pericytes, endothelial cells, and bipolar cells, with little to no detection in photoreceptors or MPs by scRNA-seq; this places the critical pharmacodynamic target of anakinra on neurovascular/astroglial elements rather than on the IL-1β-producing cells themselves (Dabouz et al., 2020; Dabouz, 2024). IL-1β is elevated in AMD patients’ vitreous and is a recognized biomarker of retinal inflammation; microglia-derived IL-1β amplifies chemokine signaling by Müller and RPE cells, indicating that RPE can serve as an IL-1–responsive amplifier of inflammation even if Il1r1 detection varies by model and assay (Nashine et al., 2022; Korhonen, 2024). RPE-derived endogenous IL-1Ra provides further evidence that IL-1 signaling—and its antagonism—are biologically relevant in the RPE microenvironment (Fabiani et al., 2017).

Inflammasome context and limitations of IL-1R blockade. In RPE and adjacent retinal cells, NLRP3 inflammasome activation is triggered by AMD-relevant stimuli including A2E/lipofuscin photooxidation, drusen components, DICER1/Alu RNA accumulation, oxidized lipoproteins, complement/cofactors, ROS, and lysosomal membrane permeabilization with cathepsin release (Treanor, n.d.; Yerramothu et al., 2018). Inflammasome activation requires priming (e.g., TLR-NF-κB–dependent upregulation of NLRP3/pro-IL-1β) followed by an activation signal (e.g., mtROS, LMP), culminating in caspase-1 activation and secretion of mature IL-1β and IL-18; inflammasome priming can shift RPE death from apoptosis to pyroptosis, increasing inflammatory cytokine release and DAMP extrusion (Treanor, n.d.; Kaarniranta et al., 2023). Critically, IL-1 pathway blockers such as anakinra do not inhibit upstream inflammasome assembly, caspase-1 activation, or IL-18 production; thus, IL-1R antagonism will block IL-1 signaling but will not shut down inflammasome-dependent IL-18 release or pyroptotic execution, which may continue to drive disease biology (Korhonen, 2024). Moreover, in some experimental paradigms, IL-18 can have protective or context-dependent roles in AMD models, complicating network-level predictions when only IL-1 signaling is blocked (Lim et al., 2020).

Evidence gaps regarding lysosomal targets. The working hypothesis posits that anakinra will prevent IL-1β-driven inflammasome amplification and lysosomal destabilization in RPE, preserving vacuolar H+-ATPase (v-ATPase) function and cathepsin D (CTSD) maturation/activity; however, the provided materials contain no direct experimental evidence that IL-1R antagonism rescues v-ATPase activity, lysosomal pH, or CTSD processing in RPE, nor that anakinra reduces lysosomal membrane permeabilization in these systems (Lim et al., 2020; Treanor, n.d.). While lysosomal destabilization and cathepsin B/L release are well-documented inflammasome triggers in RPE, and bafilomycin A1 (a v-ATPase inhibitor) has been used to model lysosomal/autophagic impairment that activates NLRP3 and IL-1β release, there is no reported dataset here demonstrating that IL-1R blockade restores lysosomal acidification or cathepsin D maturation in RPE (Yerramothu et al., 2018; Piippo, 2019). Consequently, the proposed IL-1→lysosome preservation claims remain conjectural in the absence of targeted RPE lysosomal measurements following anakinra treatment in AMD-relevant paradigms (Lim et al., 2020; Treanor, n.d.).

Expected Effect  
Assay-level effects in retinal/RPE systems. Based on the presented literature, IL-1R antagonism with anakinra is expected to reduce IL-1–driven inflammatory signaling in neuroretinal and gliovascular compartments, attenuate subretinal macrophage accumulation/activation, and preserve photoreceptor structure/function in acute inflammatory injury paradigms; these effects have been demonstrated in the blue light model (reduced GFAP, Il1b/Il6/Ccl2 transcripts, fewer subretinal MPs, more preserved ONL and ERG amplitudes) (Dabouz, 2024; Dabouz et al., 2020). In explant co-culture systems where LPS/ATP-activated macrophages produce IL-1β, IL-1R antagonists and anti-IL-1β antibodies abrogate photoreceptor death, supporting a causal role for IL-1β and a direct cytoprotective effect of IL-1 pathway blockade on IL-1RI-expressing retinal cells (Dabouz, 2024). In vitro RPE models show that IL-1Ra can suppress necrosis-induced secretion of IL-6, IL-8, and MCP-1 (likely via NF-κB), suggesting that anakinra may dampen autocrine/paracrine amplification in RPE cultures challenged with danger signals—though definitive RPE IL-1RI expression patterns in vivo remain incompletely defined in the cited mouse scRNA-seq data (Fabiani et al., 2017; Dabouz et al., 2020).

Inflammasome priming versus activation. IL-1 can contribute to priming (NF-κB–dependent) of inflammasome components; therefore, anakinra might indirectly reduce NLRP3 priming in some contexts by interrupting IL-1 positive feedback loops, leading to lower pro-IL-1β and possibly reduced susceptibility to subsequent activation triggers (Fabiani et al., 2017). However, because anakinra acts downstream of inflammasome assembly, it will not prevent caspase-1 activation, gasdermin D pore formation, pyroptosis, or IL-18 maturation/secretion once NLRP3 is engaged—which are all relevant to RPE and subretinal myeloid biology in AMD (Korhonen, 2024; Treanor, n.d.; Kaarniranta et al., 2023). Consequently, anakinra’s benefit in dry AMD models that are dominated by RPE-intrinsic inflammasome activation and pyroptosis may be partial, favoring reductions in IL-1–driven chemokine/cytokine cascades and immune cell recruitment over direct prevention of inflammasome-executed cell death (Korhonen, 2024; Treanor, n.d.).

Lysosomal endpoints. The proposed preservation of v-ATPase function and cathepsin D activity is not currently supported by primary data in the provided sources; no reports demonstrate that anakinra normalizes lysosomal pH, protease maturation (including CTSD), or membrane integrity in RPE subjected to AMD-relevant stressors (Lim et al., 2020; Treanor, n.d.; Piippo, 2019). While inhibiting IL-1 signaling may lower NF-κB–driven transcription of inflammatory mediators (and thus lessen secondary stressors to lysosomes), the extent to which this translates into measurable correction of lysosomal biology in RPE remains to be tested experimentally (Fabiani et al., 2017).

Translational dosing and safety in the eye. In mice, systemic anakinra at 4 mg/kg twice daily for 3 days produced retinal protection in the blue light model; this regimen emphasizes short-term systemic exposures in an acute injury context rather than chronic degenerative settings (Dabouz, 2024). In CNV rodents, intravitreal IL-1Ra reduced neovascularization without aggravating photoreceptor degeneration, providing proof-of-concept for posterior segment delivery and a supportive safety signal in that animal model (Sandhu et al., 2018). Clinically, systemic anakinra has an established safety profile in inflammatory eye disease cohorts, but no intravitreal human anakinra safety data in AMD/GA are presented here; systemic IL-1β blockade (canakinumab) did not improve retinopathy outcomes in a separate context, and anakinra’s daily dosing requirement remains a burden for chronic use unless long-acting ocular delivery is engineered (Korhonen, 2024; Fabiani et al., 2017).

Overall Evaluation  
Merits  
- Biological plausibility in AMD. AMD-relevant stressors activate NLRP3 in RPE and subretinal myeloid cells, elevating IL-1β and IL-18 and driving para-inflammatory cascades and pyroptosis; IL-1β is increased in AMD vitreous and plasma, and IL-1–producing MPs are present at sites of pathology, establishing IL-1 as a rational target for modulating retinal inflammation (Treanor, n.d.; Nashine et al., 2022; Korhonen, 2024).  
- Pharmacologic validation in retinal models. Anakinra significantly reduced inflammation and preserved retinal structure/function in a light-damage model and intravitreally suppressed CNV in rodents, indicating target engagement and protective potential in the posterior segment; IL-1β neutralization prevented CNV without exacerbating photoreceptor degeneration, arguing for a non-toxic profile in those contexts (Dabouz et al., 2020; Dabouz, 2024; Sandhu et al., 2018).  
- RPE-relevant immunobiology. RPE naturally produces IL-1Ra, and exogenous IL-1Ra reduces necrosis-induced inflammatory mediator release from RPE and suppresses dendritic-cell activation in RPE co-cultures, suggesting that anakinra could attenuate RPE-mediated inflammatory amplification in dry AMD (Fabiani et al., 2017; Kaur & Singh, 2021).  
- Clinical and formulation precedents. Systemic ocular use of anakinra in uveitis and scleritis shows clinical feasibility and manageable safety; topical IL-1 antagonists have robust ocular safety signals; gene therapy encoding IL-1Ra and intravitreal administration have been explored preclinically, supporting a spectrum of delivery options that could be adapted for posterior segment disease (Fabiani et al., 2017).

Limitations and risks  
- Mechanistic incompleteness vis-à-vis inflammasomes. IL-1R blockade will not prevent upstream NLRP3 activation, caspase-1 activity, gasdermin-mediated pyroptosis, or IL-18 production; in RPE-intrinsic inflammasome-dominant pathophysiology, anakinra would be expected to blunt downstream IL-1–mediated amplification and leukocyte recruitment but not stop the execution machinery of inflammasome-driven cell death (Korhonen, 2024; Treanor, n.d.; Kaarniranta et al., 2023).  
- Unsubstantiated lysosomal rescue claims. There is no evidence here that anakinra preserves lysosomal acidification (v-ATPase function), cathepsin D maturation/activity, or prevents LMP in RPE under AMD-relevant stress; this is a critical gap because the proposed therapeutic specificity hinges on cytokine–lysosome cross-talk that remains to be demonstrated (Lim et al., 2020; Treanor, n.d.; Piippo, 2019).  
- Target expression uncertainties. In mouse retina, Il1r1 is enriched in astroglia and vascular cells and not detected in photoreceptors or MPs by scRNA-seq, complicating assumptions about direct photoreceptor or RPE targeting by anakinra in vivo; while RPE responds to IL-1 in vitro and produces IL-1Ra, spatial and species differences in IL-1RI distribution may shape efficacy and delivery decisions (Dabouz et al., 2020; Dabouz, 2024; Fabiani et al., 2017).  
- Mixed translational signals. Systemic IL-1β blockade did not improve diabetic retinopathy outcomes in the cited study, and anakinra’s systemic short half-life imposes burdensome daily dosing for chronic disease; there is no documented human safety/efficacy experience with intravitreal anakinra in AMD/GA in the provided sources (Korhonen, 2024; Fabiani et al., 2017; ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.).  
- Network complexity. NLRP3/IL-18 can exert context-dependent protective or detrimental effects in AMD models; while anakinra does not block IL-18 signaling, its selective inhibition of IL-1 may not suffice to rebalance inflammasome-driven pathology if pyroptosis and IL-18-dependent pathways remain active (Lim et al., 2020).

Go/No-Go considerations and critical experiments  
- RPE-centric validation. Test anakinra in human RPE models exposed to A2E/blue-light, drusen components, DICER1/Alu RNA, or oxidized lipoproteins; quantify NLRP3 priming (NLRP3, pro-IL-1β), activation (ASC specks, caspase-1 cleavage), downstream cytokines (IL-1β, IL-18), and execution (GSDMD cleavage/pyroptosis markers), with and without anakinra to define points of pathway modulation (Treanor, n.d.; Yerramothu et al., 2018).  
- Lysosomal endpoints. Directly measure lysosomal pH, v-ATPase activity, LMP (galectin-3 puncta), cathepsin D maturation/activity, and autophagic flux in the same RPE paradigms ± anakinra to test the hypothesis that IL-1R blockade preserves lysosomal integrity and protease processing (Piippo, 2019).  
- In vivo GA-relevant models. Move from acute light injury to RPE-atrophy models (e.g., chronic photooxidative stress, Alu RNA/DICER1 disruption, lipofuscin models) to assess whether anakinra reduces rates of RPE atrophy, outer retinal thinning, and visual function decline; include comparators targeting the inflammasome (e.g., NLRP3 inhibitors) to benchmark effect sizes and mechanistic depth (Treanor, n.d.; Kaarniranta et al., 2023; Lim et al., 2020).  
- Ocular delivery and PK. Develop and test intravitreal dosing and, preferably, sustained-release formats for anakinra to overcome systemic short half-life; establish vitreous/retinal PK and receptor occupancy, and evaluate ocular safety (IOP, ERG, histopathology) and immunogenicity (anti-drug antibodies) in large-eye species (Korhonen, 2024; Fabiani et al., 2017).  
- Biomarker strategy. Use vitreous/plasma IL-1β and retinal inflammatory transcript signatures as pharmacodynamic biomarkers; consider scRNA-seq to confirm target cell engagement (Il1r1-expressing compartments) pre- and post-treatment (Nashine et al., 2022; Dabouz, 2024).

Positioning relative to alternatives  
Because anakinra does not interrupt inflammasome activation or execution, proximal inhibitors of the NLRP3 inflammasome (e.g., MCC950-class) may provide deeper control of IL-1β/IL-18 production and pyroptosis in RPE-driven disease, at the cost of potentially suppressing beneficial IL-18 signaling in some contexts; preclinical literature shows NLRP3 blockade can be protective in retinal models, meriting parallel evaluation (Lim et al., 2020). Conversely, allosteric IL-1R modulators (rytvela-type) have shown superior protection to anakinra in the light-damage model and may offer functional selectivity (e.g., sparing NF-κB while inhibiting JNK/p38/AP-1) with improved distribution and immunogenicity profiles; these properties deserve consideration if an IL-1-targeting approach is pursued (Dabouz, 2024; Dabouz et al., 2020).

Conclusions  
- Strength of rationale. There is robust evidence that IL-1β is elevated and pathogenic in retinal neuroinflammation and that IL-1R antagonism—including anakinra—can protect retina in preclinical models by reducing inflammatory signaling and immune cell recruitment; RPE-relevant IL-1 biology further supports plausibility (Dabouz et al., 2020; Dabouz, 2024; Fabiani et al., 2017).  
- Weakness of hypothesis specifics. The central claim that anakinra preserves v-ATPase function and cathepsin D maturation in RPE lacks support in the provided literature; moreover, IL-1R blockade cannot prevent upstream inflammasome activation or pyroptosis, limiting its capacity to halt RPE-intrinsic degenerative processes central to GA (Lim et al., 2020; Korhonen, 2024; Treanor, n.d.; Piippo, 2019).  
- Translational readiness. No human AMD/GA trials of anakinra are documented here; systemic IL-1β blockade has shown limited efficacy in retinal vascular disease, and anakinra’s dosing logistics are unfavorable for chronic ocular therapy without dedicated ocular delivery systems (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.; Korhonen, 2024).

Overall Evaluation  
Anakinra is a mechanistically credible anti-inflammatory candidate to blunt IL-1–driven retinal inflammation and leukocyte recruitment, with reproducible preclinical efficacy signals in posterior-segment injury models and supportive RPE-centric immunobiology; however, the specific hypothesis that it will mechanistically preserve lysosomal v-ATPase function and cathepsin D activity in RPE is currently unsupported by direct evidence, and IL-1R antagonism is unlikely to arrest inflammasome activation, IL-18 signaling, or pyroptotic cell death that contribute to RPE atrophy in dry AMD (Dabouz et al., 2020; Dabouz, 2024; Fabiani et al., 2017; Korhonen, 2024; Lim et al., 2020). Given the absence of clinical data in GA and delivery constraints, anakinra merits consideration as an adjunct anti-inflammatory approach contingent on (1) generation of RPE-focused data demonstrating modulation of inflammasome priming and lysosomal integrity, and (2) development of sustained intraocular delivery; in parallel, more proximal interventions (NLRP3 inhibitors) and IL-1R allosteric modulators with superior preclinical efficacy should be benchmarked as potentially higher-leverage strategies for disease modification in dry AMD (Lim et al., 2020; Dabouz et al., 2020).
