Proposal for Eltanexor (KPT-8602; MedKoo 406829)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Eltanexor (also known as KPT-8602, MedKoo 406829) is a synthetic small‐molecule compound belonging to the class of “selective inhibitors of nuclear export” (SINE). These agents were developed by medicinal chemists to target the nuclear export protein exportin-1 (XPO1, also known as CRM1), which mediates the transport of a wide range of proteins from the nucleus to the cytoplasm. Eltanexor, like its predecessor Selinexor (KPT-330), functions by binding within the nuclear export signal (NES) recognition site of XPO1—most notably involving interaction with a conserved cysteine residue (Cys528) in XPO1’s NES‐binding pocket—and thereby preventing export of many regulatory proteins. However, Eltanexor has been engineered with modifications that confer improved tolerability along with oral bioavailability and a favorable pharmacokinetic profile, making it an attractive candidate for repurposing. SINE compounds have been extensively studied in oncology, where their ability to cause the nuclear retention of tumor suppressors has led to approved indications in certain hematological malignancies; accordingly, the synthetic origin of Eltanexor is rooted in efforts to optimize the drug‐like properties of early XPO1 inhibitors and to improve their clinical safety profile (Newell et al., 2024).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, the SINE class has been primarily associated with anticancer applications. Early compounds such as leptomycin B (LMB) were the first to demonstrate that inhibition of XPO1 could induce nuclear retention of proteins; however, issues with toxicity limited their clinical utility. Subsequent development efforts led to the creation of SINE compounds including Selinexor (KPT-330) and more recently Eltanexor (KPT-8602), which have undergone both preclinical and early-phase clinical trials in various cancer types. In these settings, XPO1 inhibition was shown to result in the nuclear accumulation of proteins that typically support cell survival, thereby selectively promoting apoptosis in cancer cells (Newell et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2025). Outside the field of oncology, mechanistic studies have indicated that inhibiting nuclear export can also impact proteins critically involved in autophagy regulation and lysosomal homeostasis. For instance, preclinical work in neuronal and glioblastoma models has shown that XPO1 inhibitors trigger nuclear retention of the master autophagy regulator TFEB, thereby enhancing autophagic flux and lysosomal biogenesis (Abokyi & Tse, 2025; Tang et al., 2025). To date, however, there is no published study that directly employs Eltanexor or other XPO1 inhibitors as therapeutic agents for dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) or related retinal degenerative conditions. In retinal research, extensive efforts have focused on modulating TFEB activity through alternative strategies—such as using trehalose, metformin, or genetic approaches—to restore lysosomal function in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells (Ghosh et al., 2024; Abokyi & Tse, 2025). Although the direct use of XPO1 inhibitors in RPE or dry AMD models has not been experimentally demonstrated, the established efficacy of SINE compounds in other contexts of neurodegeneration and autophagy dysregulation provides a compelling rationale for their repurposing in ocular diseases (Abokyi & Tse, 2025; Falcão et al., 2025).

Mechanism of Action:  
Under normal cellular conditions, XPO1 mediates the nuclear export of a diverse set of proteins by recognizing nuclear export signals (NES) present on cargo proteins. One critical cargo is the transcription factor TFEB, which regulates the expression of lysosomal hydrolases and autophagy-related genes by binding to CLEAR (coordinated lysosomal expression and regulation) elements within gene promoters. In nutrient-rich or stress-dysregulated states, TFEB undergoes phosphorylation by kinases such as mTORC1, resulting in cytoplasmic sequestration mediated by binding to 14-3-3 proteins and subsequent export from the nucleus via XPO1 (Pan & Valapala, 2022; La Spina et al., 2021; Li, 2018). Eltanexor’s mechanism of action is to bind within the NES-binding pocket of XPO1 and disrupt its interaction with such cargo proteins. The inhibition is achieved through covalent and slowly reversible binding to the Cys528 residue in XPO1, thereby preventing the nuclear export of target proteins. When export is blocked, TFEB (and likely its paralog TFE3) accumulates in the nucleus where it is free to activate transcription of CLEAR network genes. The resulting upregulation of lysosomal genes leads to increased lysosomal biogenesis, a lowering of lysosomal pH, and enhanced activation of hydrolases such as cathepsin D—all of which are critical to maintaining proteostasis and clearing damaged proteins and organelles (Abokyi & Tse, 2025; Pan & Valapala, 2022). Published data in glioblastoma models have shown that other XPO1 inhibitors such as Selinexor induce this type of nuclear trapping of TFEB, which correlates with an increase in autophagic flux and enhanced clearance mechanisms (Tang et al., 2025). Importantly, unlike pharmacological treatments that rely on mTOR inhibition (which can have widespread metabolic effects), the SINE approach works directly at the level of nuclear export, thereby selectively increasing TFEB activity independent of upstream mTOR/AKT signaling (Abokyi & Tse, 2025; Li, 2018).

Expected Effect in Dry AMD:  
Dry age-related macular degeneration is characterized by dysfunction of the RPE, resulting in diminished autophagic flux, accumulation of lipofuscin, and impaired degradation of photoreceptor outer segments (POS). In healthy RPE cells, nuclear TFEB orchestrates a transcriptional program that maintains lysosomal homeostasis by upregulating genes involved in lysosomal biogenesis, acidification, and cargo degradation. However, in aging and diseased RPE, TFEB becomes increasingly phosphorylated and sequestered in the cytoplasm, leading to reduced CLEAR network activity (Ghosh et al., 2024; Abokyi & Tse, 2025). The hypothesis behind repurposing Eltanexor for dry AMD is that by inhibiting XPO1 in RPE cells, the drug will promote nuclear retention of TFEB and TFE3. This nuclear accumulation is expected to drive upregulation of lysosomal gene expression, resulting in the restoration of lysosomal acidification and proteolytic capacity, particularly cathepsin D activity. An effective increase in autophagic flux and lysosomal biogenesis should aid in the degradation of accumulated toxic compounds such as lipofuscin and undegraded POS debris, ultimately improving RPE function and potentially slowing or reversing progression of dry AMD (Ghosh et al., 2024; Abokyi & Tse, 2025). In addition, preclinical studies in neurodegeneration and retinal models have demonstrated that enhancing TFEB activity—whether by trehalose treatment, gene therapy approaches, or by upstream modulation of kinases such as AKT2—can protect cells from oxidative and proteotoxic stress (Abokyi & Tse, 2025; Ghosh et al., 2024). Although direct evidence in RPE cells is still pending, the large body of mechanistic work linking nuclear TFEB accumulation to improved lysosomal function provides a strong rationale for expecting similar outcomes with Eltanexor in the context of dry AMD.

Overall Evaluation:  
Eltanexor presents several attractive features as a potential therapeutic candidate for dry AMD, especially given its novel mechanism of action and its ability to modulate a fundamental cellular process that is impaired in the disease. One of the major strengths of Eltanexor lies in its targeted inhibition of XPO1, which has been well validated in oncology and neurodegeneration studies as a means to induce nuclear retention of regulatory proteins. By preventing the export of TFEB and TFE3, Eltanexor is expected to allow these key transcription factors to reactivate the CLEAR network, thereby restoring lysosomal function, lowering lysosomal pH, and boosting the activity of cathepsin D-mediated degradation. This mechanism is particularly compelling for dry AMD because RPE cell dysfunction in the disease has been closely linked to defective autophagy and impaired clearance of toxic aggregates (Ghosh et al., 2024; Abokyi & Tse, 2025). Moreover, the proposed therapeutic approach bypasses some of the limitations of mTOR-based interventions, as it directly affects nucleocytoplasmic transport rather than broadly inhibiting upstream signaling pathways. This specificity provides an additional level of mechanistic precision that could translate into a better safety profile for chronic applications in an aging population.

Nevertheless, several weaknesses and challenges must be acknowledged. First, while there is abundant evidence that XPO1 inhibitors such as Selinexor induce TFEB nuclear retention and autophagy in glioblastoma and other non-ocular models (Tang et al., 2025), there is currently a lack of direct preclinical data evaluating Eltanexor or related compounds in retinal pigment epithelial cells or in animal models of dry AMD. The extrapolation of data from cancer and neuronal models to RPE cells, while mechanistically plausible, remains to be experimentally validated. Second, because XPO1 regulates the nuclear export of a wide range of proteins—not only TFEB and TFE3—there is a potential for off-target effects that could disrupt other cellular processes. This issue is particularly critical in the context of chronic administration in elderly patients, who may be more susceptible to systemic side effects. Third, the pharmacodynamic properties required to achieve effective nuclear retention of TFEB in the RPE may differ from those established in oncology trials. For example, the concentration of Eltanexor required to affect TFEB nuclear localization and lysosomal gene expression in RPE cells might be higher than that used in cancer models, necessitating further optimization of dosing and delivery strategies. Finally, while the oral bioavailability and existing Phase I safety data of Eltanexor support its clinical feasibility (Tang et al., 2025), localized and retina-specific delivery might ultimately prove necessary to maximize its therapeutic window and minimize systemic exposure.

In summary, Eltanexor’s mechanism of inhibiting XPO1 to promote nuclear retention of TFEB/TFE3 aligns very well with emerging therapeutic strategies aimed at correcting lysosomal dysfunction in dry AMD. The extensive literature on the role of TFEB in regulating autophagy and lysosomal biogenesis in RPE cells provides a strong mechanistic foundation for this approach (Ghosh et al., 2024; Abokyi & Tse, 2025). Additionally, the demonstrated ability of XPO1 inhibitors to induce autophagy in other preclinical models (Abokyi & Tse, 2025; Tang et al., 2025) reinforces the potential for repurposing Eltanexor in an ocular setting. However, to move forward, it will be essential to generate direct experimental evidence in primary RPE cultures and in vivo dry AMD models to verify that Eltanexor indeed promotes TFEB nuclear retention, enhances CLEAR network activity, lowers lysosomal pH, and increases cathepsin D–mediated cargo degradation in the retinal environment. Addressing these issues will not only validate the therapeutic concept but will also help to optimize dosing, delivery, and safety profiles tailored for the treatment of dry AMD.

Overall, while Eltanexor shows promise as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD owing to its targeted mechanism of action and favorable preclinical attributes in other disease models, further studies focused explicitly on retinal cell biology and AMD pathology are imperative before clinical translation can be considered. The strength of the candidate lies in its mechanistic specificity and the well-established role of TFEB/TFE3 in lysosomal function, whereas the main weaknesses revolve around the current lack of direct evidence in the target system and potential off-target effects associated with global nuclear export inhibition (Abokyi & Tse, 2025; Pan & Valapala, 2022; Ghosh et al., 2024).
