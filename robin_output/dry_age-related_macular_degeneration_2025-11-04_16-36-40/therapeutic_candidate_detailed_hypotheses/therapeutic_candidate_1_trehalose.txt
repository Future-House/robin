Proposal for Trehalose (Sigma T9531)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Trehalose is a naturally occurring non‐reducing disaccharide (molecular weight 342.3 Da) found broadly in microorganisms and invertebrates, widely used as a stabilizer and excipient in foods and pharmaceuticals, and formulated in ophthalmic solutions at 3% w/v (≈87.7 mM) for ocular surface applications (e.g., dry eye) (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 1–2). In mammalian cells, trehalose enters via fluid‐phase endocytosis at millimolar concentrations and has been repeatedly reported as an autophagy enhancer that can activate AMPK signaling, stimulate lysosomal activity, and augment autophagic flux across multiple tissues, including ocular tissues (RPE) and retina (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 1–2). In the context of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), trehalose has been deployed as a small‐molecule modulator of the lysosome–autophagy axis with proposed mTOR‐independent activation of TFEB, the master transcriptional regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy (CLEAR network), positioning it mechanistically distinct from direct mTORC1 inhibitors such as rapamycin (Ghosh et al., 2024, p. 6; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 10–11). Topical ophthalmic trehalose is generally well tolerated, and preclinical studies show no overt ocular toxicity at 3% or 9% w/v after twice-daily dosing for up to two weeks, supporting feasibility for posterior segment delivery strategies; Raman spectroscopy has detected trehalose in RPE/choroid after repeated topical dosing, although posterior penetration is variable (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 8–10).

Therapeutic History  
Preclinical evidence in RPE and retina  
- Human RPE cell line (ARPE-19) oxidative stress model: Trehalose upregulates autophagy markers (LC3-II, p62/SQSTM1), increases TFEB protein and autophagy gene transcripts (ATG5, ATG7), and elevates lysosomal markers including LAMP2 and Cathepsin D (CTSD) protein; importantly, trehalose pretreatment confers cytoprotection against hydroquinone (HQ)-induced oxidative injury, and this protection is abolished by ATG5 knockdown, demonstrating autophagy dependence (typical dosing 50 mM trehalose; HQ 50 μM) (Abokyi et al., 2020, pp. 1–2, 9–11, 11–12, 12–13, 15–16). Dose–response caveats were noted: higher trehalose concentrations (≥100 mM) lost cytoprotection and were associated with stronger LC3-II accumulation, suggesting risk from excessive autophagy activation (Abokyi et al., 2020, pp. 11–12, 15–16).  
- Primary murine RPE in vitro: A non‐toxic trehalose dose (12.5 mM) increased autophagic flux measured by tandem GFP–RFP-LC3B, increasing RFP+ autolysosomes and RFP:GFP ratios without accumulating GFP+ autophagosomes, with colocalization to LysoTracker+ acidic compartments; trehalose also attenuated H₂O₂-induced mitochondrial superoxide and restored maximal respiration (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 7–10). These data indicate trehalose enhances autophagosome–lysosome fusion and downstream degradative flux in RPE under oxidative stress (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 7–10).  
- iPSC-derived human RPE carrying the CFH Y402H risk allele (CFH (H/H)): Trehalose (100 mM, 20–48 h) restored levels and enzymatic activities of lysosomal hydrolases Cathepsin D and Cathepsin L and rescued LAMP1 in complement challenge models, phenocopying constitutively nuclear TFEB (TFEB-S467A) and AKT2 inhibition; these data support trehalose as an mTOR-independent TFEB activator that rejuvenates lysosomal function in AMD-relevant RPE (Ghosh et al., 2024, p. 6; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 10–11).  
- Ex vivo/in vivo lysosomal/autophagy disease models: In Cryba1 KO RPE explants with lysosomal defects, trehalose rescued CLEAR gene expression and autophagy flux; in Cryba1 conditional KO and Akt2 knock-in mice, a regimen of 1 mg/g/day intraperitoneal trehalose for 2 days followed by 2% w/v in drinking water for 3 months starting at 6 months of age restored lysosomal function in RPE, rescued CLEAR network gene expression, and prevented early Bruch’s membrane basal deposit formation that typically emerges by 9 months (Ghosh et al., 2023, pp. 11–15; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 6–8).  
- Topical trehalose in mice with acute outer retinal stress: Twice-daily eyedrops of 9% trehalose for seven days before and after light-induced outer retinal degeneration (LIRD) reduced ONL thinning by ~53%, preserved RPE morphology and F-actin junctions, and increased LC3B and LAMP1 with decreased p62 in the RPE, consistent with enhanced autophagy-lysosome engagement in vivo (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 8–13). Raman spectroscopy detected trehalose in posterior ocular tissues (RPE/choroid > retina) after repeated dosing, supporting posterior segment exposure from topical application (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 8–10).

Broader retinal/lysosomal disease context and precedent  
- Trehalose reduced retinal degeneration, neuroinflammation, and storage burden in a lysosomal hydrolase deficiency retinal model, supporting disease modification in lysosomal storage–related retinal pathology, although model details are not provided here (Boya et al., 2023, pp. 17–18; Cox et al., 2025, p. 16).  
- Multiple reviews and mechanistic syntheses emphasize lysosomal dysfunction, impaired autophagy, and TFEB dysregulation in AMD pathogenesis, supporting the rationale for TFEB-centric lysosome restoration strategies; however, these reviews do not present trehalose-specific clinical data in AMD (Boya et al., 2023, pp. 3–6; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 2–4).

Clinical use  
- No clinical trials or clinical case series for trehalose in atrophic AMD or geographic atrophy (GA) are reported in the provided context (Abokyi et al., 2020, pp. 1–2; Cox et al., 2025, pp. 1–2). Although ophthalmic trehalose is used clinically for ocular surface disease and demonstrates a favorable safety profile, there is no clinical evidence here linking trehalose to slowed GA progression (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 1–2).

Key gaps relative to the proposed hypothesis  
- No direct measurements in the provided RPE datasets demonstrate trehalose restores lysosomal acidification (pH), and no trehalose-driven TFEB nuclear translocation imaging/quantification is shown in RPE, despite TFEB activation being implicated by transcriptional and functional rescue (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 10–11; Ghosh et al., 2023, pp. 11–15; Abokyi et al., 2020, pp. 1–2, 16–17). Lipofuscin/A2E clearance by trehalose is also not directly shown here (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7).

Mechanism of Action  
AMD-relevant lysosome–autophagy context in RPE  
- The RPE is exceptionally reliant on lysosomal degradation for both autophagy and daily heterophagy of photoreceptor outer segments (POS), and lysosomal ion homeostasis, including proton pump activity, determines acidification and hydrolase function; age-related lipofuscin accumulation, often oxidized and crosslinked, compromises lysosomal integrity and can promote lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP), driving cathepsin release and cell death cascades (Boya et al., 2023, pp. 3–4, 4–6). The mTORC1–TFEB/TFE3 axis regulates lysosomal biogenesis; TFEB dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation induce CLEAR network genes, including lysosomal hydrolases and V-ATPase subunits, while pathological signals (amino acid–Ragulator–Rag GTPases, AKT, and mTORC1) can sequester TFEB/TFE3 in the cytoplasm, thereby undermining lysosomal biogenesis (Boya et al., 2023, pp. 3–4, 4–6). In AMD models, impaired lysosomal acidification (e.g., via βA3/A1-crystallin deficits) and hyperactive mTORC1 reduce TFEB/TFE3 nuclear translocation, LAMP2 function, and degradative capacity, phenocopying AMD-relevant delays in phagosome degradation and lipofuscin handling (Boya et al., 2023, pp. 4–6). Human AMD RPE shows reduced CTSD and CTSL protein levels and activities, and increased TFEB phosphorylation with reduced nuclear TFEB staining, further linking impaired TFEB signaling and hydrolase deficiencies to AMD pathophysiology (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7).

Trehalose: TFEB pathway engagement and autophagy–lysosome restoration  
- In human iPSC-derived RPE carrying the CFH Y402H risk variant, trehalose is reported to activate TFEB independently of mTOR, rescuing CLEAR gene programs, restoring LAMP1, and significantly increasing Cathepsin D and L protein levels and enzymatic activities; trehalose effects were comparable to constitutively nuclear TFEB (TFEB-S467A) and AKT2 pathway inhibition, which are orthogonal interventions that promote TFEB nuclear activity (Ghosh et al., 2024, p. 6; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 10–11). In ex vivo Cryba1 KO RPE (a lysosomal pH/ion-homeostasis-related defect model), trehalose rescued CLEAR gene expression and autophagy flux, and in vivo chronic trehalose treatment (IP loading followed by 2% oral) restored lysosomal function in RPE and prevented basal deposit formation, while not attenuating abnormal mTORC1 activation, consistent with an mTOR-independent route to TFEB activation (Ghosh et al., 2023, pp. 11–15; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 6–8).  
- In primary murine RPE, trehalose enhances autophagosome maturation and autolysosome formation (tandem GFP–RFP-LC3B reporter), with RFP colocalization to LysoTracker+ acidic compartments, indicating augmented flux through acidified lysosomes; this occurred at a low, non-toxic concentration (12.5 mM), and was functionally coupled to mitochondrial rescue under oxidative stress (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 7–8, 8–10). In ARPE-19 cells, trehalose increased TFEB protein levels, autophagy genes (ATG5, ATG7), LAMP2, LC3, p62, and Cathepsin D protein, and protection from HQ oxidant injury required intact autophagy (ATG5-dependent), illustrating an autophagy-centric cytoprotective mechanism in human RPE cells (Abokyi et al., 2020, pp. 1–2, 11–13).  
- Importantly, while TFEB activation is repeatedly implicated, the provided RPE datasets do not demonstrate trehalose-induced TFEB nuclear translocation by imaging or nuclear fractionation; the nuclear TFEB readout is shown for a TFEB-S467A construct but not directly for trehalose (Ghosh et al., 2023, pp. 11–15; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 10–11). Likewise, the studies here do not quantify lysosomal pH normalization under trehalose, even though V-ATPase/acidification is a central determinant of lysosomal protease activity (Boya et al., 2023, pp. 3–4, 4–6).

Intersection with AKT2–TFEB dysregulation in AMD  
- Human AMD-risk iPSC-RPE (CFH Y402H) exhibits increased AKT2, which phosphorylates TFEB/TFE3 (e.g., TFEB Ser467; TFE3 S321), sequestering them in the cytosol via 14-3-3, reducing CLEAR gene expression including CTSD and CTSL and impairing lysosomal function; AKT2 overexpression disrupts autophagosome–lysosome fusion and promotes secretory autophagy, phenotypes linked to drusen biology (Ghosh et al., 2023, pp. 1–5). Pharmacologic AKT2 inhibition or constitutively nuclear TFEB rescue CLEAR targets and degradative fusion; trehalose’s phenocopying of these interventions in the same models underscores TFEB pathway engagement, but again without direct trehalose-specific TFEB nuclear localization assays in RPE in the provided texts (Ghosh et al., 2024, p. 6; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 10–11).

Lysosomal acidification, CTSD activation, and lipofuscin clearance: what is proven vs. hypothesized  
- Acidification: Quantitative lysosomal pH restoration by trehalose in RPE is not reported in the provided sources; surrogate evidence includes increased autolysosomes colocalizing with LysoTracker (acidic compartments) in primary murine RPE and biochemical restoration of lysosomal proteins/activities in human iPSC-RPE (CFH (H/H)), but pH measurements (e.g., LysoSensor ratiometry) are lacking (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 7–8; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 10–11).  
- Cathepsin D: In ARPE-19, trehalose increased CTSD protein levels (no enzymatic activity assay reported in those pages), whereas in CFH (H/H) iPSC-RPE trehalose significantly restored CTSD and CTSL activities, directly supporting trehalose-mediated hydrolase functional rescue in human RPE disease models (Abokyi et al., 2020, pp. 1–2; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 10–11). Given the reported decrease of CTSD/L protein and activity in human AMD RPE, restoration of CTSD activity aligns with a rational corrective mechanism (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 10–11).  
- Lipofuscin/A2E clearance: Direct trehalose data are absent in the provided selection; however, TFEB activation and mTOR inhibition (rapamycin) reduce A2E accumulation and lipofuscin-like granules in RPE models in a manner dependent on active lysosomal enzymes and dephosphorylated TFEB, indicating that enhancing TFEB and lysosomal function can drive lipofuscin clearance mechanistically (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7, 7–9). Whether trehalose replicates these outcomes in RPE remains to be tested explicitly.

Mechanistic risks and dose sensitivity  
- Dose-dependent overactivation of autophagy can erode cytoprotection; in ARPE-19, higher trehalose doses were associated with loss of protection against HQ despite ROS suppression, consistent with potential autophagy-associated cytotoxicity if overdosed (Abokyi et al., 2020, pp. 15–16). This argues for careful dose–response mapping and phasic exposure control in RPE (Abokyi et al., 2020, pp. 15–16).

Expected Effect  
Mechanistically expected effects in RPE assays aligned to the hypothesis  
- TFEB-dependent lysosome–autophagy augmentation: In RPE cell culture, trehalose is expected to increase expression of CLEAR network genes and lysosomal markers (e.g., LAMP1/2) and to enhance autophagic flux, evident by LC3 turnover dynamics and decreased p62/SQSTM1 as degradative flux improves; in multiple models, trehalose increased LC3B and LAMP markers and reduced p62, with autophagosome–lysosome fusion enhanced (GFP–RFP-LC3B, LysoTracker colocalization) (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 7–8; Cox et al., 2025, pp. 10–13; Abokyi et al., 2020, pp. 1–2). Given abundant TFEB expression in RPE and the centrality of TFEB to lysosomal gene programs, a TFEB-centric response to trehalose is plausible in RPE, although nuclear localization with trehalose should be measured directly (Boya et al., 2023, pp. 3–4, 4–6; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 10–11).  
- CTSD/CTSL functional rescue: In human iPSC-RPE with AMD-risk alleles, trehalose restored CTSD and CTSL abundance and enzymatic activity, suggesting that RPE harboring disease-related lysosomal deficits will show increased cathepsin activity with trehalose; therefore, in assays using disease-relevant RPE genetics or complement challenge, trehalose is expected to boost cathepsin activity (Ghosh et al., 2024, p. 6; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 10–11). Because CTSD and CTSL are reduced in human AMD RPE, this restoration aligns with a disease-relevant corrective effect (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7).  
- Lysosomal acidification: While increased autolysosome formation and LysoTracker colocalization suggest engagement of acidic compartments, trehalose has not been shown here to quantitatively lower lysosomal pH; therefore, acidification restoration is a testable hypothesis requiring direct measurement (e.g., LysoSensor ratiometry, V-ATPase subunit expression/activity assays) (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 7–8; Boya et al., 2023, pp. 3–4).  
- Reduction of oxidative stress and mitochondrial rescue: Trehalose reduced basal ROS in ARPE-19 under resting conditions and decreased mitochondrial superoxide with restoration of maximal respiration under H₂O₂ stress in primary RPE, indicating that trehalose-mediated autophagy enhancement increases organelle quality control and redox homeostasis in RPE (Abokyi et al., 2020, pp. 12–13; Cox et al., 2025, pp. 8–10).  
- Lipofuscin/A2E clearance: Based on TFEB-dependent clearance data with rapamycin and TFEB-overexpression models, trehalose could reduce A2E and lipofuscin-like granules if it drives TFEB nuclear import and maintains lysosomal hydrolase function; however, explicit verification in RPE is lacking in the provided materials and should be included in the assay plan (e.g., A2E-loaded RPE with autofluorescence quantitation, POS-fed RPE granule counts) (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7, 7–9).  
- In vivo RPE/retinal structure preservation: In acute LIRD, topical trehalose preserved ONL thickness and RPE morphology; in chronic models with lysosomal defects, systemic trehalose prevented basal deposit formation and rescued RPE lysosomal function; hence, assays probing RPE structural resilience under oxidative or complement stress are likely to show benefit with trehalose (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 8–10, 10–13; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 6–8; Ghosh et al., 2023, pp. 11–15).

Assay-linked molecular readouts and expression in RPE  
- TFEB/TFE3, LAMP1/2, LC3B, SQSTM1/p62, CTSD/CTSL, and V-ATPase subunits (e.g., ATP6V0A1) are expressed in RPE and are central to lysosomal/autophagic function, with CLEAR network control by TFEB; reduced nuclear TFEB and decreased CTSD/L activity are documented in AMD RPE (Boya et al., 2023, pp. 3–4, 4–6; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7). Given these baselines, RPE is an appropriate cell type to measure trehalose-driven TFEB pathway engagement and lysosomal protease restoration (Boya et al., 2023, pp. 3–4, 4–6; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 10–11).

Translational expectations and delivery  
- Topical dosing: The 3%–9% trehalose eyedrop regimens are feasible and safe in preclinical studies, achieve measurable posterior exposure, and produced autophagy biomarker changes and structural protection in LIRD (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 8–10, 10–13). However, posterior ocular bioavailability is typically low and variable, and much drug is lost systemically, necessitating optimization of formulation and dosing frequency for consistent RPE exposure (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 10–13, 15–16).  
- Systemic dosing: Oral trehalose can be degraded by trehalase, complicating systemic delivery; nonetheless, in mouse models (Cryba1 cKO and Akt2 KI), IP loading then 2% trehalose in drinking water for 3 months restored RPE lysosomal function and prevented deposit formation (Ghosh et al., 2023, pp. 11–15; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 6–8). Clinical translation will need to address trehalase metabolism and systemic exposure constraints (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 10–13).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
- Multi-modal preclinical evidence in RPE: Trehalose increases autophagy flux in human ARPE-19 and primary murine RPE, with autolysosome formation confirmed via tandem GFP–RFP-LC3 and LysoTracker colocalization, and cytoprotective effects depend on intact autophagy machinery (ATG5), directly aligning with the mechanistic target in RPE (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 7–8; Abokyi et al., 2020, pp. 1–2, 12–13).  
- Restoration of lysosomal hydrolase content and activity in human iPSC-RPE disease models: Trehalose rescued CTSD/L protein and activity and normalized LAMP1 under complement challenge in CFH Y402H RPE, mechanistically reversing disease-relevant lysosomal deficiencies (Ghosh et al., 2024, p. 6; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 10–11).  
- TFEB pathway engagement, mTOR-independent: Trehalose phenocopies constitutively nuclear TFEB and AKT2 inhibition in rescuing lysosomal/autophagy networks and function, and restores CLEAR transcripts in explants and in vivo without suppressing abnormal mTORC1 activation, suggesting lower risk of mTOR-related adverse effects (Ghosh et al., 2023, pp. 11–15; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 10–11).  
- In vivo efficacy signals: Topical trehalose improves autophagy markers in RPE and preserves ONL and RPE morphology after light stress; chronic systemic dosing prevents basal deposits and rescues lysosomal function in RPE in genetic lysosome-defect models (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 10–13; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 6–8; Ghosh et al., 2023, pp. 11–15).  
- Delivery feasibility and safety: Topical administration at high concentrations (3%–9%) is well tolerated and achieves posterior detection, creating a realistic translational route; topical dosing circumvents trehalase metabolism, a limitation for oral delivery (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 1–2, 8–10, 10–13).

Weaknesses and major gaps relative to the hypothesis  
- Lysosomal acidification not directly demonstrated: No quantitative lysosomal pH measurements with trehalose are reported in the provided RPE datasets; restoration of acidification remains an inference and must be demonstrated directly (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 7–8; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 10–11; Abokyi et al., 2020, pp. 1–2).  
- TFEB nuclear translocation under trehalose unproven in RPE: While trehalose is described as a TFEB activator and phenocopies TFEB-S467A, direct nuclear localization (imaging or fractionation) after trehalose in RPE is not shown here; this is a critical mechanistic gap (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 10–11; Ghosh et al., 2023, pp. 11–15; Abokyi et al., 2020, pp. 16–17).  
- Lipofuscin/A2E clearance not demonstrated for trehalose: Although TFEB activation and mTOR inhibition are known to reduce A2E/lipofuscin-like granules in RPE models, trehalose-specific evidence for lipofuscin clearance is absent in the supplied materials (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7).  
- Geographic atrophy endpoints lacking: There are no chronic GA model or clinical data showing trehalose slows lesion growth or preserves vision; LIRD is an acute stress model and does not recapitulate GA progression (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 1–2, 8–10).  
- Dosing translatability: Many in vitro effects were observed at 50–100 mM trehalose, which are high relative to likely in vivo intra-RPE exposures; dose–response optimization is needed, and excessive dosing risks autophagy-associated cytotoxicity (Abokyi et al., 2020, pp. 12–13, 15–16; Cox et al., 2025, pp. 7–8).  
- Source quality heterogeneity: Some supportive RPE studies come from lower-quality journals or preprints, warranting independent replication under rigorously controlled conditions (Abokyi et al., 2020, pp. 1–2; Ghosh et al., 2023, pp. 11–15).

Risk–benefit and development recommendations  
- Mechanistic verification package:  
  - Quantify lysosomal pH restoration with LysoSensor/DND-160 ratiometry and V-ATPase subunit/activity assays in RPE models ± trehalose, including disease-relevant stress (e.g., complement challenge, A2E loading) (Boya et al., 2023, pp. 3–4, 4–6).  
  - Directly quantify TFEB nuclear translocation (immunocytochemistry with nuclear segmentation; nuclear/cytosolic fractionation and immunoblotting) under trehalose vs. TFEB-S467A and vs. AKT2 inhibition, to confirm that trehalose’s phenotypic rescue is TFEB-dependent in RPE (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 6, 10–11).  
  - CTSD/CTSL processing and activity: Extend the CFH (H/H) results by testing trehalose across primary human RPE and AMD-donor–derived RPE; relate CTSD activity rescue to autophagic flux endpoints (LC3 turnover, p62 degradation) and to substrate clearance (POS proteolysis kinetics) (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 6, 10–11; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7).  
  - Lipofuscin/A2E clearance: In A2E-loaded and POS-fed RPE, quantify autofluorescence granules and A2E mass spectrometry before/after trehalose, and test dependence on TFEB (siRNA/CRISPR) and lysosomal hydrolase inhibition (e.g., pepstatin-A for CTSD) (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7, 7–9).  
- PK/PD and delivery optimization:  
  - For topical delivery, map trehalose concentration–time in RPE/choroid and retina with LC-MS or validated Raman signatures; explore permeation enhancers or sustained-release formulations to overcome low posterior bioavailability (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 8–10, 10–13, 15–16).  
  - Define an ocular PD biomarker panel (RPE LC3B/LAMP1 colocalization, p62 decrease, CTSD activity) and link to functional readouts (ERG, OCT metrics) in chronic complement- or lipofuscin-based models (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 10–13).  
- Comparative and combinatorial strategy:  
  - Benchmark trehalose against TFEB-centric comparators (e.g., rapalogs, Ezrin inhibitors) on shared endpoints (TFEB nuclear index, lysosomal pH, CTSD activity, lipofuscin clearance), given rapamycin’s ability to reduce A2E/lipofuscin via TFEB activation and the potential of ezrin blockade to increase lysosome number and cathepsin activity in RPE (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7, 7–9; Boya et al., 2023, pp. 4–6).  
  - Consider combination with complement modulation where indicated, as complement stress contributes to AMD-relevant lysosomal dysfunction in CFH (H/H) RPE (Ghosh et al., 2024, p. 6).

Go/no-go criteria for advancement toward GA  
- Mechanistic Go: Demonstration in RPE that trehalose increases the TFEB nuclear index by >2-fold, lowers lysosomal pH toward physiological values, and increases CTSD activity by >30% over disease-state baseline, with concordant increases in LC3B–LAMP1 colocalization and p62 degradation; abrogation of effects by TFEB knockdown would confirm mechanism (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 10–11; Cox et al., 2025, pp. 7–8; Boya et al., 2023, pp. 3–4).  
- Functional Go: Demonstrated reduction of A2E/lipofuscin by ≥25% in RPE models without cytotoxicity, and structural/functional rescue in a chronic AMD-relevant animal model (e.g., CFH-risk or complement-driven models) with durable effects beyond acute stress models (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7; Cox et al., 2025, pp. 8–10; Ghosh et al., 2024, p. 6).  
- Safety Go: No evidence of autophagy-associated cytotoxicity at the intended exposure (maintained viability; absence of excessive LC3-II accumulation without flux; ocular histopathology clean) (Abokyi et al., 2020, pp. 15–16; Cox et al., 2025, pp. 8–10).

Conclusion  
The totality of evidence supports trehalose as a plausible, repurposable modulator of the TFEB–lysosome/autophagy axis in the RPE with multiple independent signals of autophagy flux enhancement, lysosomal hydrolase restoration (CTSD/L), and cytoprotection in oxidative/complement stress contexts, delivered via mechanisms that appear mTOR-independent and therefore potentially safer than broad mTORC1 inhibition (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 10–11; Cox et al., 2025, pp. 7–8; Abokyi et al., 2020, pp. 1–2; Ghosh et al., 2023, pp. 11–15). However, the hypothesis’s most stringent claims—quantitative restoration of lysosomal acidification, trehalose-driven TFEB nuclear translocation in RPE, and direct lipofuscin/A2E clearance leading to slowed geographic atrophy progression—are not yet demonstrated in the supplied literature and require targeted mechanistic and disease-model validation (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 10–11; Abokyi et al., 2020, pp. 16–17; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7; Cox et al., 2025, pp. 8–10). Given strong biological plausibility and encouraging preclinical signals, a focused IND-enabling program that closes these gaps is justified, with early emphasis on mechanistic biomarkers (lysosomal pH, TFEB nuclear index, CTSD activity), pharmacokinetics of posterior delivery, and chronic AMD-relevant efficacy models before advancing to clinical study in GA (Boya et al., 2023, pp. 3–4; Cox et al., 2025, pp. 10–13; Ghosh et al., 2024, p. 6).
