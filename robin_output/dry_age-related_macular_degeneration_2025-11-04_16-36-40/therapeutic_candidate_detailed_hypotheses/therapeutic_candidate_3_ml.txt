Proposal for ML-SA1 (Tocris 475342)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
ML-SA1 is a synthetic, cell-permeable small-molecule agonist of the endolysosomal mucolipin/TRPML family, with primary pharmacological activity at TRPML1, the Ca2+-permeable cation channel encoded by MCOLN1 that resides on late endosome/lysosome membranes and mediates lysosomal Ca2+ efflux into the cytosol (Wang et al., 2023). ML-SA1 and chemically related “ML-SA” agonists emerged from small-molecule discovery programs aimed at pharmacologically restoring TRPML1 function; the compound is frequently referred to in the literature as “mucolipin synthetic agonist 1,” reflecting its origin as a tool compound class designed to activate TRPML channels rather than as a derivative of a natural product scaffold (Yan et al., 2021). Although the chemical structure and synthetic route for ML-SA1 are not detailed in the cited records, its provenance traces to a specialized small-molecule therapeutics effort at Calporta Therapeutics, a company subsequently acquired by Merck in 2019, signaling substantial translational interest in TRPML1 agonism for lysosomal storage and neurodegenerative disorders (Van Acker et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2023).

Functionally, ML-SA1 belongs to the broader class of TRPML1 agonists/activators that includes endogenous lipid ligands such as phosphatidylinositol-3,5-bisphosphate, PI(3,5)P2, and other synthetic small molecules (e.g., SF-22, MK6-83, and newer ML-SA chemotypes like ML-SA5), all of which gate TRPML channels to increase endolysosomal Ca2+ release (Wang et al., 2023; Más Gómez et al., 2018; Wünkhaus et al., 2024). Within this class, ML-SA1 is reported to activate TRPML1 with potency comparable to or greater than the endogenous PI(3,5)P2 under certain conditions and can act in conjunction with PI(3,5)P2 to potentiate channel opening, thereby rescuing endo-lysosomal morphology and function in cellular disease models that exhibit lysosomal stress (e.g., after PIKfyve inhibition) (Wang et al., 2023). Across preclinical research, TRPML1 agonists have been deployed as mechanistic probes and candidate therapeutics to restore lysosomal Ca2+ signaling, normalize lysosomal pH/acidification, promote autophagosome maturation and autolysosome formation, enhance lysosomal exocytosis, and induce TFEB-dependent lysosomal biogenesis in lysosomal storage and neurodegeneration models (Tedeschi et al., 2025; Pollmanns et al., 2022).

In ocular systems directly relevant to retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) biology, ML-SA1 has been used to interrogate TRPML1 function. In mouse primary RPE, human embryonic stem cell–derived RPE (hesRPE), and ARPE-19 cells, ML-SA1 produced rapid, robust, reversible increases in cytosolic Ca2+ that persisted in Ca2+-free external solutions and were blunted by lysosomal disruption, indicating a lysosomal Ca2+ source (Más Gómez et al., 2018). Critically, ML-SA1 failed to evoke Ca2+ signals in RPE from TRPML1 knockout mice, confirming channel specificity in this tissue (Más Gómez et al., 2018). The same study demonstrated that lysosomal lipid accumulation—induced pharmacologically or present in disease models—impairs ML-SA1-evoked TRPML1 Ca2+ release, mechanistically linking lysosomal lipidization to diminished TRPML1 function in RPE (Más Gómez et al., 2018). These data situate ML-SA1 squarely within a mechanistic toolkit for modulating RPE lysosomal physiology implicated in retinal degenerations associated with lipid/lipofuscin accrual (Más Gómez et al., 2018).

Therapeutic History  
There is no record in the provided corpus of ML-SA1 being used clinically in humans or specifically in trials for dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) or geographic atrophy (GA) (Más Gómez et al., 2018). However, extensive preclinical use across cell and animal models supports the biological rationale relevant to dry AMD. In RPE systems, ML-SA1 has been applied acutely to evoke TRPML1-dependent lysosomal Ca2+ release as described above (Más Gómez et al., 2018). In vivo, subretinal ML-SA1 administration in a rat retinal detachment model preserved outer nuclear layer thickness, reduced photoreceptor apoptosis, lessened cleaved caspase-3, increased autophagy markers (LC3-II/LC3-I and Beclin-1), decreased reactive oxygen species (ROS), and prevented TRPML1 degradation, collectively demonstrating functional rescue via autophagy activation and oxidative stress mitigation in injured retina (Yan et al., 2021).

Beyond the eye, multiple studies in neuronal and disease-model systems have used ML-SA1 to correct lysosomal/autophagy defects and proteostasis. In cultured neurons and HEK293T cells, ML-SA1 enhanced maturation of autophagosomes into acidic autolysosomes and reduced α-synuclein accumulation/aggregates; ML-SA1 could override bafilomycin-induced fusion/acidification blocks in both HEK293T cells and neurons, and these effects required lysosomal PI(3,5)P2 and cytosolic Ca2+ consistent with TRPML1 channel gating (Pollmanns et al., 2022). In primary cortical neurons, ML-SA1 rapidly acidified endolysosomes and prevented LDL-induced amyloidogenesis by reducing Aβ and BACE1 mislocalization/activity; TRPML1 knockdown abrogated ML-SA1’s effects and exacerbated LDL-driven Aβ production (Hui et al., 2019). In lysosomal storage disease (LSD) contexts, ML-SA1 was used as a TRPML1 activator to elicit lysosomal Ca2+ release responses that were impaired in Niemann–Pick type C (NPC1) patient fibroblasts; BK channel activation partially rescued the reduced ML-SA1 response, and BK upregulation lowered lipofuscin accumulation via lysosomal exocytosis, establishing pharmacological synergies that might be exploited in lipid storage states (Zhong et al., 2016).

Importantly for RPE-relevant lysosomal storage phenotypes, the more potent TRPML1 agonist ML-SA5 was used in ARPE-19 CLN3-knockout cells (a juvenile neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis model) to reduce lysosomal accumulation of subunit C of mitochondrial ATPase (SubC), Gb3, and lysosomal glycerophosphodiesters, accompanied by TFEB nuclear translocation and increased surface LAMP1 consistent with enhanced lysosomal exocytosis; however, BMP/LBPA lipid deficits and maturation of cathepsin D (CatD) and TPP1 remained impaired, indicating incomplete restoration of lysosomal hydrolase processing (Wünkhaus et al., 2024; Unknown Reference). Patch-clamp experiments in these cells confirmed that ML-SA1 variants evoke TRPML1 currents fully blocked by the antagonist ML-SI3, supporting pharmacological specificity in RPE lysosomes (Unknown Reference). Collectively, the body of preclinical work shows that TRPML1 agonism has repeatedly restored facets of lysosomal function (Ca2+ release, acidification, autophagosome–lysosome fusion, exocytosis, TFEB activation) across cell types and in injured retina, though complete correction of all lysosomal abnormalities (e.g., hydrolase maturation) is not assured in all disease contexts (Pollmanns et al., 2022; Yan et al., 2021; Unknown Reference).

No studies in the present corpus demonstrate ML-SA1 use in dry AMD animal models, in RPE models of drusen/lipofuscin accumulation specific to AMD, or in clinical AMD studies; however, in RPE, lipid accumulation was shown to blunt ML-SA1–evoked TRPML1 Ca2+ release, and TRPML1 KO RPE exhibited increased autofluorescence, mechanistically connecting TRPML1 functionality to handling of autofluorescent/neutral lipid material relevant to AMD pathophysiology (Más Gómez et al., 2018). The class of TRPML1 agonists (including ML-SA1) is being investigated preclinically for lysosomal storage and neurodegenerative indications, reinforcing translational plausibility even in the absence of AMD-specific studies (Wang et al., 2023).

Mechanism of Action  
TRPML1 is a lysosomal six-transmembrane TRP superfamily channel permeable to Ca2+ and other cations that localizes primarily to late endosomal/lysosomal membranes via cytosolic dileucine motifs, where it mediates lumen-to-cytosol ion efflux in response to endogenous and pharmacological gating (Wang et al., 2023; Unknown Reference). Physiological activation is conferred by the late endosomal/lysosomal phosphoinositide PI(3,5)P2, whose 3′ phosphate directly stabilizes an open-channel conformation; PI(4,5)P2 acts as an antagonist enriched at the plasma membrane, ensuring compartmental specificity (Wang et al., 2023). TRPML1-dependent lysosomal Ca2+ release acts as a master regulator of lysosomal/autophagic homeostasis via several calcium-sensitive effectors: calcineurin dephosphorylates TFEB to drive its nuclear translocation and induce the CLEAR network (lysosomal biogenesis and function genes), whereas CaMKKβ activation promotes autophagosome biogenesis; additional roles include regulation of vesicle trafficking, membrane fusion/fission, lysosomal reformation, exocytosis, and mitochondria–lysosome contacts (Wang et al., 2023; Tedeschi et al., 2025; Unknown Reference).

ML-SA1 is a synthetic agonist that binds TRPML channels and increases channel open probability, functionally mimicking or synergizing with PI(3,5)P2 to produce lysosomal Ca2+ efflux (Wang et al., 2023). In multiple cell systems, ML-SA1 triggers rapid lysosomal Ca2+ release measured by cytosolic Ca2+ reporters and genetically encoded TRPML1-proximal indicators; responses are abolished or blunted by TRPML1 gene knockout, lysosomal rupture, or pharmacological inhibitors, confirming that cytosolic Ca2+ rises originate from lysosomes through TRPML1 (Más Gómez et al., 2018). In RPE cells, ML-SA1-evoked Ca2+ signals were insensitive to extracellular Ca2+ removal and ER Ca2+ depletion with thapsigargin, but were eliminated by lysosomal disruption with GPN and absent in TRPML1−/− RPE, demonstrating the specificity and lysosomal source in the cell type of interest (Más Gómez et al., 2018).

Downstream of TRPML1 activation, ML-SA1 drives a coordinated set of lysosome-centric responses:  
- Autophagosome–lysosome fusion and maturation: ML-SA1 increases conversion of LC3-positive autophagosomes into acidic autolysosomes and shifts cargo (e.g., α-synuclein) into LAMP1-positive, acidified vesicles; notably, ML-SA1 can partially override bafilomycin A1 blocks on fusion/acidification, consistent with a Ca2+-dependent maturation/fusion mechanism (Pollmanns et al., 2022).  
- Lysosomal acidification: In primary neurons, ML-SA1 rapidly acidifies endolysosomes in a TRPML1-dependent manner, coincident with reduced LDL-induced amyloidogenesis and BACE1 localization/activity in endolysosomes (Hui et al., 2019).  
- TFEB signaling and lysosomal biogenesis/exocytosis: TRPML1 activation promotes TFEB nuclear translocation and transcription of lysosomal/autophagy genes; in ARPE-19 CLN3-KO cells, ML-SA5 triggered TFEB translocation and increased surface LAMP1 within 90 minutes, consistent with enhanced lysosomal exocytosis, and reduced lysosomal glycerophosphodiester loads (Tedeschi et al., 2025; Wünkhaus et al., 2024).  
- Proteostasis correction: ML-SA1 reduces α-synuclein protein/aggregates in cell models by steering cargo into maturing autolysosomes and promoting degradative flux; these effects require autophagy, PI(3,5)P2, and cytosolic Ca2+, aligning with TRPML1’s canonical signaling dependencies (Pollmanns et al., 2022).  
- Neuroprotection via autophagy induction and oxidative stress reduction: In vivo in retinal detachment, subretinal ML-SA1 increased LC3-II/LC3-I and Beclin-1, reduced cleaved caspase-3, decreased ROS, preserved photoreceptors, and prevented injury-induced TRPML1 degradation (Yan et al., 2021).

Two mechanism-linked constraints merit emphasis for AMD translation. First, lysosomal lipid accumulation impairs TRPML1 function in RPE: desipramine (ASM inhibition), PIKfyve inhibition (YM201636), and chloroquine/U18666A-induced neutral lipid/lipofuscin models decreased ML-SA1–evoked Ca2+ release without reducing TRPML1 mRNA or total lysosomal Ca2+, indicating functional channel inhibition by lipidized lysosomal membranes—an AMD-relevant condition (Más Gómez et al., 2018). Second, in CLN3-deficient RPE, TRPML1 activation improved substrate clearance and TFEB signaling but did not restore the mature, active forms of CatD and TPP1 despite increased transcripts and precursor/intermediate protein levels, pointing to persistent hydrolase processing defects not corrected by TRPML1 agonism alone (Unknown Reference). These observations suggest that while ML-SA1 should restore key steps (Ca2+ efflux, fusion/acidification, TFEB), lysosomal lipid environments typical of aged RPE may constrain efficacy, and hydrolase maturation deficits—if present in AMD—may not normalize solely via TRPML1 activation (Más Gómez et al., 2018; Unknown Reference).

Expected Effect  
Assay/System: RPE cells relevant to dry AMD pathophysiology, focusing on phagosome–lysosome fusion, lysosomal acidification, TFEB signaling, and clearance of drusen precursors.

Expected pharmacodynamic cascade:  
1) Channel activation and lysosomal Ca2+ efflux. ML-SA1 will directly activate TRPML1 on RPE lysosomes, eliciting cytosolic Ca2+ rises that persist in Ca2+-free external medium and are abolished by lysosomal disruption, as shown in mouse primary RPE, hesRPE, and ARPE-19 models; absence of response in TRPML1−/− RPE confirms target dependence (Más Gómez et al., 2018). TRPML1 expression in ARPE-19 is documented at the mRNA and functional current levels, supporting target presence in the proposed assay system (Wünkhaus et al., 2024; Unknown Reference).

2) Calcineurin-TFEB pathway activation and lysosomal program induction. TRPML1-mediated Ca2+ release will activate calcineurin, drive TFEB dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation, and upregulate CLEAR network genes that boost lysosomal biogenesis and function, as established for TRPML1 signaling; in RPE (CLN3-KO), ML-SA5 triggered TFEB nuclear import in both WT and mutant cells, indicating pathway engagement in ARPE-19 (Wang et al., 2023; Wünkhaus et al., 2024).

3) Lysosomal acidification and maturation of degradative organelles. ML-SA1 is expected to increase autophagosome–lysosome fusion and acidification, based on multiple cell models where ML-SA1 shifts LC3-positive vesicles toward acidic autolysosomes and augments LAMP1 colocalization; ML-SA1 also acidified neuronal endolysosomes within minutes in a TRPML1-dependent manner (Pollmanns et al., 2022; Hui et al., 2019). In retinal tissue in vivo (detachment model), TRPML1 activation promoted autolysosome formation by EM and upregulated LC3-II/LC3-I and Beclin-1, consistent with increased degradative flux (Yan et al., 2021).

4) Enhancement of degradative capacity and cargo clearance. By accelerating fusion and acidification, ML-SA1 should facilitate catabolic processing of outer segment phagosomes and lipoprotein-like cargoes that contribute to drusen precursors; in RPE knockout models of TRPML1, basal autofluorescence increases, supporting a role for TRPML1 in handling autofluorescent material, and ML-SA1-evoked TRPML1 activity is diminished by lysosomal lipid accumulation typical of aged RPE, highlighting both the therapeutic rationale and a potential barrier (Más Gómez et al., 2018; Wünkhaus et al., 2024; Unknown Reference).

Mechanistic uncertainties relevant to the hypothesis include the extent to which ML-SA1 will normalize hydrolase maturation (and consequently proteolysis) in aged or AMD RPE, given the CLN3-KO observation that mature CatD and TPP1 were not restored despite TFEB activation; while acidification generally supports protease activity, direct evidence of increased CatD activity post-TRPML1 activation in RPE is not provided here, and in CLN3-KO the maturation block persisted (Unknown Reference). Nevertheless, the acidic shift and fusion acceleration documented with ML-SA1 in neurons and the autophagy induction in retina argue for improved lysosomal degradative competence, which, together with increased exocytosis, should reduce accumulation of drusen precursors, albeit potentially incompletely if hydrolase processing bottlenecks remain (Hui et al., 2019; Yan et al., 2021; Wünkhaus et al., 2024).

Two context-specific modifiers should be anticipated:  
- Lysosomal lipid load: ML-SA1 responses are blunted by lysosomal lipid accumulation in RPE; thus, efficacy in dry AMD may require co-strategies that reduce lysosomal lipid burden or potentiate TRPML1 gating under lipidized conditions (e.g., via synergistic endogenous PI(3,5)P2, or adjuncts such as BK channel activators shown to enhance ML-SA1 responses in lipid storage contexts) (Más Gómez et al., 2018; Zhong et al., 2016).  
- TRPML isoform engagement: ML-SA1 can activate TRPML3 under certain expression conditions; while TRPML3’s role in RPE is less defined, off-target TRPML3 activation may modulate lysosomal transport and ion fluxes, warranting selectivity profiling in RPE (Unknown Reference).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
- Direct target validation in RPE: ML-SA1 elicits TRPML1-dependent lysosomal Ca2+ release in primary mouse RPE, hesRPE, and ARPE-19 cells, with responses absent in TRPML1−/− RPE and abolished by lysosomal disruption; this is the critical first step in the proposed mechanism and is robustly documented (Más Gómez et al., 2018).  
- Disease-mechanism alignment with AMD biology: Lysosomal lipid/lipofuscin accumulation—central to AMD—functionally inhibits TRPML1 signaling in RPE, directly linking a hallmark AMD stressor to the TRPML1 pathway that ML-SA1 targets; RPE from ABCA4−/− mice (linked to retinal lipid/lipofuscin pathology) exhibit impaired TRPML1-mediated Ca2+ release (Más Gómez et al., 2018). This alignment makes TRPML1 activation a rational corrective lever.  
- Downstream pathway evidence: ML-SA1/ML-SA agonism reproducibly enhances autophagosome–lysosome fusion and acidification, reduces proteotoxic burden (α-synuclein), and activates TFEB-dependent and TFEB-independent lysosomal functions across cell systems; in retina, subretinal ML-SA1 invokes autophagy, reduces ROS/apoptosis, and preserves structure/function after detachment, supporting ocular pharmacology and neuroprotection (Pollmanns et al., 2022; Yan et al., 2021).  
- Translational momentum: The TRPML1 agonist class is actively explored preclinically for LSD and neurodegenerative indications; Merck’s acquisition of Calporta underscores perceived therapeutic potential of TRPML1 agonism, supporting feasibility of a development path if RPE-specific efficacy is confirmed (Wang et al., 2023; Van Acker et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2023).

Limitations and risks  
- No AMD-specific in vivo data: There are no reports here of ML-SA1 tested in dry AMD or GA animal models, nor in clinical AMD studies; all AMD relevance is inferential from RPE biophysics, lipid-induced TRPML1 impairment, and generalized lysosomal/autophagy correction (Más Gómez et al., 2018). This gap means translational extrapolation to drusen clearance and GA slowing remains unproven.  
- Lipid-dependent resistance: Lysosomal lipid accumulation diminishes ML-SA1 responses in RPE without reducing TRPML1 expression or total lysosomal Ca2+, implying that aged/AMD RPE may exhibit attenuated pharmacodynamics; combination strategies or higher potency agonists (e.g., ML-SA5) may be needed to overcome lipidized lysosomal membranes (Más Gómez et al., 2018; Wünkhaus et al., 2024).  
- Hydrolase maturation not universally rescued: In CLN3-deficient RPE, TRPML1 activation failed to restore mature CatD and TPP1 despite TFEB induction, indicating that some degradative deficits—potentially relevant to AMD—may not normalize solely via TRPML1 activation; this could limit improvements in cathepsin-dependent proteolysis of drusen precursors (Unknown Reference).  
- Selectivity considerations: ML-SA1 can activate other TRPML family members (e.g., TRPML3) in overexpression systems, and pan-TRPML agonism in RPE may have uncharacterized effects on lysosomal trafficking and axonal transport; selectivity profiling in RPE and concentration-response mapping are needed to de-risk off-target TRPML isoform engagement (Unknown Reference).  
- Context-dependent acidification: ML-SA1 acidifies neuronal endolysosomes and enhances degradative flux, but direct demonstrations of increased CatD activity in RPE after ML-SA1 are not provided here; given the CLN3-KO findings on hydrolase maturation, reliance on acidification alone may not guarantee improved protease activity in diseased RPE (Hui et al., 2019; Unknown Reference).

Development guidance  
Given the strong RPE target validation and consistent downstream biology, ML-SA1 (or a more potent TRPML1 agonist such as ML-SA5) is a credible mechanistic candidate for dry AMD; however, AMD-relevant pharmacology must be demonstrated directly. The most decision-informative steps are:  
- Establish efficacy in AMD-relevant RPE contexts: Test ML-SA1 in iPSC-derived human RPE from older donors and/or RPE cultured with A2E/lipofuscin and oxidized lipids to recapitulate lysosomal lipidization; quantify TRPML1 Ca2+ responses, TFEB translocation, lysosomal pH, autophagosome–lysosome fusion, and cargo clearance (e.g., ApoE/cholesterol-rich particles, complement-containing deposits), benchmarking against the lipid-impairment reported in RPE (Más Gómez et al., 2018).  
- Measure hydrolase activity and maturation: Directly assay CatD and other cathepsin activities and maturation states post-ML-SA1 in lipidized RPE; if maturation remains deficient, evaluate combinations with agents that restore hydrolase processing (e.g., modulators of endolysosomal trafficking or mannose-6-phosphate receptor pathways) (Unknown Reference).  
- Explore potentiation strategies: Consider co-targeting known TRPML1 modulators to overcome lipidized lysosomal membranes—examples include augmenting PI(3,5)P2 availability or adding BK channel activators, which rescued impaired ML-SA1 responses and reduced storage in NPC-related settings (Wang et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2016).  
- Prioritize potency and exposure: Given blunted responses in lipidized RPE, evaluate higher-potency ML-SA chemotypes (ML-SA5 EC50 ~80 nM in a TFE3/TFEB-related translocation assay vs. ML-SA1 in the µM range) for superior pharmacodynamics; subretinal tolerability is supported by RD studies but needs AMD-model confirmation (Wünkhaus et al., 2024; Yan et al., 2021).  
- In vivo validation in AMD models: Move to aged/multiple-hit models that accumulate sub-RPE deposits (e.g., high-fat diet, light exposure, Abca4−/− backgrounds) to test whether TRPML1 agonism reduces drusen-like material, preserves RPE function, and slows GA-like endpoints; note that Abca4−/− RPE exhibited impaired TRPML1 Ca2+ release, making it a relevant sensitized background to assay pharmacological rescue (Más Gómez et al., 2018).

Conclusion  
ML-SA1 is a tractable, mechanistically well-supported TRPML1 agonist with direct RPE target validation and robust evidence that its downstream effects—lysosomal Ca2+ release, TFEB activation, autophagosome–lysosome fusion/acidification, and exocytosis—can correct core lysosomal deficits that plausibly drive drusen precursor accumulation in dry AMD (Más Gómez et al., 2018; Pollmanns et al., 2022; Wünkhaus et al., 2024). The principal risks are the lipid-dependence of TRPML1 dysfunction in RPE and the incomplete rescue of hydrolase maturation observed in CLN3-deficient RPE, both of which may attenuate the magnitude of effect on complex AMD cargoes and necessitate combination strategies or use of more potent agonists (Más Gómez et al., 2018; Wünkhaus et al., 2024). On balance, the mechanistic specificity, ocular proof-of-concept in retinal detachment, and extensive preclinical precedent for lysosomal restoration argue that ML-SA1 (or next-generation TRPML1 agonists) merits prioritized evaluation in AMD-relevant RPE assays and in vivo models, with an early focus on overcoming lipidized lysosomal resistance and verifying improvements in both acidification and hydrolase function (Yan et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023; Tedeschi et al., 2025).

Strength of evidence for the dry AMD hypothesis: moderate, driven by strong RPE target biology and retinal neuroprotection data but constrained by the absence of AMD-specific in vivo studies and by lipid-imposed pharmacodynamic limits (Más Gómez et al., 2018; Yan et al., 2021). If forthcoming AMD-relevant experiments confirm that TRPML1 activation restores lysosomal Ca2+ signaling and degradative capacity in lipidized RPE, and that drusen precursors are reduced, ML-SA1/ML-SA5 would represent a differentiated, mechanism-based approach compared with broad mTOR inhibition, with the potential to correct proximal lysosomal defects underlying GA progression (Wang et al., 2023; Pollmanns et al., 2022).

Detailed, component-wise assessment  

- Target presence and engagement in RPE: TRPML1 expression and function are present in ARPE-19 and primary RPE; ML-SA1 evokes lysosomal Ca2+ release that is TRPML1-dependent (absent in TRPML1−/− RPE), establishing on-target engagement (Más Gómez et al., 2018).  
- Relevance of TRPML1 signaling to AMD pathobiology: Lysosomal lipid/lipofuscin accumulation impairs TRPML1 function in RPE and is germane to AMD; RPE autofluorescence increases in TRPML1 KO, linking the channel to handling of drusen-like material (Más Gómez et al., 2018).  
- Downstream mechanisms aligned with therapeutic hypothesis: ML-SA1 triggers calcineurin-TFEB signaling, augments autophagosome–lysosome fusion and acidification, and enhances exocytosis—mechanisms expected to improve clearance of drusen precursors (Wang et al., 2023; Pollmanns et al., 2022).  
- Evidence for improved degradative flux: In neurons, ML-SA1 acidifies endolysosomes and reduces Aβ/BACE1 pathology; in retina in vivo, ML-SA1 increases autophagy markers and preserves photoreceptors; in RPE disease models, TRPML1 activation decreases stored substrates and reduces lysosomal surface area (Hui et al., 2019; Yan et al., 2021; Wünkhaus et al., 2024).  
- Constraints/unknowns: Lipid-imposed TRPML1 inhibition in RPE may limit efficacy; CatD/TPP1 maturation not rescued in CLN3-KO RPE; no direct AMD data; potential TRPML3 co-activation (Más Gómez et al., 2018; Unknown Reference).

Recommendation  
Advance ML-SA1 (and evaluate ML-SA5 as a higher-potency comparator) into AMD-relevant RPE assays with lipidized lysosomes, incorporating quantitative readouts of TRPML1 Ca2+ signaling, TFEB translocation, lysosomal pH, hydrolase maturation/activity, and cargo clearance, and then proceed to in vivo testing in drusen-accumulating models if in vitro data are supportive; in parallel, explore combination strategies (e.g., BK channel activation) to overcome lipid-dependent resistance to TRPML1 agonism (Más Gómez et al., 2018; Wünkhaus et al., 2024; Zhong et al., 2016).
