Proposal for Lithium chloride (Sigma L9650)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Lithium chloride (Sigma L9650) is an inorganic salt belonging to the family of lithium compounds that has been in use for several decades, primarily as a mood stabilizer in the treatment of bipolar disorder. It is synthesized by combining lithium carbonate or lithium hydroxide with hydrochloric acid, yielding a highly water‐soluble salt that is widely available for both clinical and experimental research purposes (Carmassi et al., 2016; Vallée et al., 2021). As a prototypical member of the lithium salts, lithium chloride has been repurposed in recent years as a candidate for neuroprotection because of its ability to inhibit glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK3β), a serine/threonine kinase involved in numerous intracellular signaling cascades. Lithium’s chemical simplicity belies its complex pharmacology; its effects extend well beyond mood stabilization to encompass modulation of cell survival, inflammatory responses, and protein degradation pathways. In this context, lithium salts are now recognized as small molecule modulators that can affect intracellular transcriptional programs, including nuclear import of factors such as transcription factor EB (TFEB) and β-catenin, which in turn control the expression of genes necessary for lysosomal biogenesis and autophagic flux (Carmassi et al., 2016; Vallée et al., 2021). These mechanistic insights, originally developed in studies of neurodegenerative and psychiatric diseases, have motivated investigations into whether lithium chloride might be repurposed to address the autophagic and lysosomal dysfunction observed in dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD), particularly in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) (Carmassi et al., 2016; Vallée et al., 2021).

Therapeutic History:  
Lithium salts have a long-standing clinical history in psychiatry, especially in the management of bipolar disorder, where their dosing and long-term safety profiles have been thoroughly characterized. In addition, numerous preclinical studies in neuroscience have demonstrated that lithium can reduce tau phosphorylation and improve neuronal cell survival, partly by inhibiting GSK3β and modulating downstream targets such as β-catenin and Bcl-2 (Carmassi et al., 2016; Vallée et al., 2021). Lithium has been evaluated in several neurodegenerative contexts—for instance, it has been examined for its potential benefits in Alzheimer’s disease, Huntington’s disease, and Parkinson’s disease, with some studies reporting modest improvements in neuroprotection and behavioral outcomes, although clinical results have been mixed (Singh et al., 2022; Vallée et al., 2021). More specifically, preclinical data in the central nervous system have shown that prolonged exposure to lithium increases cortical grey matter and enhances neuronal markers such as N-acetyl-aspartate, suggesting that lithium exerts trophic and neuroprotective effects (Carmassi et al., 2016). In the ocular arena, while lithium’s use has been predominantly described in the context of glaucoma—in which its inhibition of GSK3β was shown to modulate WNT/β-catenin signaling in trabecular meshwork cells—the literature offers no direct clinical or in-depth preclinical evaluation of lithium chloride in dry AMD or for modulating autophagy specifically in RPE cells (Singh et al., 2022; Vallée et al., 2021). A search in clinical trial registries has not identified any trials using lithium chloride for macular degeneration, underscoring a notable gap between the neuroprotective models in which lithium has been studied and its potential application in dry AMD (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.).

Mechanism of Action:  
Lithium chloride’s mechanisms of action are multifaceted, but a significant aspect relevant to the dry AMD hypothesis is its ability to inhibit GSK3β. GSK3β normally phosphorylates a variety of substrates including β-catenin and TFEB, leading to cytosolic retention and subsequent degradation; inhibition of GSK3β by lithium therefore results in stabilization and nuclear accumulation of β-catenin and promotes TFEB nuclear entry (Carmassi et al., 2016; Vallée et al., 2021). In detail, lithium binds directly to the magnesium-sensitive site of GSK3β and also promotes its inhibitory N-terminal serine phosphorylation, leading to a dose-dependent suppression of GSK3β activity (Carmassi et al., 2016). The release of β-catenin from the destruction complex allows it to accumulate in the nucleus, where it acts as a transcriptional coactivator for genes that contribute to cell survival and homeostasis, including those involved in lysosomal biogenesis (Carmassi et al., 2016; Vallée et al., 2021). Similarly, TFEB is a master transcription factor that coordinates the expression of a variety of genes encoding lysosomal enzymes and autophagy-related proteins. Under basal conditions, phosphorylation retains TFEB in the cytosol; inhibition of GSK3β can contribute to dephosphorylation of TFEB, thereby facilitating its nuclear translocation and transcriptional activation of targets such as cathepsin D (CTSD) and subunits of the vacuolar ATPase (v-ATPase) that are crucial for lysosomal acidification (Carmassi et al., 2016; Friedman et al., 2015). In neuronal models, lithium treatment has been demonstrated to increase levels of neurotrophic factors and cell survival molecules, as well as to enhance autophagy-mediated clearance of toxic proteins including aggregated forms of tau and other proteins implicated in neurodegenerative disease (Vallée et al., 2021). Furthermore, lithium’s inhibition of inositol monophosphatase further contributes to autophagy induction through an mTOR-independent mechanism, reinforcing its capacity to modulate autophagic flux (Friedman et al., 2015). Despite these promising mechanistic insights, it is important to note that most of the supporting data have been generated in neuronal or non-ocular cell systems; direct evidence for lithium chloride-mediated activation of TFEB/β-catenin signaling and upregulation of lysosomal acidification machinery specifically in RPE cells remains limited (Golestaneh et al., 2018; Guha et al., 2014).

Expected Effect:  
Based on the hypothesis, treatment with lithium chloride in RPE cells is anticipated to inhibit GSK3β, leading to a cascade of intracellular events that restore impaired autophagic flux—a major pathological hallmark in dry AMD. Specifically, inhibition of GSK3β should reduce the phosphorylation-dependent cytosolic retention of TFEB and β-catenin, thereby facilitating their nuclear import. In the nucleus, these transcription factors can activate a broad transcriptional program that upregulates the synthesis of lysosomal proteins such as cathepsin D and components of the v-ATPase complex, ultimately leading to enhanced lysosomal acidification (Carmassi et al., 2016; Friedman et al., 2015). This improved lysosomal function is expected to accelerate the degradation of photoreceptor outer segment debris and other autophagic substrates, leading to a restoration of autophagic flux and a reduction in lipofuscin accumulation—a prominent feature of dry AMD pathology (Golestaneh et al., 2018). In parallel, lithium’s neuroprotective effects, including the upregulation of prosurvival factors like Bcl-2 and the attenuation of proinflammatory signaling pathways, may further support RPE cell survival and function under chronic oxidative stress conditions (Vallée et al., 2021). Given that lysosomal alkalinization is known to impair cathepsin D activity and autophagic degradation in RPE cells, the upregulation of cathepsin D via TFEB activation represents a critical therapeutic target to counteract the reduced degradative capacity observed in dry AMD (Guha et al., 2014). Moreover, the candidate’s reported ocular penetration and long-term safety in other contexts provide a translational rationale, though the literature is notably deficient in direct measurements of RPE-specific outcomes—thus, further studies are required to validate that lithium chloride achieves effective intraocular concentrations without toxicity (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.; Vallée et al., 2021). The expected outcome in an in vitro assay would be an increase in nuclear TFEB and β-catenin as determined by immunocytochemistry and Western blot of nuclear extracts, an increase in mRNA and protein levels of lysosomal acidification machinery (e.g., v-ATPase subunits) and cathepsin D, restoration of autophagic flux as evidenced by normalized LC3-II/LC3-I ratios and p62 degradation, and improved clearance of photoreceptor outer segment-derived material in RPE cell culture models (Golestaneh et al., 2018).

Overall Evaluation:  
The repurposing of lithium chloride for dry age-related macular degeneration is based on a compelling mechanistic rationale that builds upon well-established aspects of lithium’s pharmacology. On one hand, the drug’s ability to inhibit GSK3β—and thereby to promote the nuclear translocation of TFEB and β-catenin—aligns well with the therapeutic goal of upregulating lysosomal gene expression and restoring autophagic flux in RPE cells (Golestaneh et al., 2018). Lithium’s longstanding use in psychiatric medicine has resulted in a rich and well-documented clinical safety profile, and its neuroprotective benefits in various models lend additional support to its potential utility in other degenerative conditions including ocular diseases (Carmassi et al., 2016; Vallée et al., 2021).

However, there are several notable weaknesses and gaps that must be addressed before lithium chloride can be considered a robust therapeutic candidate for dry AMD. First, while preclinical studies in neuronal models offer supportive evidence that lithium can modulate autophagy and enhance lysosomal function, there is a lack of direct data demonstrating these mechanisms in RPE cells specifically (Golestaneh et al., 2018). Second, the pharmacokinetic profile of lithium chloride in the eye—especially the extent to which it penetrates the RPE following systemic or local administration—remains poorly characterized, and this is a critical factor given the importance of achieving therapeutic concentrations in the retina without causing off-target toxicity (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.). Third, although lithium’s long-term safety has been established in psychiatric populations, its known systemic toxicities, including renal and thyroid effects, as well as its narrow therapeutic window, raise concerns about the potential risks of repurposing it for an ocular indication where localized delivery may be necessary to avoid systemic exposure (Singh et al., 2022; Vallée et al., 2021). Finally, the absence of any registered clinical trials or robust preclinical studies in an AMD model means that the translational viability of lithium chloride for this indication is still highly speculative (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.).

Despite these concerns, the strengths of lithium chloride as a repurposed agent for dry AMD are significant. Its ability to target GSK3β provides a unique molecular entry point that could bypass some of the drawbacks of other autophagy modulators that primarily act through mTOR inhibition—a strategy that has yielded mixed clinical results (Friedman et al., 2015; Vallée et al., 2021). Moreover, the multi-modal action of lithium, which encompasses both anti-inflammatory and anti-excitotoxic effects in addition to its autophagy-promoting properties, suggests that it could provide a broad-spectrum protective effect in the stressed cellular environment of the RPE (Carmassi et al., 2016; Vallée et al., 2021). Nonetheless, given the complexity of RPE biology and the multifactorial nature of dry AMD pathogenesis—as evidenced by the alterations in lysosomal pH, reduced cathepsin activity, and compromised membrane trafficking in these cells—any candidate drug must be rigorously evaluated in appropriate RPE or AMD models before clinical translation is attempted (Golestaneh et al., 2018; Guha et al., 2014).

In summary, lithium chloride holds promise as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD through its inhibition of GSK3β and the subsequent activation of nuclear TFEB/β-catenin signaling pathways, which are anticipated to upregulate lysosomal acidification machinery and cathepsin D expression and thereby restore autophagic flux in the RPE. The candidate’s well-documented clinical history and long-term safety in other indications provide additional encouragement for its further preclinical exploration. However, critical issues—such as the need for direct experimental evidence in RPE system models, the establishment of effective and safe ocular dosing regimens with adequate retinal penetration, and thorough evaluations of potential systemic and ocular toxicities—must be rigorously addressed. Until these gaps are filled by focused preclinical and translational research, the use of lithium chloride for dry AMD remains a promising yet still unproven strategy that warrants additional investigation (Carmassi et al., 2016; Friedman et al., 2015; Golestaneh et al., 2018; Guha et al., 2014; Vallée et al., 2021; ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.; Singh et al., 2022).
