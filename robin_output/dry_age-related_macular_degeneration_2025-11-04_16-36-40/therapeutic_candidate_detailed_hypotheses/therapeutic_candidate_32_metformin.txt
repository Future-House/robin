Proposal for Metformin (Sigma D150959)

Abstract—Metformin, a synthetic biguanide derived from galegine (French lilac), is widely used as first-line therapy for type 2 diabetes mellitus and has pleiotropic cellular effects that intersect with age-related macular degeneration (AMD) biology, including AMPK activation, modulation of mTOR signaling, autophagy induction, anti-inflammatory actions, mitochondrial support, anti-senescence effects, and anti-angiogenic signaling in retinal models (Amin et al., 2022). Multiple preclinical studies in RPE cell lines and rodent retinal injury/degeneration models show that metformin preserves RPE barrier integrity, reduces oxidative and inflammatory stress, supports mitochondrial biogenesis, and maintains photoreceptor structure/function, largely via AMPK-dependent pathways; however, dry AMD–specific randomized clinical efficacy data remain limited and observational epidemiology is heterogeneous, with mixed associations across populations, exposure definitions, and AMD subtypes (Amin et al., 2022; Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Gokhale et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2022; Moir et al., 2023; Romdhoniyyah et al., 2021). Critically, while AMPK→mTORC1→autophagy is well-supported in retinal contexts, direct evidence for metformin-driven TFEB nuclear translocation, V-ATPase upregulation, or cathepsin D (CTSD) maturation in human RPE/AMD models is not demonstrated in the excerpted literature, representing material gaps for the central hypothesis (Amin et al., 2022; Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Buonfiglio et al., 2024). Overall, metformin is a biologically plausible, low-cost, and generally safe repurposing candidate for dry AMD that warrants mechanistic confirmation in RPE for lysosome-centric endpoints and rigorous randomized trials across AMD stages (Amin et al., 2022; Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Moir et al., 2023).

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Metformin is a small-molecule biguanide synthesized as a derivative of galegine, a guanidine-class alkaloid originally isolated from the plant Galega officinalis; clinically it has been used for more than 60 years as the most commonly prescribed oral antihyperglycemic agent worldwide (class: biguanides) (Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Amin et al., 2022). Chemically, metformin comprises two coupled guanidine moieties conferring cationic, hydrophilic properties and cellular accumulation via organic cation transporters, consistent with mitochondrial complex I engagement and AMPK pathway activation described in mechanistic literature (Augustin & Atorf, 2023). Beyond glycemic control, metformin exhibits pleiotropic effects—anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidative, anti-angiogenic, anti-apoptotic and anti-senescence—reported in diverse cell types and tissues, many of which intersect with pathways implicated in AMD pathogenesis (oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired proteostasis/autophagy, para-inflammation, vascular dysregulation) (Amin et al., 2022; Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Kozlowski, 2023). In ocular contexts, preclinical work indicates that metformin activates AMPK in RPE and neural retina and can preserve retinal structure and function under oxidative, metabolic, and phototoxic stressors (Amin et al., 2022). Collectively, these properties position metformin as a rational repurposing candidate for dry AMD, wherein RPE dysfunction, impaired autophagy/lysosomal clearance, mitochondrial stress, and chronic inflammation are central pathogenic nodes (Amin et al., 2022; Buonfiglio et al., 2024).

Therapeutic History  
Systemic use: Metformin is established first-line therapy for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and has been investigated broadly for aging-related conditions due to putative geroprotective effects; long-term safety has been documented, including in the Diabetes Prevention Program (DPP) where gastrointestinal intolerance is the most common adverse event and lactic acidosis was not observed under trial conditions, though B12 deficiency can occur with chronic use (Amin et al., 2022; Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Zhou & Xue, 2024). Preclinical ocular use: In retinal models, metformin has been tested systemically (oral/IP) and intravitreally, demonstrating protective effects in RPE and photoreceptors across light-damage, iodate-induced injury, inherited retinal degeneration (rd1/rd10), and oxygen-induced retinopathy (OIR) with readouts including preserved RPE tight junctions (ZO-1, occludin), outer nuclear layer thickness, and ERG amplitudes; notable anti-angiogenic activity includes decreased CNV lesion size and VEGF/VEGFR signaling modulation in several models (Amin et al., 2022). In these preclinical settings, metformin’s ocular benefits are frequently attributed to AMPK activation and downstream autophagy/mitochondrial and anti-inflammatory pathways, without overt ocular toxicity reported in rodents; however, human ocular safety data and pharmacokinetics under intravitreal or high-dose local exposure are not detailed in the excerpts (Amin et al., 2022).

Clinical/epidemiologic AMD history: Observational epidemiology offers mixed signals. Several large retrospective cohorts and case–control analyses associate metformin use with reduced odds or risk of any AMD or early AMD, with dose–duration trends in some datasets (e.g., MarketScan/claims OR 0.93 and propensity-matched HR 0.57; Taiwanese cohorts HR ~0.54 with stronger protection at >4 years and higher average daily doses), and lower odds reported in multiple studies across US, Europe, and Asia (Amin et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2022; Kozlowski, 2023; Moir et al., 2023). Other datasets report null or complex associations: a high-quality UK T2DM cohort found no differential AMD risk with metformin using time-varying exposure models; a U.S. claims analysis focusing on dry AMD reported current use slightly increased dry AMD risk while prior use trended protective; additional analyses show no association or heterogeneity by diabetic retinopathy status (Eton et al., 2022; Gokhale et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2022). Meta-analyses also conflict: a systematic review of diabetics suggested decreased odds of AMD with metformin, whereas a broader 2024 meta-analysis concluded no overall prophylactic/treatment role; a European meta-analysis of antidiabetic/lipid-lowering drugs found reduced AMD prevalence overall but not for late-stage AMD specifically (Elhalag et al., 2024; Mauschitz et al., 2023). Importantly, these retrospective designs face confounding (e.g., smoking, comorbidities, health-care utilization), outcome misclassification (wet vs dry AMD), and exposure misclassification (adherence), limiting causal inference and stage-specific conclusions (Brown et al., 2019; Gokhale et al., 2023; Moir et al., 2023). Prospective interventional testing for dry AMD is underway: a randomized, single-blind Phase II trial of metformin to slow geographic atrophy (GA) progression has been conducted, with publication reported in 2024 (Shen et al., 2024); however, the excerpts do not provide efficacy outcomes in GA and emphasize that randomized evidence remains needed (Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Shen et al., 2024).

No regulatory approval exists for metformin in AMD (dry or wet), and the totality of evidence remains preclinical and observational for efficacy, with one randomized GA study reported but without effect-size details here; therefore, clinical utility in dry AMD is presently unproven (Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Shen et al., 2024).

Mechanism of Action  
Canonical pharmacology: Metformin partially inhibits mitochondrial respiratory complex I, reducing hepatic ATP and increasing AMP/ADP, thereby activating AMPK, the cellular energy sensor that shifts metabolism toward ATP-generating pathways; AMPK-dependent and ‑independent mechanisms operate systemically, including reduced hepatic gluconeogenesis, increased enterocyte glucose uptake/GLP-1 secretion, and microbiome modulation with potential systemic anti-inflammatory effects (Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Kozlowski, 2023).

Retinal/RPE mechanisms: In RPE cell lines (e.g., ARPE-19), metformin increases pAMPK and triggers autophagy, reduces ROS, decreases apoptosis, and preserves tight junction proteins (ZO-1, occludin), thereby maintaining barrier integrity under oxidative challenges (Amin et al., 2022; Augustin & Atorf, 2023). At the molecular level, treatment increases Sirt1 and multiple mitochondrial biogenesis and antioxidant markers (SOD-2, Nrf1, Tfam, PGC-1α, COX-II), restores NADH:NAD+ ratios and ATP, and lowers inflammatory mediators (NF-κB signaling, IL-6) (Amin et al., 2022). These signatures are consistent with AMPK’s coordinate regulation of autophagy and mitochondrial homeostasis observed in RPE/retinal contexts, contributing to improved proteostasis and reduced oxidative stress—key failure modes in dry AMD (Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Amin et al., 2022).

In vivo, systemic or intravitreal metformin preserves photoreceptors and RPE structure/function in light-damage, iodate injury, and inherited degeneration models (rd1, rd10), with ERG preservation and ONL maintenance; mechanistically, AMPK activation is necessary for light-damage protection in an isoform-specific manner (AMPKα2 dependency), and retina-specific Pparδ is not required, supporting an AMPK-centric retinal mechanism rather than PPARδ signaling (Amin et al., 2022). Metformin also dampens retinal senescence and SASP in OIR after intravitreal dosing, with decreased cdkn1a/cdkn2a and NF-κB/IL-6 signaling, implicating anti-senescence immunomodulation as an additional neurovascular protective mechanism (Amin et al., 2022). Anti-angiogenic actions involve AMPK-dependent and ‑independent pathways: decreased VEGFR2 via enhanced ubiquitin–proteasome degradation, altered VEGF-A isoform splicing toward less angiogenic forms, reduced Flk-1 expression, and inhibition of ALK1-mediated angiogenesis; collectively these actions reduce CNV lesion size, endothelial proliferation/migration/tube formation, and neovascularization in OIR, diabetic, and laser-CNV models (Amin et al., 2022; Augustin & Atorf, 2023). Inflammatory suppression includes reductions in ICAM-1/MCP-1 (AMPK-dependent) and NF-κB p65/IL-8 (AMPK-independent), accompanied by decreased leukostasis and cytokines across models; NLRP3 inflammasome inhibition and modulation of macrophage polarization further contribute to reduced para-inflammation (Amin et al., 2022; Dang et al., 2021; Augustin & Atorf, 2023).

mTOR/autophagy axis: AMPK activation by metformin can inhibit mTORC1, activate the ULK complex, and initiate autophagy, which in RPE promotes clearance of damaged organelles, reduces ROS, and protects against oxidative injury—mechanistically vital for countering autophagy/lysosomal dysfunction in AMD (Dang et al., 2021; Zhou & Xue, 2024). Several retinal studies report AMPK-linked increases in autophagosome formation, and some contextual discussion references mTOR modulation in retinal protection, including translational control via EF2K and general proteostasis benefits (e.g., mutant opsin trafficking) with mixed in vivo translation (Amin et al., 2022). While AMPK→mTOR→autophagy activation is strongly implicated, explicit experimental confirmation in the provided corpus for metformin-driven TFEB nuclear translocation, V-ATPase upregulation, or increased cathepsin D maturation/activity in RPE is lacking; several reviews and excerpts explicitly note these as gaps requiring targeted mechanistic studies (Amin et al., 2022; Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Buonfiglio et al., 2024).

Dose/context dependence and AMPK-independent considerations: Some analyses suggest that AMPK activation by metformin in vitro can require suprapharmacologic concentrations and that certain systemic antihyperglycemic effects are AMPK-independent in hepatocytes; in retinal contexts, isoform-specific AMPKα2 dependence is observed for photoreceptor protection, yet disease models can yield mixed outcomes (e.g., limited benefit or worse function in some HFD diabetic retinopathy paradigms), underscoring the need to define dosing, timing, and disease-stage context for retinal efficacy (Amin et al., 2022; Moir et al., 2023). These data caution against assuming uniform AMPK-mediated benefit across all retinal diseases or AMD stages and highlight the importance of pharmacokinetics, tissue exposure, and therapeutic window in the eye (Moir et al., 2023).

Translational addenda—microbiome and lipid handling: Metformin alters gut microbiota and systemic metabolites, which has been linked to reduced retinal neovascularization through a putative “gut–retina axis,” and may also reduce OxLDL uptake and promote cholesterol efflux via AMPK/LXR–ABCA1/G1 signaling, potentially limiting drusen-related lipid accumulation and NLRP3 activation in RPE (Dang et al., 2021; Zhou & Xue, 2024).

Summary of mechanistic sufficiency for the hypothesis: The literature robustly supports metformin’s activation of AMPK in RPE/retina, inhibition of inflammatory and oxidative pathways, modulation of mTOR/autophagy, and preservation of RPE and photoreceptor function in multiple preclinical models—mechanistic axes consistent with dry AMD biology (Amin et al., 2022; Zhou & Xue, 2024). However, direct experimental evidence in the provided corpus for metformin-induced TFEB nuclear translocation, increased V-ATPase expression, or enhanced cathepsin D maturation/activity in human RPE is not shown; several reviews explicitly note TFEB/V-ATPase/CTSD endpoints as critical gaps that merit targeted validation in AMD-relevant assays (Amin et al., 2022; Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Buonfiglio et al., 2024).

Expected Effect  
Assay-level predictions for RPE models: In human RPE cell lines and iPSC-RPE, metformin at concentrations compatible with cellular AMPK activation is expected to increase pAMPK (Thr172), reduce mTORC1 activity (e.g., decreased pS6 and p4EBP1), and initiate autophagy (increased LC3-II with enhanced flux in the presence of bafilomycin, reduction in p62/SQSTM1 under steady-state), while decreasing ROS and apoptosis under oxidative stress (e.g., H2O2), preserving tight junction integrity (occludin, ZO-1), and improving mitochondrial function (increased ATP, restored NADH:NAD+ ratio, increased PGC-1α/Tfam/Nrf1 and SOD-2) (Amin et al., 2022). Published in vitro data already support many of these endpoints: metformin reduced ROS and apoptosis, increased pAMPK and antioxidative molecules including Sirt1/Nrf2, and preserved occludin/ZO-1 in ARPE-19 challenged with H2O2 or glyoxal; AMPK inhibition abrogated the protection, indicating dependence on AMPK signaling (Amin et al., 2022). In oxidant or lipid-rich RPE conditions, decreased NF-κB activation and inflammasome signaling (e.g., NLRP3) would be anticipated, consistent with prior reports of metformin inhibiting NF-κB and NLRP3 activation in ocular/immune models (Augustin & Atorf, 2023).

Hypothesized lysosomal/TFEB endpoints: The working hypothesis proposes that AMPK activation by metformin will inhibit mTORC1, promote TFEB nuclear translocation, increase V-ATPase expression, enhance lysosomal acidification, and increase cathepsin D (CTSD) maturation, restoring autophagic flux and preventing RPE degeneration. While the AMPK→mTORC1→autophagy cascade is supported in RPE and retina, the provided literature does not directly demonstrate metformin-induced TFEB nuclear translocation, V-ATPase upregulation, or CTSD maturation increases in RPE; thus, these are testable predictions rather than established metformin actions in RPE (Amin et al., 2022; Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Augustin & Atorf, 2023). Given TFEB’s role in lysosomal biogenesis and function, metformin’s dampening of mTORC1 could plausibly facilitate TFEB nuclear entry, but explicit experimental validation (e.g., TFEB nuclear localization quantification, lysosomal pH assays, V-ATPase subunit expression/activity, pro-CTSD→mature CTSD processing and enzymatic activity assays) is needed to confirm the hypothesized mechanism in AMD-relevant RPE models (Dang et al., 2021; Ebeling et al., 2022).

Target expression plausibility: AMPK is expressed in RPE and retina; stimulation prevents RPE and photoreceptor degeneration in multiple mouse models, and metformin increases pAMPK in human RPE cell lines—indicating sufficient expression and pathway competence for drug action in the assay system (Elhalag et al., 2024; Amin et al., 2022). Downstream readouts of autophagy and mitochondrial biogenesis/antioxidant pathways (LC3-II, PGC-1α, SOD-2, Nrf1, Tfam) have been measured and improved with metformin in RPE/retinal models, supporting assay feasibility and expected directions of effect (Amin et al., 2022).

In vivo expected effects in dry-AMD–relevant models: In toxin- and stress-driven models of RPE/photoreceptor degeneration (e.g., sodium iodate, light-induced damage) and inherited retinal degeneration, metformin is expected to preserve RPE morphology and function, maintain ONL thickness, and preserve ERG signals, consistent with reported benefits mediated by AMPK activation; however, model- and disease-stage–specific responses can vary, and negative/neutral results have been documented in some diabetic retinopathy paradigms, underscoring the need to test across multiple AMD-relevant models and to incorporate timing and dose-response studies (Amin et al., 2022). In models of para-inflammation and senescence, metformin is expected to reduce SASP mediators and senescent RPE or retinal cells, aligning with intravitreal metformin effects in OIR and broader anti-senescence literature (Amin et al., 2022; Kozlowski, 2023).

Clinical expected effects: Based on observational associations, any protective effect may be stronger for early or non-exudative AMD and may display dose–duration dependence; however, epidemiologic heterogeneity and potential confounding mean that no specific effect size can be assumed, and randomized trial confirmation in geographic atrophy and earlier AMD stages is necessary to define clinical benefit and exposure requirements (Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Liang et al., 2022).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
- Mechanistic convergence on AMD biology: Metformin engages AMPK-dependent and ‑independent processes that directly counter core dry AMD pathomechanisms—oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired autophagy/proteostasis, para-inflammation/senescence, and, secondarily, maladaptive vascular/angio-inflammatory signaling—supported by coherent molecular readouts (pAMPK↑, autophagy induction, mitochondrial biogenesis markers↑, NF-κB/NLRP3↓) in RPE and retinal models (Amin et al., 2022; Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Zhou & Xue, 2024). Preclinical efficacy in preserving RPE barrier proteins and photoreceptor function across multiple insults strengthens biological plausibility for dry AMD (Amin et al., 2022).
- Epidemiologic signal and translational momentum: Multiple large retrospective analyses show reduced odds/risk of AMD—often with dose–duration trends—among metformin users, and a randomized GA trial has been conducted, indicating clinical interest and feasibility for prospective testing (Amin et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2022; Moir et al., 2023; Shen et al., 2024). Meta-analytic work in diabetics suggests potential protective associations, especially in early AMD, reinforcing the rationale to test in non-diabetic AMD populations with controlled exposure (Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Liang et al., 2022).
- Favorable systemic safety and low cost: Decades of broad clinical use, with well-characterized safety signals (GI intolerance, B12 deficiency; rare lactic acidosis risk under renal/hepatic compromise), make metformin attractive from a risk–benefit and access perspective for an elderly AMD population, assuming ocular safety is confirmed (Amin et al., 2022; Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Zhou & Xue, 2024).
- Ocular delivery flexibility and mechanistic redundancy: Both systemic and intravitreal dosing have produced retinal benefits in animals; intravitreal delivery achieved senescence suppression independent of anti-VEGF effects, suggesting non-overlapping mechanisms that could complement existing therapies (Amin et al., 2022). Mechanistic redundancy (anti-oxidant, autophagy, anti-inflammatory, anti-angiogenic) could enhance robustness in multifactorial AMD pathophysiology (Amin et al., 2022; Dang et al., 2021).

Weaknesses and Gaps  
- Critical mechanistic gaps relative to the hypothesis: Direct evidence in human RPE/AMD-relevant systems for metformin-driven TFEB nuclear translocation, increases in V-ATPase expression/activity, and enhanced cathepsin D maturation/activity is absent in the provided literature; several reviews explicitly highlight TFEB/V-ATPase/CTSD endpoints as missing and necessary to establish lysosome-centric restoration of autophagic flux (Amin et al., 2022; Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Buonfiglio et al., 2024). Without these data, the proposed AMPK–TFEB–lysosome axis remains inferential in RPE.
- Heterogeneous and contradictory epidemiology: While many retrospective datasets suggest benefit (often in T2DM cohorts), high-quality time-varying analyses and some claims datasets report null or even an increased risk signal for current users regarding dry AMD; meta-analyses differ in conclusions, and late-stage AMD associations are particularly uncertain (Elhalag et al., 2024; Gokhale et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2022; Mauschitz et al., 2023). Confounding (smoking, DR severity, comedications), misclassification (wet vs dry), and exposure uncertainty limit inferential strength (Brown et al., 2019; Moir et al., 2023; Gokhale et al., 2023).
- Limited randomized clinical efficacy data in dry AMD: A GA randomized trial has been reported, but efficacy outcomes are not detailed in the excerpts; prior DPP follow-up imaging showed no significant AMD prevalence difference between metformin, lifestyle, and placebo, though that study was not designed for AMD endpoints; consequently, clinical efficacy remains unproven (Moir et al., 2023; Shen et al., 2024).
- Context dependence and dose constraints: Retinal benefits can be model- and isoform-specific (AMPKα2 dependence) and mixed in metabolic DR models; some analyses suggest suprapharmacologic concentrations may be necessary for robust AMPK activation in vitro, raising translational questions regarding retinal exposure at tolerated systemic doses, and arguing for ocular PK and target-engagement studies (Amin et al., 2022; Moir et al., 2023).
- Ocular safety and pharmacokinetics: Human ocular safety data for chronic exposure (systemic or local) in older non-diabetic AMD patients are not detailed here; intravitreal metformin safety in humans is uncharacterized in these excerpts, necessitating staged ocular toxicology and PK prior to (or within) clinical trials (Amin et al., 2022; Moir et al., 2023).

Development Implications and Recommendations  
- Mechanism validation in RPE: Prioritize targeted studies in human iPSC-RPE and donor-derived RPE to test the hypothesized AMPK→mTORC1→TFEB→lysosome axis. Required assays: TFEB nuclear localization (immunocytochemistry, subcellular fractionation), lysosomal pH (LysoSensor/LysoTracker), V-ATPase subunit expression and activity (ATPase assays), CTSD maturation (pro/mature CTSD Western) and enzymatic activity, autophagic flux (LC3 turnover with/without lysosomal blockade), and rescue experiments with AMPK and TFEB perturbation (e.g., CRISPRi/siRNA) (Amin et al., 2022; Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Ebeling et al., 2022).
- Translational pharmacology: Define retinal/RPE exposure–response using clinically relevant systemic concentrations and ocular distribution modeling; consider topical/periocular/intravitreal delivery if systemic exposure is insufficient for retinal AMPK engagement and lysosome-centric endpoints, with rigorous ocular toxicology (Moir et al., 2023).
- Indication and stage targeting: Given epidemiologic signals skew toward early/non-exudative AMD and limited evidence for late AMD benefit, prioritize early/intermediate AMD cohorts and GA progression trials with mechanistic biomarkers (RPE autofluorescence dynamics, OCT-based RPE/ONL metrics, aqueous humor cytokines/miRNAs) to increase sensitivity to disease-modifying effects (Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Liang et al., 2022; Moir et al., 2023).
- Clinical trial design: Incorporate robust confounder control and stratification (smoking, DR status, renal function, OCT1 polymorphisms) and embed target engagement (e.g., peripheral blood or aqueous biomarkers of AMPK/mTOR/autophagy) and safety surveillance (B12 levels, GI tolerability, lactate in at-risk) to bridge mechanism to clinical outcome (Gokhale et al., 2023; Zhou & Xue, 2024).
- Combination/adjunct strategies: Given non-overlapping mechanisms with anti-VEGF and complement inhibitors, explore combinatorial approaches in late-stage disease (e.g., GA with complement inhibition) if target engagement and safety are demonstrated (Amin et al., 2022; Shen et al., 2024).

Conclusion: Metformin is a credible repurposing candidate for dry AMD on the strength of convergent mechanistic rationale (AMPK activation, autophagy/mitochondrial support, anti-inflammatory/anti-senescence actions), robust preclinical retinal protection across models, and suggestive—but inconsistent—observational human data; however, decisive gaps remain for the central lysosome-centric hypothesis (TFEB/V-ATPase/CTSD in RPE) and for randomized clinical efficacy, especially in GA. The program should proceed with focused mechanistic validation of TFEB/lysosome endpoints in RPE, retinal target engagement and pharmacology at clinically feasible exposures, and staged, biomarker-rich randomized trials in early/intermediate AMD and GA to quantify efficacy and safety. Until lysosomal endpoints are confirmed and clinical benefit demonstrated, the hypothesis that metformin restores RPE autophagic flux via TFEB/V-ATPase/CTSD to prevent dry AMD remains plausible but unproven (Amin et al., 2022; Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Shen et al., 2024; Gokhale et al., 2023; Elhalag et al., 2024).
