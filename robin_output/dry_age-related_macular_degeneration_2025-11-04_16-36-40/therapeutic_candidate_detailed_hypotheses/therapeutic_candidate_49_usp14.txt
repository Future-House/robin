Proposal for USP14 inhibitor IU1 (Sigma SML0010)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
USP14 inhibitor IU1 (Sigma SML0010) is a synthetic small-molecule compound originally discovered through high-throughput screening efforts aimed at identifying inhibitors of deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) associated with the proteasome. IU1 belongs to the class of small-molecule DUB inhibitors that specifically target USP14, a proteasome-associated deubiquitinase responsible for trimming ubiquitin chains from substrates before their degradation by the 26S proteasome. Early medicinal chemistry studies led to the development of IU1 and its more potent analogs (such as IU1-47), which exploit structure–activity relationships on a benzene–dimethyl-pyrrole scaffold with tailored electron-withdrawing substitutions and lipophilic enhancements designed to improve potency, selectivity, and bioavailability (Lim et al., 2024; Boselli et al., 2017; Qin et al., 2024). These compounds are cell permeable and have been employed as chemical probes in multiple preclinical settings to modulate proteostasis via targeted inhibition of USP14. In essence, IU1 reflects a series of rationally designed molecules aimed at shifting the balance of proteasome-mediated degradation by preventing USP14’s “trimming” activity, thereby effectively accelerating the clearance of misfolded and aggregation-prone proteins.

Therapeutic History:  
The therapeutic history of USP14 inhibitors like IU1 is primarily rooted in preclinical research addressing proteostasis dysfunction in models of aging and neurodegeneration rather than in clinical or veterinary practice. In Drosophila models of proteostasis-associated aging, IU1 treatment was shown to reduce the accumulation of polyubiquitinated aggregates, increase autophagy markers such as LC3, improve behavioral phenotypes, and even extend lifespan, with these benefits abrogated when autophagy was experimentally disrupted (Lim et al., 2024). In mammalian cell proteotoxicity models—using stressors such as canavanine plus azetidine—IU1 has similarly been reported to reduce ubiquitin conjugate accumulation and enhance autophagic responses (Lim et al., 2024). Moreover, in primary neuronal cultures, more potent analogs like IU1-47 have been used to promote degradation of pathologic proteins such as tau, thereby supporting the concept that USP14 inhibition enhances proteasome-assisted clearance of aggregation-prone substrates (Boselli et al., 2017; Kiprowska, 2016). Despite these promising preclinical data in models of neurodegeneration and systemic aging, there is no direct evidence to date that IU1 or its analogs have been applied to treat dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) or geographic atrophy specifically. However, given that several key observations in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells—such as evidence of impaired ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS) and autophagy cross-talk, aggregate accumulation, and lysosomal dysfunction—are central to AMD pathology (Blasiak et al., 2019; Kaarniranta et al., 2023; Shang & Taylor, 2012), repurposing IU1 to restore proteostasis in RPE represents an innovative extension of its preclinical use.

Mechanism of Action:  
The known mechanism of IU1 centers on its inhibition of USP14, a deubiquitinating enzyme that transiently associates with the proteasome. USP14 normally functions by “trimming” ubiquitin chains on substrates as they enter the proteasome, an activity that can delay substrate degradation and allow for substrate rescue under stress conditions. IU1 and its optimized analogs (e.g., IU1-47) act through allosteric mechanisms—not by directly competing at the catalytic site but by binding a pocket distal (approximately 8.3 Å away) from the catalytic center, thereby perturbing USP14’s structural conformation and catalytic efficiency (Qin et al., 2024; Lim et al., 2024). Inhibition of USP14 by IU1 leads to a reduced removal of ubiquitin chains, effectively “locking in” the ubiquitin tag on misfolded and aggregation-prone proteins and facilitating their recognition and degradation by the 26S proteasome. Beyond its direct impact on proteasomal degradation, USP14 inhibition has been reported to modulate autophagy—evident from increased LC3 lipidation and autophagic flux in certain cellular contexts (Lim et al., 2024; Kim, 2020). Some studies suggest that USP14 may also indirectly influence autophagy by regulating the turnover of factors such as UVRAG, a key autophagy regulator, although these connections remain to be fully elucidated. Notably, while IU1 has been shown to accelerate aggregate clearance in neuronal and Drosophila models, a series of studies have also flagged potential off-target effects at higher concentrations, including inhibition of mitochondrial Complex I leading to ATP depletion and subsequent activation of calpain-mediated proteolysis (Kiprowska, 2016; Kiprowska et al., n.d.). Hence, the molecular interactions of IU1 are complex and highly context-dependent, with the potential to both enhance proteasomal degradation and, at non-optimized doses, induce metabolic perturbations.

Expected Effect:  
In the context of dry age-related macular degeneration, particularly geographic atrophy, the central hypothesis is that IU1 will inhibit USP14 in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells, thereby accelerating the proteasome-assisted clearance of misfolded, aggregation-prone proteins. RPE cells in AMD display defects in both the UPS and the autophagy/lysosomal systems, with studies documenting decreased proteasome activity, accumulation of ubiquitinated aggregates, elevated levels of the selective autophagy adaptor SQSTM1/p62, and lysosomal dysfunction including impaired acidification mediated by vacuolar ATPase (v-ATPase) (Blasiak et al., 2019; Kaarniranta et al., 2023; Shang & Taylor, 2012; Viiri, 2019). By inhibiting USP14, IU1 is expected to shift the proteostasis balance in RPE cells towards enhanced degradation of misfolded proteins via the proteasome. This would not only reduce the intracellular aggregate burden but also alleviate the overloading of the lysosomal pathway—in turn potentially restoring lysosomal functions such as proper acidification by v-ATPase activity. Improved lysosomal acidification is known to facilitate the maturation and activity of cathepsin D, a key lysosomal protease responsible for degrading various protein aggregates and damaged organelles (Kaarniranta et al., 2023). Moreover, preclinical findings in neuronal cells have demonstrated that USP14 inhibition reduces aggregate load (e.g., tau) and promotes autophagic flux (Boselli et al., 2017; Lim et al., 2024), lending indirect support to the concept that these effects might be recapitulated in RPE cells. Although no direct data exist for IU1 in RPE, the mechanistic parallels between proteostasis impairment in neurons and RPE—as documented in extensive AMD literature—suggest that IU1 could reduce aggregate accumulation and oxidative stress in RPE, thereby slowing the progression of geographic atrophy.

Overall Evaluation:  
The overall evaluation of USP14 inhibitor IU1 as a therapeutic candidate for dry age-related macular degeneration is cautiously optimistic yet highlights several critical areas for further investigation. On the positive side, IU1 represents an innovative strategy targeting a central node in protein quality control—USP14—that regulates the degradation of misfolded, aggregation-prone proteins via the proteasome and modulates autophagic pathways. Preclinical evidence in Drosophila models and neuronal cultures demonstrates that pharmacological USP14 inhibition can enhance aggregate clearance, improve proteostasis, and even extend lifespan and motor function under proteotoxic stress conditions (Lim et al., 2024; Boselli et al., 2017). These findings support the concept that modulating the UPS–autophagy interplay may be beneficial in diseases where proteostasis failure is a key driver, such as dry AMD, in which RPE cells experience aggregate build-up, defective autophagy, and lysosomal dysfunction (Blasiak et al., 2019; Kaarniranta et al., 2023; Shang & Taylor, 2012).

Furthermore, the biochemical mechanism by which IU1 operates—namely, reducing the deubiquitinating activity of USP14 to promote ubiquitin-tag retention and substrate commitment to degradation—provides a theoretically attractive approach to simultaneously reduce the proteasomal backlog and relieve lysosomal burden. The potential secondary effects on autophagy, including improved LC3-II turnover and possibly upregulated clearance of aggregates through aggrephagy, add further appeal to the candidate as a multi-modal modulator of proteostasis. The cell-permeable, small-molecule nature of IU1, along with available more potent analogs (such as IU1-47), offers promising opportunities for medicinal chemistry optimization and dosage refinement, especially important given the need for safe application in delicate ocular tissues.

However, several weaknesses and challenges must be addressed before IU1 can be considered a viable therapeutic candidate for dry AMD. A significant concern originates from studies in neuronal systems that indicate off-target effects at higher concentrations—specifically, inhibition of mitochondrial Complex I, ATP depletion, and activation of calpain-mediated proteolysis (Kiprowska, 2016; Kiprowska et al., n.d.). These potential toxicities raise the possibility that a narrow therapeutic window may exist, necessitating thorough dose-response studies in RPE cell models to ensure efficacy without compromising cell viability. Moreover, no direct preclinical studies have yet evaluated IU1 in RPE cells or in animal models of geographic atrophy. As such, critical parameters—such as its ability to modulate v-ATPase function, maintain lysosomal acidification, boost cathepsin D activity, and ultimately clear RPE-specific aggregates such as lipofuscin or oxidized photoreceptor debris—remain to be validated (Kaarniranta et al., 2023; Shang & Taylor, 2012).

Additionally, while the mechanistic data from neurodegenerative models are compelling, the biochemical environment of RPE cells differs from that of neurons in several key respects. RPE cells are highly phagocytic, exposed to constant oxidative stress, and rely on a finely tuned balance between proteasomal and lysosomal pathways to manage the constant influx of photoreceptor outer segment material (Blasiak et al., 2019; Viiri, 2019). Thus, any intervention that modulates proteostasis must be carefully tailored to avoid unintended disruptions in these homeostatic processes. The current literature also underscores the complexity of deubiquitinase biology; while USP14 inhibition appears beneficial in some contexts, the intricate interplay with other DUBs and the broader UPS could lead to compensatory mechanisms that diminish the anticipated therapeutic effect or even trigger adverse consequences (Faber & Roepman, 2019; Kim, 2020).

In summary, USP14 inhibitor IU1 offers an innovative and potentially transformative approach to restoring proteostasis in RPE cells by enhancing proteasomal degradation and autophagic clearance of misfolded proteins—a strategy that directly addresses the pathobiology of dry AMD and geographic atrophy as defined by aggregate accumulation and lysosomal dysfunction (Lim et al., 2024; Blasiak et al., 2019; Kaarniranta et al., 2023). The strengths of this candidate include its robust preclinical data in non-RPE systems, its well-characterized mechanism of action, and its amenability to further chemical optimization. However, significant weaknesses, including potential off-target mitochondrial toxicity, an unestablished therapeutic window in RPE cells, and the lack of direct preclinical studies in AMD models, necessitate cautious and rigorous further investigation. To move forward, it will be essential to conduct targeted RPE-specific in vitro assays and in vivo studies—assessing proteasome activity, autophagy flux, lysosomal acidification (v-ATPase function), cathepsin D activity, and aggregate clearance—while carefully monitoring for cytotoxicity. Only with such detailed preclinical validation can the promise of IU1 as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD be fully assessed (Lim et al., 2024; Blasiak et al., 2019; Kiprowska, 2016; Shang & Taylor, 2012).
