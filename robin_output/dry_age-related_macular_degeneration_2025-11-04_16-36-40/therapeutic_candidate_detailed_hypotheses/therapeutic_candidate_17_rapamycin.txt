Proposal for Rapamycin (Sigma R0395)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Rapamycin (sirolimus), commercially available as Sigma R0395, is a macrocyclic lactone originally isolated from the bacterium Streptomyces hygroscopicus, a discovery that dates back to studies on soil samples from Easter Island. As a naturally derived compound now synthesized and purified for clinical use, rapamycin belongs to the macrolide class and is best known for its potent immunosuppressive properties, having been used extensively to prevent organ rejection in transplantation. Over time, its application expanded beyond transplantation to include areas such as oncology, neurodegeneration, and age-related diseases due to its ability to inhibit the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1). The rationale for repurposing rapamycin in the treatment of dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD), specifically geographic atrophy (GA), stems from its established pharmacological profile, extensive safety record, and well-characterized biochemical functions. Its clinical approval and long-term safety data in other contexts provide a solid foundation for exploring its potential in ocular indications, particularly given its capacity to modulate autophagy in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells (Wang et al., 2022).

Therapeutic History:  
Rapamycin’s development began with its use as an immunosuppressant in organ transplantation, and over the ensuing decades, its application has broadened considerably. Biochemical and preclinical studies have demonstrated that rapamycin is an effective inhibitor of mTORC1, a critical regulator not only of cell proliferation but also of autophagy and lysosomal biogenesis. Early investigations revealed that rapamycin could induce autophagy in various cell types, including RPE cells, by inhibiting mTORC1, thereby facilitating the clearance of toxic aggregates and damaged organelles. This mechanism has been explored extensively for neurodegenerative conditions, and more recently, there has been focused investigation into its utility for dry AMD. Clinical trials have tested rapamycin in forms ranging from intravitreal to subconjunctival injections in patients with GA; for example, a clinical trial by the National Eye Institute aimed to evaluate intravitreal injections of sirolimus for GA, although this study was terminated due to safety concerns (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2012). Similarly, other studies using subconjunctival injections demonstrated that while rapamycin appears safe and well-tolerated, significant benefits in terms of preventing GA progression have not been conclusively demonstrated (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2008, 2011). In animal studies and in vitro RPE models, rapamycin enhanced autophagic flux, increased the activity of cathepsin D (CTSD), and reduced the accumulation of toxic material such as A2E—an effect that is consistent with a potential for slowing GA progression (Wang et al., 2022; Falcão et al., 2024). Thus, rapamycin’s therapeutic history encompasses a combination of robust preclinical evidence supporting its mechanistic action and mixed clinical outcomes in ocular trials, setting the stage for further investigation into optimal dosing, delivery methods, and patient selection criteria (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2008, 2011).

Mechanism of Action:  
Rapamycin exerts its biological effects primarily through direct inhibition of mTORC1, a serine/threonine kinase complex that integrates signals from nutrients, growth factors, and cellular energy status to regulate cell growth, proliferation, and autophagy. In the absence of rapamycin, active mTORC1 phosphorylates downstream targets such as S6 kinase and 4E-BP1, thereby promoting anabolic processes and suppressing autophagy. When rapamycin binds to its intracellular receptor, FKBP12, the resulting complex interacts with the FKBP12-rapamycin binding (FRB) domain of mTORC1, leading to its inhibition. This inhibition results in dephosphorylation and subsequent activation of transcription factor EB (TFEB), a master regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy-related genes. Activated TFEB then translocates to the nucleus, where it enhances the expression of genes involved in lysosomal function, acidification, and the production of lysosomal enzymes such as cathepsin D (CTSD) (Falcão et al., 2025; Falcão et al., 2024; Sinha et al., 2016). In the context of dry AMD, the proposed mechanism hinges on rapamycin’s ability to restore cellular homeostasis in RPE cells by increasing autophagic flux and lysosomal clearance of accumulated drusen components, which include toxic aggregates like A2E. Preclinical studies have shown that treatment of RPE cells with rapamycin results in a marked reduction in the accumulation of autofluorescent granules (AFGs) that are indicative of impaired lysosomal degradation (Falcão et al., 2024; Falcão et al., 2025). The cascade initiated by rapamycin—mTORC1 inhibition, TFEB activation, and enhanced expression of lysosome-associated proteins such as LAMP1 and pro-cathepsin D—forms the crux of its proposed therapeutic action in mitigating the progression of GA by promoting the dismantling and clearance of pathological deposits (Falcão et al., 2024).

Expected Effect:  
In RPE cells, the inhibition of mTORC1 by rapamycin is expected to release the inhibitory phosphorylation on TFEB, enabling its nuclear translocation and activation of a transcriptional program that drives lysosomal biogenesis, acidification, and the production of key lysosomal enzymes such as cathepsin D. This molecular shift is anticipated to markedly enhance the autophagic capacity of the RPE, leading to increased degradation of drusen components, including lipofuscin and A2E, which are hallmarks of dry AMD pathology. The activation of autophagy not only facilitates the clearance of accumulated toxic material but also mitigates oxidative stress by removing damaged mitochondria and other organelles that may contribute to reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation (Wang et al., 2022; Falcão et al., 2024). Moreover, preclinical evidence indicates that, in ARPE-19 cell models, rapamycin improves cellular viability in the face of oxidative injury and reduces markers of cellular senescence by enhancing autophagic flux. These cellular effects are directly linked to the expression of critical proteins in RPE cells; for example, TFEB is known to be expressed in these cells and its activation correlates with increased lysosomal gene expression and function (Falcão et al., 2024) while cathepsin D, a lysosomal protease, is vital for the degradation of phagocytosed photoreceptor outer segments (Chang et al., 2022; Sinha et al., 2016). The intended therapeutic outcome is to slow or potentially halt the progression of GA by reducing the burden of drusen and preserving RPE function, which could translate into better preservation of visual acuity over time. However, it is important to note that while these mechanisms are promising in vitro, clinical translation has encountered challenges related to dosing, delivery, and the stage of disease at which intervention occurs (Wang et al., 2022; Falcão et al., 2024).

Overall Evaluation:  
Rapamycin presents itself as a scientifically promising yet clinically challenging candidate for the treatment of dry AMD. One of the key strengths of rapamycin is its well-established mechanism of action: as a potent mTORC1 inhibitor, it leverages a well-characterized pathway involving TFEB activation that directly targets the core pathology observed in dry AMD—the accumulation of drusen components and impaired lysosomal function in RPE cells. Preclinical studies have consistently demonstrated that rapamycin can enhance autophagic flux, lower levels of toxic aggregates such as A2E, and increase the activity of lysosomal enzymes like cathepsin D, thereby supporting its target validation in the context of RPE health (Falcão et al., 2025; Falcão et al., 2024). Additionally, rapamycin has an extensive history of clinical use in other indications, and its safety profile, while not without concerns, is well documented from its use as an immunosuppressant in transplantation and in various oncology settings (Wang et al., 2022).

However, the clinical translation of rapamycin for dry AMD has not been straightforward. Multiple clinical trials have assessed its application using different routes of administration, such as intravitreal and subconjunctival injections, with mixed results. While safety and tolerability have generally been acceptable, the efficacy endpoints—specifically, the prevention or slowing of GA progression—have not been definitively met in these studies (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2008, 2011, 2012; Wang et al., 2022). Furthermore, some trials encountered complications such as sterile endophthalmitis or observed decreases in visual acuity relative to untreated eyes, suggesting that even if the underlying molecular mechanisms are engaged, the overall benefits in late-stage disease might be limited (Wang et al., 2022; Falcão et al., 2024).

Another potential weakness is the complexity inherent in mTOR signaling itself. The pathway plays a dual role in cellular homeostasis—while its inhibition can promote autophagy and lysosomal clearance, inappropriate or excessive inhibition could lead to adverse effects such as increased retinal neuron apoptosis, particularly in advanced stages of AMD where the cellular milieu is significantly altered (Wang et al., 2022; Chang et al., 2022). Moreover, the optimal dosing regimen and delivery method for achieving the desired ocular concentration without incurring systemic side effects remain unresolved. Some studies have suggested that modifications to the formulation, such as encapsulation in synthetic nanoparticles, could enhance ocular penetration and prolong the effective duration of rapamycin within the eye, thereby minimizing side effects and improving efficacy (Jo et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022).

Despite these challenges, the strength of rapamycin’s mechanism—the inhibition of mTORC1 and consequent activation of TFEB leading to enhanced lysosomal biogenesis—remains compelling. The molecular rationale is robust, and the repeated demonstration of reduced levels of autofluorescent granules in RPE cells following rapamycin treatment is particularly encouraging (Falcão et al., 2024). The concept of using a repurposed, clinically approved drug to address a significant unmet need in dry AMD is attractive, especially given the limited treatment options currently available for this degenerative condition. In this light, further investigations—ideally, in earlier stages of AMD and with innovative delivery modalities—may reveal a niche where rapamycin can exert a clinically meaningful benefit (Wang et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2019).

In conclusion, while rapamycin has significant potential grounded in sound molecular pharmacology and extensive preclinical validation, its clinical application for dry AMD warrants careful optimization. The candidate’s strengths lie in its potent and well-understood mechanism of mTORC1 inhibition, the activation of TFEB, and subsequent improvements in lysosomal function and autophagic clearance in RPE cells. Conversely, clinical challenges including mixed efficacy outcomes in GA trials, potential adverse effects related to mTOR pathway modulation in vulnerable retinal neurons, and limitations in current dosing/delivery strategies represent hurdles that must be surmounted. Based on these comprehensive literature insights, rapamycin remains a promising candidate for further investigation, albeit with recommendations to explore its use in earlier disease stages and in combination with advanced ocular delivery systems to maximize its therapeutic benefit while minimizing risks (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2012; Wang et al., 2022; Falcão et al., 2024; Falcão et al., 2025).
