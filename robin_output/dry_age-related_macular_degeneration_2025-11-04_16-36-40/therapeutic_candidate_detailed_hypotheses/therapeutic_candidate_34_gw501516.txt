Proposal for GW501516 (Sigma G3536)

Below is our detailed evaluation of GW501516 as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD).

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
GW501516 is a fully synthetic small‐molecule agonist of peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor delta (PPARδ, also known as PPARβ/δ) developed during intensive medicinal–chemistry programs for metabolic diseases. Its synthesis and optimization were driven by the need to generate a molecule with high binding affinity for the spacious ligand–binding pocket of PPARδ, a receptor known for its promiscuity with respect to ligand chemical structure (Acharya, 2017). GW501516 belongs to a class of synthetic PPARδ agonists that emerged alongside compounds such as GW0742, with these molecules originally explored for their ability to improve lipid metabolism, increase fatty acid β–oxidation, and modulate inflammatory responses in tissues such as skeletal muscle, heart, and vascular endothelium (Acharya, 2017; Choudhary et al., 2016). As a representative of this chemical class—and marketed historically under the Sigma catalog number G3536—the compound has been a subject of numerous preclinical studies and has been tested in rodent models to assess its metabolic and anti–inflammatory effects (Acharya, 2017). Its synthetic origin and structural design allow it to bind selectively to PPARδ in order to elicit transcriptional responses that adjust cellular energy metabolism, including the regulation of fatty acid oxidation and mitochondrial biogenesis. Given that the PPAR family acts as obligate heterodimers with the retinoid X receptor (RXR) to regulate gene expression through peroxisome proliferator response elements (PPREs), GW501516’s design confers it the ability to influence transcriptional networks important not only in metabolic tissues, but potentially also in ocular tissues such as the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) (Acharya, 2017; Choudhary et al., 2016).

Therapeutic History:  
Originally, GW501516 was investigated extensively as a therapeutic candidate for metabolic disorders, such as dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, and atherosclerosis. Preclinical studies demonstrated that it could increase serum high–density lipoprotein (HDL) levels, reduce adiposity, and even improve glucose tolerance in rodent models (Acharya, 2017; Choudhary et al., 2016). The compound advanced into phase 2 clinical trials for metabolic indications; despite promising metabolic effects in animal studies, it has not been approved due mainly to safety concerns—in particular, rodent carcinogenicity that emerged in long–term studies (Acharya, 2017; Choudhary et al., 2016). While the focus of its initial development was in the realm of systemic metabolic disease, later investigations have expanded the scope of PPARδ agonists to other pathology areas. For instance, PPARδ has been found to regulate angiogenic cell behaviors, and its modulation has been implicated in experimental models of retinal neovascularization and oxygen–induced retinopathy (Choudhary et al., 2016; Dou & Duerfeldt, 2020). In ocular contexts, research by Choudhary et al. has shown that ligand activation of PPARδ can reduce intracellular lipid accumulation in RPE cells and that genetic ablation of PPARδ in mice exacerbates early dry AMD–like phenotypes while reducing choroidal neovascularization (Choudhary et al., 2016). Furthermore, selective PPARδ modulators including GW501516 have been listed among candidates in reviews discussing lipid–activated nuclear receptors and their potential role in modulating microglia and extracellular matrix turnover in AMD (Choudhary et al., 2016; Choudhary & Malek, 2016). Although no prior clinical studies have directly evaluated GW501516 for dry AMD or related RPE disorders, its history in metabolic research and its identification in ocular-focused preclinical studies support its consideration as a repurposed agent. Notably, it must be borne in mind that while metabolic endpoints were favorable in early studies, systemic administration in humans was halted because of potential carcinogenic events in rodent models, suggesting that if GW501516 is to be advanced for ocular use, localized delivery may be required to mitigate such risks (Acharya, 2017; Choudhary et al., 2016).

Mechanism of Action:  
Mechanistically, GW501516 functions by binding to the ligand–binding domain of PPARδ, thereby inducing a conformational change that releases associated corepressors and enables the recruitment of transcriptional coactivators such as PPARγ coactivator 1 alpha (PGC-1α). This heterodimeric complex, formed with RXR, then binds to specific peroxisome proliferator response elements (PPREs) in target gene promoters to modulate gene expression (Acharya, 2017). In metabolic tissues, this cascade leads to the upregulation of genes implicated in fatty acid β–oxidation (e.g., pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase isozyme 4 (PDK4)) and genes involved in the regulation of lipid handling such as angiopoietin-like 4 (ANGPTL4) (Choudhary et al., 2016). In the context of retinal pigment epithelium, the hypothesis is that GW501516-induced activation of PPARδ would similarly upregulate genes involved in fatty acid oxidation. Such metabolic reprogramming is thought to reduce intracellular lipid accumulation and may simultaneously promote enhancement of mitochondrial function through PGC-1α–dependent pathways. Not only does PGC-1α drive mitochondrial biogenesis, but it also interacts with other transcription factor networks such as the transcription factor EB (TFEB), a master regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and function (Ji et al., 2021). In theory, the activation of these pathways would lead to enhanced autophagic flux and lysosomal acidification, thereby upregulating cathepsin D activity—the lysosomal protease crucial for metabolizing complex lipid species. Such a biochemical cascade would be particularly beneficial in the setting of dry AMD, where drusen formation is in part the result of impaired lipid clearance in the RPE (Choudhary et al., 2016). However, a note of caution comes from studies in rodent retinal degeneration models. Organotypic retinal explants from rd1 mice showed that while PPARα and PPARγ agonists reduced photoreceptor cell death, activation of PPARδ using GW501516 (often abbreviated as GW516 in these studies) failed to protect and even worsened cell viability in wild–type explants, indicating that the tissue–specific role of PPARδ in the retina may be more complex than that seen in metabolic tissues (Wang et al., 2025). Nonetheless, the proposed mechanism in RPE focuses on reducing drusen lipid burden by promoting mitochondrial β–oxidation and improving lysosomal degradation via enhanced TFEB and cathepsin D activity—a pathway that has analogies in other cell types and disease models (Choudhary et al., 2016).

Expected Effect:  
Based on the hypothesis for using GW501516 in dry AMD, its anticipated effects in RPE cells would be multifold. First, activation of PPARδ by GW501516 is expected to increase the transcription of genes involved in fatty acid oxidation, essentially reprogramming the metabolic machinery of RPE cells to enhance the catabolism of long–chain fatty acids. Such a shift would theoretically lead to a decrease in intracellular lipid accumulation, which is a key pathological hallmark observed in dry AMD as drusen and sub–RPE deposits (Choudhary et al., 2016). Second, by triggering the release and/or upregulation of coactivators like PGC-1α, GW501516 would facilitate not only mitochondrial biogenesis and function but also might potentiate the activity of TFEB—a central regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy. The cooperative action of PGC-1α and TFEB is believed to enhance lysosomal acidification and increase the activity of cathepsin D, thereby accelerating the clearance of aggregated lipids and proteinaceous debris within the RPE (Ji et al., 2021; Choudhary et al., 2016). Third, improved lysosomal and mitochondrial function would ultimately support the homeostatic balance within RPE cells, potentially slowing or reversing the degenerative process characteristic of dry AMD. Importantly, expression data indicate that PPARδ and its coactivators are indeed present in AMD-vulnerable cells—including primary RPE cells and choroidal endothelial cells—suggesting that the molecular targets of GW501516 are accessible in the intended tissue (Choudhary et al., 2016). On the other hand, preclinical animal models of retinal degeneration (such as the rd1 explant studies) have shown that PPARδ activation with GW501516 did not confer protection to photoreceptors and was even toxic at certain concentrations (Wang et al., 2025); however, these models focused primarily on photoreceptor survival in retinal degeneration rather than lipid clearance in RPE. Therefore, while the expected effect in the RPE is an enhancement of fatty acid oxidation and lysosomal processing leading to reduced drusen formation, the exact dose and delivery modality will be critical, particularly to avoid adverse effects observed in other retinal cell types and to bypass the systemic carcinogenicity that halted previous clinical development (Choudhary et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2025).

Overall Evaluation:  
In summary, GW501516 presents both promising and challenging attributes as a potential repurposed agent for dry AMD. One of its chief strengths is its well-defined mechanism in metabolic regulation: as a highly selective PPARδ agonist, it robustly stimulates pathways leading to increased fatty acid oxidation, mitochondrial biogenesis, and—in theory—the enhancement of lysosomal function via cross-talk with PGC-1α and TFEB (Acharya, 2017; Choudhary et al., 2016; Ji et al., 2021). In the paradigm of dry AMD, where defective lipid metabolism and impaired autophagy contribute to drusen formation and RPE dysfunction, the metabolic–lysosomal axis targeted by GW501516 is particularly attractive (Choudhary et al., 2016). Moreover, the underlying rationale is supported by studies showing that ligand activation of PPARδ in RPE cells can diminish lipid accumulation—a finding directly relevant to reducing the drusen burden associated with dry AMD (Choudhary et al., 2016).  

However, notable weaknesses temper the enthusiasm for GW501516. Historically, its systemic administration in metabolic disease trials was halted due to carcinogenicity observed in rodents, raising significant safety concerns that would have to be mitigated by strategies such as localized ocular delivery (Acharya, 2017; Choudhary et al., 2016). The risk of adverse systemic effects could be minimized by intravitreal injection or by employing sustained-release formulations such as ocular hydrogels or nanocarriers, but such approaches require extensive preclinical safety validation. Another critical point is that the beneficial effects of PPARδ activation in ocular cells have not been uniformly observed. For instance, in rd1 organotypic retinal explant studies—which, while focused on photoreceptor degeneration rather than RPE lipid clearance—PPARδ agonism with GW501516 did not reduce photoreceptor death and even increased toxicity in wild-type retinal tissue (Choudhary et al., 2016; Dou & Duerfeldt, 2020). Additionally, while the hypothesis posits that GW501516 will operate through a metabolic–lysosomal mechanism involving PGC-1α and TFEB to enhance lysosomal acidification and cathepsin D activity, direct evidence in RPE models linking these endpoints is sparse. Much of the molecular framework comes from studies in muscle, neuronal, and systemic metabolic contexts rather than from robust ocular models (Ji et al., 2021; Choudhary et al., 2016).  

Furthermore, it remains to be rigorously established whether PPARδ activation in the RPE would indeed result in a meaningful reduction in drusen lipid burden in dry AMD patients. Preclinical studies have indicated that genetic knockouts of PPARδ can lead to early dry AMD–like pathology in mice (Choudhary et al., 2016), supporting the idea that proper receptor function is necessary for lipid homeostasis; yet pharmacologic activation may not recapitulate the complete spectrum of protective effects observed with endogenous receptor function. Moreover, the carcinogenicity concerns associated with GW501516 underscore a key translational hurdle. Even if localized delivery can theoretically reduce systemic exposure, the long-term risk of oncogenic transformation in ocular cells must be assessed with extreme caution given the potential for even low-level systemic distribution or local proliferative effects that could increase melanocytic or retinal tumor risk (Acharya, 2017; Choudhary et al., 2016). Lastly, efficacy in a multifactorial disease such as dry AMD is unlikely to be achieved with a single-target approach. While modulating the metabolic–lysosomal axis is compelling, dry AMD is characterized by inflammation, oxidative stress, and extracellular matrix remodeling as well. Thus, combining GW501516 with other agents that target complementary pathways might offer greater clinical benefit than monotherapy, albeit with additional complexity in evaluating drug–drug interactions and compounded safety risks (Choudhary et al., 2016).

Overall, while GW501516 shows promise in theory by linking PPARδ activation to enhanced fatty acid oxidation and lysosomal capacity—a mechanistic pathway that could, if properly harnessed, ameliorate the drusen lipid burden in the RPE—the existing literature suggests several areas of concern. These include its historical carcinogenicity in long-term systemic studies, mixed and sometimes adverse effects in retinal explant models, and a relative paucity of direct evidence in RPE cells regarding TFEB and cathepsin D upregulation. Before advancing GW501516 as a candidate for dry AMD, further targeted studies are necessary. Such studies would need to assess the molecular effects of localized ocular delivery on lysosomal biogenesis and acidification in human RPE cells, confirm proper engagement of the PGC-1α/TFEB axis, and determine whether any measurable reduction in drusen formation can be achieved without deleterious off-target effects in the retina. In parallel, robust toxicology studies using localized dosing schemes must be performed to address the historical safety concerns. In conclusion, while the metabolic–lysosomal hypothesis for treating dry AMD is scientifically attractive and GW501516 embodies some key attributes that support this approach, its previous toxicity profile and mixed preclinical ocular findings advise caution and the need for significant additional research (Acharya, 2017; Choudhary et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2025).

Taken together, our comprehensive literature review indicates that GW501516 has a sound mechanistic rationale—through activation of PPARδ to enhance fatty acid oxidation and possibly upregulate autophagy–lysosomal functions via cooperation with PGC-1α and TFEB. However, its therapeutic history is marred by safety concerns due to rodent carcinogenicity, and ocular preclinical models have shown variable results that necessitate careful dose optimization and targeted delivery. Ultimately, GW501516 may have potential for treating dry AMD if its adverse systemic effects can be mitigated by localized ocular formulations and if future studies confirm that activation of the metabolic–lysosomal axis within RPE cells translates into meaningful clinical benefit. Further research should focus on detailed in vitro assays in human RPE cells, animal models using localized delivery to the retina, and comprehensive toxicology studies in ocular tissues before committing to clinical trials.
