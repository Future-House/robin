Proposal for Vorinostat (SAHA; Selleckchem S1047)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Vorinostat (suberoylanilide hydroxamic acid, SAHA; Selleckchem S1047) is a synthetic small molecule originally discovered through a screening process aimed at identifying inhibitors of histone deacetylases (HDACs) and belongs to the hydroxamic acid class of compounds. It was developed as an anticancer agent and later received U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval for the treatment of cutaneous T cell lymphoma; its chemical synthesis involves derivatization of an anilide moiety with a hydroxamic acid function, enabling a high degree of specificity for the zinc-dependent catalytic pockets of HDAC enzymes. As a pan-HDAC inhibitor, Vorinostat is designed to modulate epigenetic regulation by binding to the active site of multiple HDAC isoforms, thereby increasing histone acetylation and altering the transcriptional profile of target genes. This class of compounds has been broadly used in oncology, where the modulation of chromatin structure reactivates suppressed tumor suppressor genes and triggers cell cycle arrest and apoptosis. In the context of drug repurposing, the rationale for exploring Vorinostat in dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is centered on its potential to epigenetically reprogram retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells by enhancing the acetylation status and activation of key transcription factors such as TFEB and FOXO, which are critical regulators of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy—a mechanism distinctly different from kinase inhibition strategies (Buniello et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025).

Therapeutic History:  
Vorinostat’s clinical journey has predominantly revolved around its anticancer applications. Early-phase clinical trials in various solid tumors, including breast, colorectal, and non-small cell lung cancer, as well as studies in metastatic thyroid cancer, relapsed or refractory leukemia, and multiple myeloma, have provided a robust safety profile and dosing regimen for this therapeutic candidate (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2004, 2005, 2006; Ghosh et al., 2024). Although its use in oncology has been extensive—with studies demonstrating its ability to block cell growth by inhibiting key enzymes required for proliferation—the direct evidence for its application in ocular diseases, especially in dry AMD, remains limited. Nonetheless, the broader class of HDAC inhibitors has shown promising results in preclinical ocular models. For instance, several studies have demonstrated that HDAC inhibitors can attenuate choroidal neovascularization, protect retinal tissues from ischemic injury, and promote cellular differentiation in the retinal pigment epithelium (Luo et al., 2025). These findings provide an indirect validation of the concept that epigenetic modulators of HDAC activity may be repurposed to combat the autophagy and lysosomal dysfunction observed in dry AMD. Although Vorinostat itself has not been extensively studied in the context of dry AMD or similar RPE-driven degenerative diseases, its established pharmacological profile and ocular tolerability in other clinical contexts suggest that it could offer mechanistic benefits by modulating retinal epigenetic landscapes (Luo et al., 2025).

Mechanism of Action:  
Vorinostat exerts its biological effects by inhibiting the activity of zinc-dependent histone deacetylases. Under normal physiological conditions, HDACs remove acetyl groups from the lysine residues on histone tails, leading to chromatin condensation and transcriptional repression; by inhibiting these enzymes, Vorinostat results in the accumulation of acetylated histones and a more relaxed chromatin structure that promotes gene transcription. The current hypothesis contends that in RPE cells, Vorinostat-mediated HDAC inhibition will enhance the acetylation of the transcription factors TFEB (transcription factor EB) and members of the FOXO (forkhead box O) family. TFEB is a master regulator of the coordinated lysosomal expression and regulation (CLEAR) network, which governs the transcription of genes encoding lysosomal enzymes, including cathepsin D (CTSD), and other proteins necessary for autophagy and lysosomal acidification (Buniello et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025). Similarly, FOXO transcription factors are implicated in cellular stress responses and can promote the expression of antioxidant enzymes as well as proteins involved in maintaining mitochondrial integrity. In neuronal models, HDAC inhibition has been linked to increased expression, acetylation, and nuclear localization of TFEB, leading to upregulation of lysosomal gene expression and enhanced autophagic flux (Buniello et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025). It is anticipated that Vorinostat will operate through a similar mechanism in RPE cells by facilitating the dissociation of HDACs from the promoters of TFEB/FOXO target genes, thereby promoting their transcriptional activation. This would initiate a cascade of events that enhance lysosomal biogenesis, improve lysosomal acidification, and increase the activity of cathepsin D—the lysosomal protease that plays a critical role in degrading accumulated protein aggregates and damaged cellular organelles, which are pathognomonic of dry AMD pathology (Luo et al., 2025). Moreover, it is relevant that other HDAC inhibitors, such as trichostatin A (TSA) and valproic acid (VPA), have demonstrated the capacity to modulate similar pathways in retinal cells, thereby indirectly supporting the proposed mechanism of action for Vorinostat in restoring lysosomal function and autophagic balance in RPE cells (Luo et al., 2025).

Expected Effect:  
Based on the proposed mechanism of action, the expected effect of Vorinostat in the context of dry AMD is multifaceted. The compound is anticipated to inhibit HDAC activity in RPE cells, leading to an increase in overall protein acetylation, including that of histones and transcription factors. This epigenetic modulation should facilitate the nuclear translocation and activation of TFEB, thereby upregulating the CLEAR network of genes involved in lysosomal biogenesis, acidification, and autophagy. In turn, the increased expression and activity of cathepsin D will improve the degradative capacity of the lysosomes, fostering the clearance of lipofuscin and other deleterious protein aggregates that accumulate with age in RPE cells. Additionally, enhanced FOXO activity could promote an improved antioxidant response and mitigate mitochondrial dysfunction, which together would reduce oxidative stress—a key driver of RPE degeneration in dry AMD (Buniello et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025).

The anticipated outcome in preclinical assays would be a measurable restoration of autophagic flux as evidenced by reductions in markers of cellular stress and accumulation of autophagy substrates. Cellular assays may demonstrate increased lysosomal enzyme activities, such as that of cathepsin D, and improved acidification of lysosomes in treated RPE cells. In animal models engineered or induced to exhibit dry AMD-like pathology, treatment with Vorinostat is expected to lead to an improvement in retinal structure and function, potentially assessed through morphological analyses and electroretinography. Furthermore, the restoration of TFEB/FOXO activity should enhance the overall cellular resilience of RPE, thereby delaying or preventing the progression of degenerative changes associated with dry AMD. Given Vorinostat’s established oral bioavailability and systemic tolerability in oncology patients, there is potential for its repurposing with adjusted dosing regimens aimed at achieving therapeutic concentrations in the retina with minimal systemic toxicity (Buniello et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025).

Overall Evaluation:  
In evaluating Vorinostat as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD, several strengths and weaknesses emerge. One of the principal strengths of Vorinostat is its well-characterized pharmacological profile, which is supported by extensive clinical data in oncology. Its FDA-approved status and known systemic tolerability provide a translational advantage over novel compounds that are still in early developmental stages. The robust mechanism of action of HDAC inhibitors, with demonstrated capacity to modulate gene expression through epigenetic reprogramming, aligns well with the pathobiology of dry AMD, where impaired autophagy and lysosomal dysfunction are central features. The specific hypothesis that Vorinostat will promote the acetylation and activation of TFEB and FOXO transcription factors to drive lysosomal biogenesis, improve acidification, and enhance cathepsin D activity addresses well-validated therapeutic targets in the disease process (Buniello et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025).

However, there are also significant weaknesses and potential challenges that must be carefully considered. First, while the mechanistic rationale is compelling, direct experimental evidence supporting Vorinostat’s efficacy in RPE cells or animal models of dry AMD is currently sparse. Much of the supporting data is extrapolated from studies using other HDAC inhibitors such as TSA, VPA, or AN7, and from cancer studies where the cellular context differs substantially from that of the RPE (Luo et al., 2025). Second, the pharmacokinetic profile and ocular tissue penetration of Vorinostat when administered systemically remain uncertain. The blood–retinal barrier presents a formidable challenge to achieving therapeutic drug concentrations in the retina, and while clinical trials in oncology have established systemic dosing guidelines, it is unclear whether these doses will translate into efficacious levels within the RPE while maintaining safety. Third, the potential for off-target effects and retinal toxicity cannot be overlooked; certain HDAC inhibitors have been associated with deleterious retinal effects when administered at inappropriate doses or over prolonged periods, emphasizing the need for careful dose optimization and possibly localized (e.g., intravitreal) delivery strategies (Luo et al., 2025). Fourth, the complex interplay between various HDAC isoforms in the retina may necessitate a more selective approach than that offered by the broad-spectrum inhibition provided by Vorinostat, as indiscriminate inhibition could disrupt other acetylation-dependent regulatory mechanisms critical for normal retinal function (Luo et al., 2025).

In addition, while the hypothesis posits that Vorinostat will upregulate TFEB and FOXO to enhance autophagy and lysosomal function, the regulation of these transcription factors is multifactorial. For example, TFEB activity is tightly controlled by phosphorylation events mediated by mTORC1 and AKT2 signaling pathways, and the interplay between these kinases, deacetylases such as SIRT5, and HDACs remains an active area of investigation (Abokyi & Tse, 2025; Buniello et al., 2025; Ghosh et al., 2024). Thus, while there is mechanistic plausibility in promoting lysosomal biogenesis via HDAC inhibition, the precise molecular outcomes in the unique environment of aging RPE cells require further delineation. Furthermore, studies in other models have revealed that the beneficial effects of HDAC inhibition on autophagy and lysosomal function may vary depending on the timing, duration, and specific isoform selectivity of the inhibitor used (Luo et al., 2025).

Taking these factors into account, a cautious but optimistic view emerges regarding the repurposing of Vorinostat for dry AMD. The compound’s established clinical track record in oncology is a substantial advantage, providing a foundation for subsequent translational studies. The convergent evidence supporting the role of HDAC inhibition in enhancing autophagy and mitigating oxidative stress in retinal diseases further bolsters the rationale for this approach (Falcão et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025). However, to fully validate the therapeutic potential of Vorinostat in dry AMD, rigorous preclinical studies are needed to (1) confirm its efficacy in restoring TFEB/FOXO activity and lysosomal function in RPE cells, (2) determine its pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties in ocular tissues, (3) assess its safety profile within the retina over chronic dosing schedules, and (4) establish optimized dosing strategies that achieve target engagement without compromising retinal integrity.

Moreover, future investigations might explore alternative delivery mechanisms, such as nanoparticle-based systems or sustained-release intravitreal formulations, to circumvent the challenges posed by the blood–retinal barrier and achieve localized drug concentrations that are both effective and safe. In parallel, combinatorial therapeutic strategies that pair Vorinostat with other agents that modulate complementary pathways—such as antioxidants, SIRT1 activators, or agents that specifically target mTORC1 signaling—could be considered to provide a multifaceted approach to treating dry AMD. These strategies would leverage the ability of Vorinostat to reprogram epigenetic signatures in RPE cells while simultaneously addressing other pathogenic mechanisms contributing to AMD progression (Buniello et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025).

In summary, Vorinostat represents a promising candidate for drug repurposing in dry AMD due to its potent HDAC inhibitory activity, favorable clinical history in oncology, and a plausible mechanism by which it could restore lysosomal biogenesis and autophagic flux in RPE cells—a central defect in dry AMD pathogenesis. The strengths of this approach include the compound’s established approval and known pharmacology, coupled with emerging evidence that epigenetic modulation can address key cellular dysfunctions in retinal degeneration. Conversely, the critical weaknesses pertain to the lack of direct preclinical evidence in the context of AMD, uncertainties regarding effective ocular delivery, and potential risks of off-target effects in a delicate tissue such as the retina. A comprehensive series of translational studies, beginning with in vitro models of RPE dysfunction and progressing to in vivo dry AMD models, is essential to fully establish the therapeutic window, efficacy, and safety profile of Vorinostat for this new indication. If these studies yield positive results, Vorinostat could offer a novel, mechanistically precise approach to the treatment of dry AMD, addressing a significant unmet clinical need by restoring the autophagy–lysosome pathway and ultimately preserving vision in affected patients (Buniello et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025).
