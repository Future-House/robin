Proposal for MK-2206 (Selleckchem S1078)

Below is a detailed evaluation of MK-2206 as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD), structured into five sections.

─────────────────────────────  
Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
─────────────────────────────  
MK-2206 is a synthetically derived, orally bioavailable small‐molecule inhibitor that functions as an allosteric modulator of the serine/threonine kinase AKT. Unlike classical ATP‐competitive agents, MK-2206 binds to an allosteric site outside the catalytic pocket, thereby altering the conformation of AKT and preventing its full activation. Originally discovered and optimized for oncological indications, its development was driven by the need to inhibit aberrant AKT signaling observed in multiple cancers. The compound belongs to a class of drugs known as allosteric AKT inhibitors, which historically have been explored for their ability to modulate critical intracellular signaling pathways that govern cellular proliferation, survival, and metabolism. More specifically, MK-2206 has been demonstrated in vitro to reduce the phosphorylation of AKT at key regulatory residues (for example, S473) in multiple cell types, including primary retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cultures where its application at 1 μM for 24 hours was shown to inhibit AKT phosphorylation and promote nuclear translocation of TFEB (Transcription Factor EB) – the master regulator of lysosomal gene expression (Karim et al., 2020). Although the detailed synthetic route is not fully delineated within our context, the compound has been thoroughly characterized for its bioavailability, metabolic stability, and cell‐penetrant properties during its extensive preclinical and clinical evaluation in oncology (Guo et al., 2021; ClinicalTrials.gov, 2009). These attributes underpin its suitability for repurposing; its well‐defined medicinal chemistry profile and advanced clinical development stage (availability of Phase I data) position MK-2206 as an attractive candidate for investigating autophagy regulation in non‐oncologic contexts such as AMD (Karim et al., 2020).

─────────────────────────────  
Therapeutic History  
─────────────────────────────  
Historically, MK-2206 has been developed and evaluated predominantly as an anticancer agent. During its clinical evolution, it was subjected to multiple Phase I and combination Phase I/II clinical trials in patients with advanced solid tumors, HER2-positive cancers, and other malignancies. The compound has been administered on both an every-other-day (QOD) schedule and on a once-weekly (QW) regimen in dose‐escalation studies (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2008; 2010). These early clinical studies provided valuable safety, tolerability, and pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) data that have now established an acceptable safety profile in humans. Outside the field of oncology, no published clinical study has yet directly evaluated MK-2206 in ocular indications. However, a separate body of preclinical research has increasingly linked dysregulated AKT activation in RPE cells with impaired lysosomal function and defective autophagy – two pathological hallmarks of dry AMD (Karim et al., 2020). In immunoproteasome‐deficient RPE models (for example, LMP2 knockout [KO] cells), hyper‐phosphorylation of AKT is associated with reduced nuclear TFEB and compromised autophagic flux, features that correlate with RPE stress and lipofuscin accumulation (Karim et al., 2020). Preclinical in vitro data have demonstrated that pharmacological inhibition of AKT using MK-2206 leads to decreased AKT phosphorylation, increased nuclear TFEB localization, and enhanced autophagy flux in RPE, rescuing some of the defects observed in these models (Karim et al., 2020). Although MK-2206 per se has not been directly tested in animal models of AMD, related preclinical evidence based on the modulation of the AKT–TFEB axis in RPE supports its potential utility in the AMD context (Karim et al., 2020). In addition, several patent reviews have discussed MK-2206 within the broader portfolio of AKT inhibitors that are being repurposed for indications beyond oncology, further reinforcing the plausibility of using MK-2206 to target cellular pathways implicated in AMD (Guo et al., 2021).

─────────────────────────────  
Mechanism of Action  
─────────────────────────────  
The established mechanism of MK-2206 is anchored in its ability to inhibit AKT signaling through an allosteric mechanism. Under normal conditions, the AKT kinase is activated by phosphorylation events – typically at threonine 308 and serine 473 – in response to upstream signals from receptor tyrosine kinases and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) pathways. AKT then phosphorylates a wide range of substrates that control cell survival, growth, and metabolism. One of the downstream effects of activated AKT is the phosphorylation of TFEB (and possibly other MiT/TFE family members such as TFE3) at specific serine residues (for example, S467). This phosphorylation event hampers TFEB’s capacity to translocate from the cytosol to the nucleus, thereby repressing its transcriptional activity on the CLEAR (Coordinated Lysosomal Expression and Regulation) network, a gene set that drives lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy (Karim et al., 2020).

MK-2206 exerts its effect by binding to an allosteric site on AKT, a mode of inhibition that distinguishes it from ATP‐competitive inhibitors. In doing so, it reduces the phosphorylation of AKT at Ser473, thereby lowering its kinase activity (Karim et al., 2020). In RPE cells – particularly under pathogenic conditions such as immunoproteasome deficiency that leads to AKT hyper‐phosphorylation – MK-2206 administration has been shown to result in a marked decrease in AKT activity. This decrease, in turn, permits the dephosphorylation of TFEB, facilitating its nuclear import (Karim et al., 2020). Once in the nucleus, TFEB upregulates the transcription of genes involved in lysosomal function, autophagosome formation, and the expression of critical enzymes such as cathepsin D and components of the V-ATPase, which are necessary for maintaining lysosomal acidification (Karim et al., 2020).

Although the research cited does not explicitly verify isoform‐specific inhibition of AKT2 by MK-2206, the underlying hypothesis – that AKT2 hyperactivity in RPE suppresses TFEB nuclear localization – provides a strong rationale for repurposing MK-2206 in AMD. Some studies have proposed that AKT2, among the three isoforms, may predominantly drive the inflammatory and autophagy‐related deficits encountered in RPE in AMD models (Ghosh et al., 2019). This hypothesis is further supported by evidence showing that AKT2 inhibition can attenuate inflammatory cascades (such as IFNλ–LCN-2–driven neutrophil infiltration) that contribute to early RPE pathology (Ghosh et al., 2019). Thus, the mechanism of action of MK-2206 – namely, its ability to allosterically inhibit AKT activity leading to increased nuclear translocation of TFEB and subsequent upregulation of lysosomal/autophagy pathways – directly addresses key pathobiological mechanisms implicated in dry AMD.

─────────────────────────────  
Expected Effect  
─────────────────────────────  
Based on the hypothesis under review, the administration of MK-2206 to RPE cells is expected to yield several critical downstream effects that are therapeutically desirable in the context of dry AMD. First, MK-2206 should decrease the hyper‐phosphorylation of AKT observed in stressed or disease‐impacted RPE, which is thought to be a major contributing factor to the suppression of TFEB nuclear localization in these cells (Karim et al., 2020). By relieving this inhibitory phosphorylation, MK-2206 should facilitate the dephosphorylation and subsequent nuclear translocation of TFEB. Once TFEB is allowed to enter the nucleus, it can promote the transcription of a broad array of genes responsible for lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy, including those involved in the expression of vacuolar H-ATPase (V-ATPase) components and cathepsin D (CTSD) (Karim et al., 2020).

The restoration of nuclear TFEB activity is expected to lead to several beneficial cellular effects. In the RPE – which relies on robust autophagy for the clearance of photoreceptor outer segments and maintenance of cellular homeostasis – enhanced TFEB‐driven gene expression should improve lysosomal acidification and enzymatic activity, including that of cathepsin D (a key lysosomal protease) (Karim et al., 2020). Improved lysosomal acidification is critical for the maturation and function of cathepsin D, which in turn is essential for the degradation of protein aggregates and lipofuscin. Since drusen and lipofuscin accumulation represent major pathological hallmarks of dry AMD, the normalization of cathepsin D function could help to clear these toxic deposits, potentially slowing or reversing disease progression (Karim et al., 2020; Ghosh et al., 2019).

Furthermore, other reports suggest that AKT activation in RPE not only hinders autophagy but also promotes pro‐inflammatory pathways that contribute to tissue degeneration (Ghosh et al., 2019). Thus, the inhibition of AKT signaling by MK-2206 is anticipated to diminish such inflammatory cascades (Ghosh et al., 2019). In aggregate, the expected net effect is a re‐establishment of cellular proteostasis within the RPE via two interrelated mechanisms: (1) an enhancement of autophagic flux and lysosomal clearance via the TFEB pathway, and (2) a reduction in deleterious inflammatory and stress responses mediated by aberrant AKT2 signaling. Given that RPE dysfunction, lipofuscin accumulation, and drusen formation underlie the pathogenesis of dry AMD, MK-2206 could thereby serve to counteract disease progression at a mechanistic level (Karim et al., 2020).

─────────────────────────────  
Overall Evaluation  
─────────────────────────────  
In evaluating MK-2206 as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD, several key points emerge. On the strength side, MK-2206 is a well-characterized small-molecule allosteric inhibitor of the AKT pathway – a pathway increasingly implicated in the regulation of autophagy and lysosomal function in RPE cells. Preclinical studies in immunoproteasome-deficient RPE have demonstrated that MK-2206 can inhibit AKT phosphorylation, restore nuclear TFEB localization, and promote autophagic flux (Karim et al., 2020). This mechanistic link is especially relevant, as dry AMD is characterized by reduced autophagy, impaired lysosomal clearance, and resultant accumulation of drusen and lipofuscin. Moreover, the rationale that AKT2 hyperactivity in RPE cells suppresses TFEB function – thereby weakening the cell’s proteolytic capacity – is well supported by the available preclinical data (Ghosh et al., 2019). In addition, MK-2206 has already been through several Phase I oncology trials, meaning that its safety and pharmacokinetic profiles are largely established in humans, which dramatically de-risks its repurposing for a different indication such as AMD (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2008; 2010). This could accelerate the clinical development timeline, as much of the initial toxicity and dosing work has been completed.

Nonetheless, there are several challenges and potential weaknesses to be addressed. First, while the available studies support that MK-2206 can modulate the AKT–TFEB axis in RPE cells under preclinical conditions, many of these studies do not provide isoform‐specific data; our hypothesis hinges on the idea that AKT2 is the critical isoform to be inhibited. MK-2206 is generally described as a pan-AKT inhibitor, which means that it may also affect AKT1 and AKT3. The lack of isoform specificity could lead to unanticipated off-target effects or impact cellular processes in non-RPE tissues if systemic exposure is not tightly controlled (Karim et al., 2020). Second, although the mechanism of promoting nuclear TFEB activity through AKT inhibition is conceptually sound and supported by cellular data, direct measurements of downstream endpoints critical to AMD – such as lysosomal acidification (e.g., via V-ATPase activity assays) and cathepsin D maturation or activity – are limited or indirect in the current literature (Karim et al., 2020). Without robust evidence that MK-2206 improves these specific lysosomal functions in RPE cells, the link between restored autophagy and actual therapeutic benefit in terms of drusen clearance remains inferential. Third, previous clinical development of MK-2206 in oncology has encountered variable efficacy when used as a monotherapy, prompting its evaluation predominantly in combination regimens (Guo et al., 2021; ClinicalTrials.gov, 2009). The implications of these findings for a non‐oncologic, degenerative condition such as AMD are not straightforward, and careful attention must be paid to optimizing both delivery routes (for example, local versus systemic administration) and dosing strategies that are tailored to the ocular microenvironment.

Furthermore, while the literature provides a strong rationale linking AKT hyperactivation to impaired autophagy in RPE, dry AMD is a multifactorial disease with complex pathophysiology that includes oxidative stress, inflammation, and genetic risk factors such as CFH variants. It is not yet fully clear whether correcting the autophagy defect alone will suffice to produce clinically meaningful outcomes in all patients with dry AMD. In addition, toxicity concerns must be carefully revisited: although MK-2206’s safety profile is defined in the context of oncology, potential ocular toxicity – such as RPE cell toxicity, off-target retinal effects, or interference with normal retinal cellular turnover – must be rigorously evaluated via dedicated preclinical ocular studies before clinical translation. Finally, the intellectual property and regulatory landscape for repurposing a cancer drug to treat an ocular disease will require further scrutiny, although the existing clinical data on MK-2206 do provide an important head-start compared to wholly novel compounds (Guo et al., 2021; ClinicalTrials.gov, 2009).

In summary, the strengths of MK-2206 as a candidate for repurposing in dry AMD include:  
• A well-characterized mechanism of action that aligns with pathobiological insights from RPE autophagy studies, namely the inhibition of aberrant AKT signaling leading to enhanced nuclear TFEB activity and autophagy restoration (Karim et al., 2020).  
• Established clinical safety and PK data from multiple Phase I oncology trials, which reduce translational risk and may accelerate repurposing efforts (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2008; 2010).  
• A plausible hypothesis that links AKT2 hyperactivity in RPE (observed in CFH-risk models and immunoproteasome-deficient systems) to lysosomal dysfunction, drusen accumulation, and ultimately, AMD pathology (Ghosh et al., 2019).

Conversely, potential weaknesses include:  
• Limited evidence regarding isoform specificity toward AKT2, as MK-2206 is generally considered a pan-AKT inhibitor, which may inadvertently modulate other AKT isoforms and lead to systemic side effects (Karim et al., 2020).  
• Incomplete preclinical data linking MK-2206 directly to improvements in lysosomal acidification and cathepsin D activity in RPE, which remain key therapeutic endpoints predicted to counteract drusen formation (Karim et al., 2020).  
• The challenge of adapting systemic dosing regimens used in oncology to the ocular setting, where achieving therapeutic retinal concentrations without causing off-target toxicity is paramount (Guo et al., 2021; ClinicalTrials.gov, 2009).  
• The multifactorial nature of dry AMD that may require combination approaches, meaning that modulation of the AKT–TFEB axis alone might not be sufficient to achieve robust clinical benefit in all patients.

Overall, MK-2206 represents a promising candidate to repurpose for dry AMD based on its ability to allosterically inhibit AKT, a central node in a pathway that has been implicated in RPE autophagy and lysosomal dysfunction. The preclinical rationale – that inhibition of AKT releases the block on TFEB nuclear translocation, thereby improving lysosomal acidification and cathepsin D activity – is well founded in the literature (Karim et al., 2020). Moreover, the favorable clinical safety profile gleaned from its extensive evaluation in oncology serves as an important asset when considering repurposing strategies. However, critical questions remain regarding isoform specificity, the optimal route and dosing for ocular application, and whether restoring autophagy via this pathway is sufficient to reverse or halt the progression of dry AMD in patients. Future studies should focus on establishing robust preclinical data in AMD-relevant animal models —including direct measures of lysosomal acidification, cathepsin D function, and drusen clearance —as well as refining ocular delivery strategies that minimize systemic exposure. Addressing these gaps will be essential for de-risking MK-2206’s application in the AMD setting and for paving the way toward clinical trials specifically targeting the AKT–TFEB pathway in the context of retinal degeneration.

─────────────────────────────  
Conclusion  
─────────────────────────────  
In conclusion, our comprehensive literature review supports the repurposing of MK-2206 for dry AMD on the following grounds: it is a well‐studied allosteric AKT inhibitor with a known safety profile from Phase I oncology trials; preclinical investigations in RPE models implicate AKT hyperactivation in the suppression of TFEB nuclear localization and consequent lysosomal dysfunction; and mechanistic hypotheses derived from these models suggest that inhibiting AKT (and, by extension, AKT2) can restore autophagy, improve lysosomal acidification, boost cathepsin D activity, and potentially counteract drusen accumulation – a key driver of dry AMD pathology. While challenges remain, particularly with respect to isoform selectivity and the translation of dosing paradigms from oncology to ocular applications, the existing data provide a strong rationale for advancing MK-2206 into more focused preclinical studies for dry AMD. Such work should include detailed analyses of lysosomal pH, cathepsin D activity, and the overall impact on RPE proteostasis in in vivo models that recapitulate the human disease. With these additional studies, MK-2206 could emerge as a viable candidate in our therapeutic pipeline for addressing the unmet need in dry AMD (Karim et al., 2020; Ghosh et al., 2019; Guo et al., 2021).
