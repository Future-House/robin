Proposal for Eculizumab (Alexion)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Eculizumab (Soliris, Alexion) is a humanized IgG monoclonal antibody derived from the murine anti–human C5 antibody m5G1 via CDR grafting and human framework engineering to reduce immunogenicity and extend serum half-life, designed to bind complement component C5 with high affinity and sterically prevent its cleavage by C5 convertases into C5a and C5b (thereby blocking terminal pathway activation and MAC assembly) (Park et al., 2019; Troutbeck et al., 2012; Yehoshua et al., 2014; Park et al., 2021). It belongs to the class of complement inhibitors that directly target an effector protein (C5) at the convergence of the classical, lectin, and alternative pathways, in contrast to upstream amplification-targeting agents (e.g., C3 or factor D inhibitors) (Park et al., 2019; Tzoumas et al., 2023). Systemically administered eculizumab has established clinical utility in complement-mediated hematologic and renal disorders, including paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria and atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, and more recently generalized myasthenia gravis, reflecting a mature pharmacology and a well-characterized risk profile centered on increased susceptibility to encapsulated organisms (notably meningococcal infection) due to terminal pathway blockade (Park et al., 2019; Yehoshua et al., 2014).

Within ophthalmology, the terminal complement pathway is an attractive target for dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) and geographic atrophy (GA) because C5a is a proinflammatory anaphylatoxin detected in drusen that can drive leukocyte recruitment and VEGF induction in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), and C5b nucleates formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC, C5b–C9), which accumulates with age and AMD in Bruch’s membrane and choriocapillaris and can lyse or sub-lytically activate choroidal endothelial and RPE cells (Kassa et al., 2019). The class of C5 inhibitors therefore aims to reduce both inflammatory signaling (via C5a) and cytolytic/proinflammatory MAC effects while preserving upstream opsonization and some host-defense functions mediated by C3b, potentially retaining aspects of immune competence relative to C3 inhibition (Halawa et al., 2021; Park et al., 2019).

Therapeutic History  
Approved indications and systemic pharmacology  
Eculizumab is FDA/EMA approved for paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria and atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, where weight-based intravenous regimens achieve sustained trough serum concentrations and complete inhibition of hemolytic activity by blocking C5 cleavage; these regimens produce serum levels > 35 μg/mL and complete systemic C5 functional blockade, but require meningococcal vaccination to mitigate Neisseria risk inherent to terminal complement inhibition (Park et al., 2019; Yehoshua et al., 2014). In other complementopathies, clinical experience has highlighted a mechanism-consequent shift of pathology upstream (e.g., C3-loading and extravascular hemolysis in PNH under C5 blockade), underscoring that C5 inhibition leaves C3 activation intact (Hammadi et al., 2023).

Clinical investigation in geographic atrophy of dry AMD  
Eculizumab was investigated in the prospective, randomized, double-masked, single-center Phase 2 COMPLETE study that enrolled 30 participants with GA (primary cohort) plus an intermediate AMD/drusen cohort, randomized 2:1 to intravenous eculizumab versus saline over 24 weeks (with follow-up to 52 weeks) (Yehoshua et al., 2014). Two dosing regimens were tested: a low-dose arm (600 mg weekly × 4, then 900 mg every 2 weeks through week 24) and a high-dose arm with higher induction and maintenance dosing; all patients received meningococcal vaccination prior to first infusion (Yehoshua et al., 2014; Patel et al., 2022). Pharmacokinetically, systemic exposure and target engagement were confirmed: eculizumab concentrations were measurable at all assessed time points (mean ~ 65–189 μg/mL in low-dose and ~ 122–259 μg/mL in high-dose cohorts), with plasma C5 activity reduced to < 9% by week 1 and < 1% by week 2, indicating complete systemic terminal complement blockade sustained through treatment (Yehoshua et al., 2014).

Despite robust systemic pharmacodynamics, COMPLETE detected no effect on GA progression: at 26 weeks the change in square-root–transformed GA area was 0.19 ± 0.12 mm in eculizumab-treated eyes versus 0.18 ± 0.15 mm with placebo (P = 0.96), and at 52 weeks both arms exhibited ~ 0.37 mm enlargement (P ≈ 0.93), effectively excluding a large treatment effect (> 55% slowing) in this cohort (Yehoshua et al., 2014). Drusen volume endpoints in the intermediate AMD cohort were also negative (no participant achieved a 50% reduction), and fellow-eye analyses mirrored primary-eye results (Patel et al., 2022; Yehoshua et al., 2014). Visual function showed no durable benefit: a 26-week letter gain difference favoring eculizumab was attributed to a single outlier in placebo, and by 52 weeks there was no between-group difference; pooled estimates across small RCTs rated the certainty of BCVA effects as low-to-very-low with confidence intervals overlapping no effect (Yehoshua et al., 2014; Tzoumas et al., 2023). Safety was acceptable in the trial: no drug-related serious adverse events were identified through 52 weeks, and no eyes converted to neovascular AMD during the study; however, systemic C5 blockade requires vaccination and vigilance for meningococcal infection, and an independent systemic anti-C5 AMD trial was terminated early due to uncertainty about risk/benefit in light of reported meningococcal infections in real-world eculizumab users (Yehoshua et al., 2014; Tzoumas et al., 2023).

Interpretations from COMPLETE and subsequent reviews point to several plausible explanations for the null result: inadequate intraocular/RPE target engagement with systemic dosing due to blood–retinal and Bruch’s barriers; the possibility that C5 is the wrong target in GA given genetic associations that more strongly implicate proximal complement components (e.g., C3, CFH) rather than terminal pathway constituents; the short on-treatment window (24 weeks) and small sample size; and the biological reality that C5 inhibition preserves upstream C3a/C3b biology, which may continue to drive pathology (Yehoshua et al., 2014; Halawa et al., 2021; Hammadi et al., 2023; Tzoumas et al., 2023).

Comparative C5 and complement-targeting experience in AMD  
Intravitreal C5 inhibition has produced divergent results. LFG316 (tesidolumab), an anti-C5 monoclonal antibody administered intravitreally, failed to alter GA lesion growth or visual outcomes in a randomized phase 2 trial (Halawa et al., 2021; Park et al., 2019). By contrast, avacincaptad pegol (Zimura), an intravitreal C5 aptamer, reduced GA growth rates by approximately 27–30% over 12–18 months in pivotal trials, leading to FDA approval, although potential increased risk of macular neovascularization has been signaled and requires ongoing pharmacovigilance (Patel et al., 2022; Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023; Tzoumas et al., 2023). Upstream targeting at C3 with intravitreal pegcetacoplan (SYFOVRE) significantly slowed GA enlargement in phase 2–3 programs and achieved regulatory approval, but at the cost of increased rates of conversion to exudative AMD and, in the postmarketing setting, reports of occlusive retinal vasculitis, highlighting a target-dependent safety trade-off between efficacy and pro-angiogenic/immune-privilege perturbation risks (Halawa et al., 2021; Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023; Qin et al., 2024). Alternative-pathway inhibition via factor D (lampalizumab) produced a phase 2 signal but failed in two phase 3 trials, suggesting that partial pathway blockade may be insufficient in GA (Piri & Kaplan, 2023; Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023).

Taken together, these data place systemic eculizumab among C5-directed strategies where local delivery has demonstrated anatomic efficacy in GA, whereas systemic delivery has not, and they highlight that complement modulation can slow atrophy progression without improving visual function and may introduce target- and route-specific risks such as neovascularization or serious infection (Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023; Tzoumas et al., 2023).

Mechanism of Action  
Complement cascade biology and eculizumab target engagement  
Complement activation arises via classical, lectin, and alternative pathways, converging at the generation of C3 convertases that cleave C3 into C3a (anaphylatoxin) and C3b (opsonin/convertase component), with amplification by the alternative pathway accounting for the majority (80–90%) of downstream MAC generation in physiologic and pathologic settings (Tzoumas et al., 2023). C5 convertases (C4b2a3b for classical/lectin and C3bBbC3b for alternative pathway) cleave C5 into C5a, a potent anaphylatoxin that recruits/activates myeloid cells and stimulates RPE to produce VEGF and proinflammatory cytokines, and C5b, which seeds assembly of the terminal membrane attack complex (C5b–C9) on nearby membranes (Tzoumas et al., 2023; Kassa et al., 2019). Eculizumab binds C5 to prevent convertase-mediated cleavage, thereby abrogating both C5a generation and MAC formation independent of the initiating pathway; this preserves upstream C3 activation (C3a/C3b) and the alternative-pathway amplification loop while blunting terminal effector functions (Park et al., 2019; Halawa et al., 2021).

Ocular and RPE relevance of C5 and MAC  
Multiple lines of evidence implicate terminal pathway effectors in AMD pathophysiology at the RPE–choriocapillaris interface. Histopathology shows increasing MAC deposition with aging and AMD in Bruch’s membrane and the choriocapillaris; sub-lytic MAC on choroidal endothelial cells triggers pro-angiogenic/proteolytic responses (VEGF, MMPs), while high-level deposition can induce lysis of choroidal endothelial cells and bystander injury contributing to RPE atrophy (Kassa et al., 2019). C5a is present in drusen and is a strong inflammatory mediator that can recruit immune cells and induce VEGF release from RPE, linking terminal pathway activation to both atrophic and neovascular processes relevant to the AMD spectrum (Kassa et al., 2019). Additional experimental work demonstrates that sub-lytic C5b-9 can alter RPE function in ways consistent with AMD, including stress responses and secretory changes, reinforcing that terminal complement activation can be injurious to the RPE even in the absence of frank lysis (Park et al., 2019).

Complement production and regulation in the eye—and systemic versus local contributions  
Complement proteins are synthesized predominantly by the liver and circulate systemically, but they are also produced locally within ocular tissues (retina, RPE, choroid), with local activation and regulation shaped by cell-intrinsic complement regulatory proteins (e.g., CFH, CD46, CD59) and by oxidative stress effects that reduce cell-surface complement inhibition on RPE (Tzoumas et al., 2023; van Lookeren Campagne et al., 2016). Several studies highlight local complement expression and activation in retinal cells and macrophages, as well as increased local complement turnover preceding clinical disease, supporting the concept of an ocular complement “microenvironment” that can be dysregulated independent of systemic levels (Patel et al., 2022; Piri & Kaplan, 2023). Conversely, transcriptomic mapping suggests that posterior-pole tissues have limited local transcription of lectin and terminal pathway components (except C5 and C7), implying reliance on systemic sources for some terminal pathway proteins, which in principle could make systemic C5 blockade effective if sufficient intraocular penetration and target engagement are achieved (Troutbeck et al., 2012). These seemingly conflicting observations are reconciled by recognizing that both local production and systemic supply contribute to ocular complement biology, and that their relative importance likely varies by compartment (RPE, choriocapillaris, sub-RPE space), disease stage, and barrier integrity.

Genetic and biomarker context for C5 targeting in AMD  
GWAS and biomarker studies have repeatedly implicated complement dysregulation in AMD susceptibility and progression, including associations at CFH, C3, C2/CFB, CFI, and C9 loci, and elevated systemic and ocular concentrations of complement activation products (C3a, C5a, MAC) in AMD patients (Dascalu et al., 2024). However, the genetic signal is stronger and more consistent for proximal regulators (e.g., CFH, C3) than for C5, and at least one analysis noted C3 risk alleles do not necessarily correlate with GA progression (and in some contexts associated with decreased progression), emphasizing the complexity of translating susceptibility signals to progression biology and target selection in late AMD (Yehoshua et al., 2014; Halawa et al., 2021). Mechanistically, C5 blockade leaves upstream C3a/C3b biology intact; in extra-ocular diseases this can unmask C3-related pathophysiology under C5 inhibition, illustrating a general limitation of terminal pathway-only targeting (Hammadi et al., 2023). In GA, this implies that C3a/C3b-driven processes—chemoattraction, opsonization, and immune modulation—could continue in the outer retina/RPE even under complete C5 suppression, potentially blunting the benefit of C5-only therapy (Halawa et al., 2021; Tzoumas et al., 2023).

Therapeutic delivery constraints to ocular complement modulation with systemic C5 blockade  
Large biologics such as eculizumab face formidable physical barriers to ocular penetration, including the inner and outer blood–retina barriers and Bruch’s membrane/RPE tight junctions, whose permeability declines with increasing molecular weight and hydrophilicity (a profile characteristic of IgG antibodies), making intraocular access by systemic dosing uncertain without active transport mechanisms (Hammadi et al., 2023; Yehoshua et al., 2014). This is the most straightforward explanation for COMPLETE’s combination of complete systemic pharmacodynamic suppression with no effect on lesion enlargement: the effective intraocular concentration of antibody at relevant RPE–choroid interfaces was likely insufficient to inhibit terminal complement activation locally, a conclusion echoed by the trial investigators (Yehoshua et al., 2014).

Expected Effect  
Proposed pharmacologic and cellular effect  
Under the working hypothesis, systemic eculizumab would reduce MAC formation on RPE and choroidal endothelial surfaces and diminish C5a-driven inflammatory signaling, thereby protecting RPE cellular homeostasis and the RPE–choriocapillaris unit from complement-mediated injury and slowing expansion of GA lesions (Kassa et al., 2019; Park et al., 2019). Mechanistically, suppression of MAC would be expected to decrease lytic and sub-lytic injury to choroidal endothelial cells and RPE, attenuating downstream induction of VEGF and MMPs, and limiting bystander tissue damage around atrophic borders where complement activation is thought to be active (Tzoumas et al., 2023; Kassa et al., 2019). Reducing C5a generation could blunt recruitment and activation of myeloid cells and RPE proinflammatory cytokine production, decreasing local inflammatory amplification in the outer retina (Tzoumas et al., 2023; Kassa et al., 2019).

Expression and target presence in relevant cells/tissues  
C5 and terminal pathway components are present in the ocular compartments relevant to GA pathobiology: choriocapillaris/Bruch’s membrane deposition of MAC is well documented and increases with age and AMD, and C5a is detected within drusen; RPE-derived complement regulators (e.g., CD59) and choroidal endothelial susceptibility to MAC are described, establishing both target and downstream effectors within the disease-relevant tissues (Kassa et al., 2019). Complement components and regulators are expressed in retina, RPE, and choroid, with transcriptomic data indicating relatively higher choroidal expression and limited local transcription of some terminal components (except C5 and C7), consistent with both local and systemic sources contributing substrate for terminal pathway assembly (Troutbeck et al., 2012; Tzoumas et al., 2023).

Translational and clinical outcome expectations  
At the disease level, a therapy that meaningfully reduces local terminal complement activation in GA should slow the rate of GA lesion enlargement measured on fundus autofluorescence (FAF) and/or SD-OCT, endpoints accepted by the FDA/NEI and standard across GA trials; functional outcomes such as BCVA and low-luminance VA typically lag structural changes and may not improve even when lesion growth is slowed (Dascalu et al., 2024; Qin et al., 2024). In COMPLETE, these endpoints were precisely those evaluated; the absence of any measurable effect on square-root GA area at 26 and 52 weeks indicates that, at the tested systemic dosing regimens, eculizumab did not achieve sufficient intraocular target engagement to translate the mechanistic rationale into clinical benefit (Yehoshua et al., 2014).

Reasons for divergence between expected and observed effects  
Three non-exclusive explanations align with the totality of evidence:

  • Inadequate intraocular bioavailability from systemic dosing. Antibody size and hydrophilicity, combined with blood–retina and RPE–Bruch’s barriers, likely prevented sufficient drug from reaching the RPE–choriocapillaris compartment to inhibit local terminal pathway activation, despite verified systemic C5 suppression (Hammadi et al., 2023; Yehoshua et al., 2014; Tzoumas et al., 2023).

  • Target selection may be suboptimal for GA biology. The stronger genetic and pathophysiologic evidence for proximal complement dysregulation (e.g., C3, CFH) and the preservation of upstream activation under C5 blockade mean that C5-only inhibition may not adequately suppress the complement-driven processes that sustain GA border expansion, as supported by the comparatively stronger clinical signals with C3 inhibition and mixed-to-positive signals with intravitreal C5 inhibition (Yehoshua et al., 2014; Halawa et al., 2021; Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023).

  • Trial limitations. The COMPLETE cohort was small and on-treatment duration was six months, which may be insufficient to observe smaller effects on GA growth; however, confidence intervals excluded large effects, and lack of any trend over 52 weeks reduces the likelihood that longer dosing would have changed the outcome meaningfully (Yehoshua et al., 2014).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
  • Strong mechanistic rationale for terminal pathway blockade in AMD: C5a is proinflammatory and detected in drusen; MAC accumulates at the RPE–choriocapillaris interface with age/AMD and can injure choroidal endothelium and RPE via lytic and sub-lytic mechanisms, linking C5 effector functions directly to AMD pathophysiology (Kassa et al., 2019). Blocking C5 suppresses both MAC and C5a, targeting inflammatory and cytolytic arms while leaving upstream C3-mediated opsonization intact, an immunologic advantage over C3 blockade (Halawa et al., 2021; Park et al., 2019).

  • Mature systemic pharmacology with demonstrated ability to completely suppress plasma C5 activity at well-tolerated doses, including in AMD patients, making systemic development logistically straightforward and with known requirements (e.g., meningococcal vaccination) (Yehoshua et al., 2014).

  • Practical systemic-delivery advantages: single systemic therapy treats both eyes simultaneously and avoids intravitreal injection–related ocular risks (endophthalmitis, IOP spikes), which have nontrivial incidence in chronic IVT regimens (Yehoshua et al., 2014; Tzoumas et al., 2023).

  • External validation within the class: intravitreal C5 inhibition (avacincaptad pegol) has replicated GA-slowing effects in phase 2/3 programs, supporting the therapeutic value of suppressing terminal pathway activity in the outer retina if adequate local exposure is achieved (Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023).

Limitations  
  • Lack of efficacy in the definitive systemic eculizumab GA study. COMPLETE showed no reduction in GA lesion growth at 26 or 52 weeks despite complete systemic C5 blockade and adequate serum exposure, effectively excluding a large treatment effect and arguing strongly against systemic eculizumab as a viable GA therapy (Yehoshua et al., 2014).

  • Ocular pharmacokinetic barrier. The most parsimonious explanation for the COMPLETE result is inadequate intraocular target engagement from systemic dosing due to the blood–retina/RPE–Bruch’s barriers and the large size/hydrophilicity of IgG, an inherent limitation of systemic antibody delivery to the sub-RPE/choroidal compartment (Hammadi et al., 2023; Yehoshua et al., 2014).

  • Target-level uncertainties. Genetic and biomarker studies more consistently implicate proximal complement dysregulation (CFH/C3) than C5 in AMD susceptibility; moreover, C5 inhibition preserves upstream C3a/C3b activity, which may continue to drive border-zone inflammation and remodeling in GA (Yehoshua et al., 2014; Dascalu et al., 2024; Halawa et al., 2021). Clinical comparisons reinforce this: upstream C3 inhibition has produced larger and more consistent anatomic effects (with important safety trade-offs), whereas anti-C5 trials are split—systemic eculizumab negative; IVT LFG316 negative; IVT avacincaptad positive—pointing to delivery and target–context dependencies (Halawa et al., 2021; Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023).

  • Systemic safety constraints. While COMPLETE itself reported no drug-related serious adverse events over one year, terminal complement blockade carries a class risk of meningococcal infection requiring vaccination and risk mitigation; at least one planned AMD systemic anti-C5 trial was terminated early due to uncertainty regarding the systemic infection risk/benefit in this population (Yehoshua et al., 2014; Tzoumas et al., 2023).

  • Limited translatability of systemic complement activation to local disease control. Although systemic complement activation correlates with AMD and disease stage in several studies, the relative importance of local versus systemic complement sources—and the ability of systemic blockade to suppress complement activity at the RPE–choriocapillaris interface—is unresolved and likely unfavorable for large biologics, reducing the mechanistic plausibility of systemic C5 blockade for GA (Hammadi et al., 2023; Park et al., 2019).

Risk–benefit and competitive landscape assessment  
Eculizumab’s negative randomized, controlled data in GA, in the face of confirmed systemic pharmacodynamics, places a high bar against further systemic development for this indication, a conclusion explicitly drawn by the COMPLETE investigators who noted the linearity of GA growth and lack of signal as making additional systemic investigations with eculizumab unlikely (Yehoshua et al., 2014). In the evolving complement-therapeutic landscape, local (intravitreal) complement inhibitors have achieved regulatory approval (C3 inhibitor pegcetacoplan and C5 aptamer avacincaptad pegol), demonstrating that complement modulation can slow GA progression when adequate ocular exposure is achieved, albeit without functional vision improvement and with target-dependent safety liabilities (e.g., increased conversion to exudative AMD observed particularly with C3 inhibition) (Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023; Qin et al., 2024). Against this backdrop, systemic eculizumab offers no efficacy advantage, carries systemic infection risk and costs, and fails the core requirement of achieving ocular target engagement necessary for GA modification.

Operationally, even if one hypothesized that higher systemic dosing, longer exposure, or patient stratification could rescue efficacy, several facts weigh against that strategy: COMPLETE’s pharmacodynamics confirmed near-complete systemic C5 suppression within two weeks; genetic analyses within the trial found no association between at-risk alleles and GA growth or treatment response; and extended follow-up to a year showed no divergence in growth rates (Yehoshua et al., 2014). Moreover, retinal pharmacokinetics and barrier biology make it unlikely that increasing systemic doses would proportionally increase intraocular concentrations to therapeutic levels without exacerbating systemic risks (Hammadi et al., 2023). In addition, C5 may not be the optimal node for GA progression biology in many patients, given preserved upstream activity and the complex contributions of C3a/C3b, opsonization, and local complement production that may continue to drive border-zone degeneration independent of MAC inhibition (Yehoshua et al., 2014; Halawa et al., 2021; Piri & Kaplan, 2023).

Recommendation  
Based on the aggregate mechanistic, translational, and clinical evidence, eculizumab (systemic C5 inhibition) does not have compelling potential as a repurposed therapy for dry AMD/GA. The drug has a clear and relevant mechanism and an acceptable short-term tolerability profile in older adults, but it fails to slow GA lesion growth in a randomized, controlled trial despite complete systemic target engagement, and there are strong mechanistic and pharmacokinetic reasons to expect insufficient intraocular exposure with systemic delivery (Yehoshua et al., 2014; Hammadi et al., 2023). The therapeutic hypothesis—reducing MAC on RPE/choriocapillaris to preserve tissue integrity and slow atrophy—is supported conceptually and by local C5 inhibition successes; however, achieving that biology in vivo appears to require intraocular delivery or targeting upstream nodes, neither of which is compatible with systemic eculizumab administration (Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023; Halawa et al., 2021).

If complement-targeted strategies remain a program priority, resources should pivot to modalities with demonstrated ocular target engagement and anatomic efficacy (intravitreal C3 or C5 agents) while carefully managing known safety trade-offs (e.g., neovascularization risk), and to biomarker-driven questions (e.g., whether baseline low-luminance visual acuity deficit—correlated with faster GA growth in COMPLETE—can enrich for patients who derive greater anatomic benefit) (Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023; Tzoumas et al., 2023; Yehoshua et al., 2014). For systemic complement modulation in AMD more broadly, alternative targets with better ocular PK profiles (e.g., small-molecule systemic alternative-pathway inhibitors) and rigorous ocular target-engagement measurements would be required to justify late-stage development, but systemic C5 blockade with eculizumab itself should not be advanced further for GA (Hammadi et al., 2023; Tzoumas et al., 2023; Park et al., 2019).

In summary, while the mechanistic logic for terminal complement inhibition in AMD is robust, the totality of evidence argues against systemic eculizumab as a viable candidate to slow GA progression, and the development program should discontinue pursuit of this repurposing in favor of locally delivered complement inhibitors or alternative mechanisms better suited to the ocular compartment and GA pathobiology (Yehoshua et al., 2014; Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023).
