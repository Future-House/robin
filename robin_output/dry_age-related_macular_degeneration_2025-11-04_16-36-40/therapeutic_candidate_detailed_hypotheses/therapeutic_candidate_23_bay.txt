Proposal for BAY 11-7082 (Sigma B5556)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
BAY 11-7082 is a synthetic, small-molecule inhibitor originally discovered as an irreversible inhibitor of IκBα phosphorylation, which blocks NF-κB activation. Chemically, it is classified as a phenyl vinyl sulfone compound featuring an α,β-unsaturated moiety that functions as a Michael acceptor. This reactive electrophilic center enables the compound to covalently modify nucleophilic cysteine residues on target proteins. Early studies identified BAY 11-7082 for its capacity to inhibit NF-κB signaling in endothelial cells, and subsequent research expanded its profile to include inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome via cysteine alkylation of critical regulatory sites. In essence, BAY 11-7082 belongs to the class of covalent inhibitors that have been widely studied in the context of inflammatory and oncologic diseases, and its availability as Sigma B5556 has made it a common tool compound in preclinical research (Juliana et al., 2010; Marchetti, 2019; Yu et al., 2018).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, BAY 11-7082 has been utilized extensively in biochemical, cellular, and animal studies as an anti-inflammatory agent. Initial applications centered on its ability to block the phosphorylation and degradation of IκBα, thereby inhibiting downstream NF-κB–driven gene expression. This mechanism was exploited in models of endothelial inflammation and various cancer cell lines, including studies showing potent anticancer, neuroprotective, and anti-inflammatory effects (Lee et al., 2012; Melisi & Chiao, 2007). Furthermore, its repurposing has been explored in fields as diverse as hematologic malignancies, diabetes-related complications, and preclinical psoriasis models, where it served as a dual inhibitor of NF-κB and the NLRP3 inflammasome (Irrera et al., 2017; Kolati et al., 2015). Despite this broad research history, BAY 11-7082 has not been previously tested explicitly in the context of dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) or diseases with a similar pathology in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). However, its known capacity to reduce inflammatory cytokine production and preserve lysosomal integrity in nonocular cell types provides a rationale for evaluating its potential utility in dry AMD—a disease characterized by chronic inflammation, impaired lysosomal clearance, and inflammasome activation (Blevins et al., 2022; Irrera et al., 2017; Marchetti, 2019).

Mechanism of Action:  
BAY 11-7082 exerts its pharmacological effects predominantly through irreversible covalent inhibition. Its most well‐characterized mechanism is the blockade of NF-κB signaling by preventing the phosphorylation of the inhibitory protein IκBα. Under normal conditions, phosphorylation of IκBα triggers its degradation, thus allowing NF-κB (typically the p65/p50 heterodimer) to translocate into the nucleus and activate pro-inflammatory target genes. By inhibiting IκBα phosphorylation, BAY 11-7082 effectively sequesters NF-κB in the cytosol, thereby suppressing the expression of genes associated with inflammation such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and various chemokines (Marchetti, 2019; Melisi & Chiao, 2007).  

Moreover, BAY 11-7082 has been repurposed based on its ability to inhibit the NLRP3 inflammasome. Mechanistically, the compound forms covalent adducts with reactive cysteine residues on the NLRP3 protein, most likely within the ATPase domain—an essential activity domain required for inflammasome activation. This alkylation event, which proceeds via a Michael-type addition reaction, results in irreversible inactivation of NLRP3’s ATPase function, thereby preventing the assembly and subsequent activation of the inflammasome complex (Cocco et al., 2014; Juliana et al., 2010; Marchetti, 2019). In addition to these primary actions, BAY 11-7082 has been reported to inhibit other cysteine-dependent enzymes such as protein tyrosine phosphatases and even components like gasdermin D that contribute to pyroptosis. Consequently, the compound displays a broad-spectrum inhibitory profile that may diminish inflammatory cascades not only at the transcriptional (NF-κB) level but also at the inflammasome activation stage (Yu et al., 2018; Zahid et al., 2019).  

Expected Effect in Dry Age-Related Macular Degeneration:  
In the context of dry AMD, the hypothesis is that chronic inflammation in the RPE drives cellular dysfunction through mechanisms that include sustained NF-κB activation and priming of the NLRP3 inflammasome. Inflammatory cytokines and resultant oxidative stress contribute to lysosomal impairment, reducing the activity of v-ATPase and lysosomal proteases such as cathepsin D (CTSD), which are crucial for the clearance of cellular debris. BAY 11-7082 is expected to intervene at an early stage in this inflammatory cascade by inhibiting NF-κB signaling. In doing so, it would reduce the transcriptional upregulation of inflammasome components and pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly IL-1β, a key mediator in inflammasome activation (Blevins et al., 2022; Marchetti, 2019).  

Experimental studies in macrophages and psoriasis models have demonstrated that NF-κB inhibition with BAY 11-7082 leads to a reduction in IL-1β production and preserves lysosomal integrity. Although these studies were conducted in nonocular tissues, the expression of NF-κB and NLRP3 inflammasome components in RPE cells is well-documented in the literature. Therefore, it is reasonable to propose that BAY 11-7082, when applied to RPE cell models, could diminish the priming of the NLRP3 inflammasome. This reduction in inflammasome activity is expected to mitigate downstream lysosomal damage, preserve v-ATPase function, and maintain cathepsin D–mediated clearance. Ultimately, these cellular improvements could slow the degenerative progression characteristic of dry AMD (Cocco et al., 2014; Irrera et al., 2017).  

The proposed testing strategy leverages the rapid on–off pharmacology observed in cell culture models, facilitating dose optimization to achieve sufficient anti-inflammatory activity while minimizing potential toxicity. Importantly, targeting an upstream event, namely NF-κB activation and inflammasome priming, distinguishes BAY 11-7082 from therapeutic strategies that focus on downstream caspase inhibition. In retinal cells, preservation of lysosomal function is critical; thus, preventing inflammasome-triggered lysosomal rupture could have a marked protective effect on RPE health (Ajetunmobi et al., 2023; Zahid et al., 2019).

Overall Evaluation:  
BAY 11-7082 presents a compelling therapeutic candidate for dry AMD based on its dual mechanism of action. One of its primary strengths is that it targets two key upstream pathways—inhibition of NF-κB signaling and direct inactivation of the NLRP3 inflammasome—which are critically implicated in the inflammatory processes that drive RPE dysfunction and subsequent retinal degeneration in dry AMD. This upstream intervention could, in theory, reduce the chronic production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and prevent lysosomal abnormalities that compromise the clearance of metabolic waste in RPE cells. The promising preclinical data from psoriasis, diabetic nephropathy, and other inflammatory disease models provide an encouraging foundation regarding its anti-inflammatory capacity (Blevins et al., 2022; Irrera et al., 2017; Marchetti, 2019).

However, there are several potential weaknesses and challenges to consider. First, BAY 11-7082 operates via irreversible covalent binding through its Michael acceptor; while this can yield sustained inhibition, it also raises significant concerns regarding off-target effects and general toxicity due to the potential for nonselective alkylation of other cysteine-containing proteins. Such promiscuous reactivity has been observed in studies involving diverse cell types, and cellular toxicity in contexts such as platelets or myeloma cells has been reported, albeit at higher concentrations (Coles et al., 2021; Paul et al., 2017; Rauert-Wunderlich et al., 2013). Second, although extensive preclinical data exist demonstrating its anti-inflammatory and anti-cancer activity, BAY 11-7082 has not been previously evaluated in ocular tissues. The unique physiology of the eye, particularly the retinal pigment epithelium, could result in significantly different pharmacokinetics and tissue-specific toxicity profiles. The documented rapid on–off pharmacology in cell culture is encouraging, yet extrapolation to an in vivo retinal environment must be approached cautiously (Paul et al., 2017; Zahid et al., 2019).  

Another consideration is the narrow therapeutic window that may exist; while doses in animal models have been optimized for systemic inflammation or cancer, achieving effective local concentrations in the RPE without compromising cell viability is a delicate balance. Previous studies in nonocular systems suggest that BAY 11-7082 can function without overt toxicity at concentrations up to 20 μM in cell culture and 20 mg/kg/day in animal models, but whether similar dosing regimens would be applicable for ocular delivery remains to be determined (Mabuchi et al., 2004; Paul et al., 2017). Additionally, because BAY 11-7082 modulates ubiquitin system components beyond NF-κB and NLRP3 inflammasome targets, there is a risk of unintended interference with normal protein turnover and cellular homeostasis, potentially exacerbating cellular stress in a tissue already vulnerable to degenerative processes, as seen in dry AMD (Coles et al., 2021; Strickson et al., 2013).

In summary, BAY 11-7082 shows promise as a candidate repurposed for dry AMD because it strategically targets early inflammatory events that have been implicated in RPE dysfunction, including NF-κB–mediated inflammasome priming and consequent lysosomal impairment. The compound’s established anti-inflammatory effects in various preclinical models lend strong support to the hypothesis that its use in RPE could preserve lysosomal function and enhance CTSD-mediated clearance. Nonetheless, the inherent risks associated with its covalent, irreversible mechanism demand rigorous ocular-specific toxicity and pharmacokinetics investigations. If dose optimization and targeted delivery—perhaps through localized ocular formulations—can mitigate off-target effects, BAY 11-7082 might offer a novel approach that differentiates itself from therapies focused solely on downstream caspase inhibition. Overall, while the repurposing of BAY 11-7082 for dry AMD is mechanistically attractive and supported by a broad preclinical research history in nonocular inflammatory models (Blevins et al., 2022; Irrera et al., 2017; Marchetti, 2019), further dedicated studies in retinal cell models and in vivo ocular systems are essential to fully validate its potential and safety profile for this indication.
