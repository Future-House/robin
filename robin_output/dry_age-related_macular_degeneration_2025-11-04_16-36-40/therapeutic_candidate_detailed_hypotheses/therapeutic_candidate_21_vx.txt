Proposal for VX-765 (Belnacasan; Selleckchem S2731)

Below is our comprehensive evaluation of VX-765 (Belnacasan; Selleckchem S2731) as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD).

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
VX-765 is an orally bioavailable small‐molecule agent belonging to the caspase-1 inhibitor class. It is a synthetically derived peptidomimetic prodrug that, once metabolized, yields an active derivative capable of selectively binding to and inhibiting caspase-1. Originally developed by Vertex Pharmaceuticals, VX-765 was designed to address the shortcomings of earlier peptide-based caspase inhibitors by improving bioavailability, stability, and selectivity. As a caspase-1 inhibitor, it falls within a broader category of agents that have been used to blunt excessive inflammasome responses in various inflammatory and immune-mediated conditions. In the context of drug discovery, caspase-1 inhibitors have been of keen interest because caspase-1 is the key enzyme responsible for converting pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into their active cytokine forms, thereby playing a central role in mediating inflammation and the programmed cell death process known as pyroptosis. VX-765 has been evaluated primarily in systemic inflammatory and cardiovascular settings, where its favorable pharmacokinetic profile and excellent oral bioavailability have been demonstrated (Audia et al., 2018; MacKenzie & Schipper, 2010).

Therapeutic History:  
The therapeutic history of VX-765 underscores its extensive preclinical development and early clinical evaluation in several inflammatory settings. In preclinical cardiovascular models, for instance, VX-765 demonstrated significant cardioprotection when administered during reperfusion, reducing infarct size and preserving mitochondrial enzyme activities (Audia et al., 2018). It has undergone Phase I and Phase II clinical studies—its Phase II safety data indicate that it is well tolerated at doses that achieve a suppression of proinflammatory cytokines in plasma (MacKenzie & Schipper, 2010). Although the major clinical investigations with VX-765 have focused on disorders such as epilepsy and other inflammatory diseases rather than ophthalmic disorders, the rationale for its use in dry AMD stems from overlapping inflammatory mechanisms. Specifically, dry AMD shares underlying pathological processes such as chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and lysosomal dysfunction in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells. Prior experimental studies in RPE models using caspase-1 inhibitors like Z-YVAD-FMK have demonstrated that inhibiting caspase-1 activity reduces IL-1β release and can influence cell death pathways (Brandstetter, 2016; Tseng et al., 2013). However, despite this promising mechanistic background, VX-765 itself has not yet been directly evaluated in preclinical models of dry AMD or in similar ocular diseases. This gap in ophthalmic precedent is notable, but given its established safety profile in nonocular clinical settings and robust anti-inflammatory activity, VX-765 remains an attractive candidate for repurposing into the dry AMD arena (Audia et al., 2018; MacKenzie & Schipper, 2010).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, VX-765 exerts its effect primarily through selective inhibition of caspase-1, a cysteine protease critically involved in the processing of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Under conditions of cellular stress—such as those occurring in RPE cells in response to lipofuscin accumulation and photooxidative damage—lysosomal destabilization can occur. This lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP) leads to the release of cathepsins and other proteases into the cytosol, which, in turn, can serve as an activator of the NLRP3 inflammasome. Once assembled, the inflammasome facilitates the autocatalytic cleavage of caspase-1 into its active p10/p20 subunits, thereby enabling it to convert pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into their mature, secreted forms. VX-765 binds to the active site of caspase-1, thereby preventing this proteolytic cleavage and subsequent cytokine release. This action not only blunts the inflammatory cascade but, by inhibiting caspase-1–dependent processing, may also prevent the activation of gasdermin-D (GSDMD) which is responsible for forming membrane pores during pyroptosis (Brandstetter, 2016; Tseng et al., 2013).

The hypothesis for using VX-765 in dry AMD is that inhibition of caspase-1 will disrupt a pathological amplification loop. In RPE cells exposed to chronic oxidative stress and lipofuscin-related photooxidative damage, inflammasome activation has been shown to promote rapid pyroptotic cell death rather than a slower apoptotic process. This results in an autocrine/paracrine loop where secreted IL-1β further primes neighboring cells for inflammasome activation, exacerbating cell death and causing tissue dysfunction. By blocking caspase-1 activity, VX-765 is expected to prevent this destructive cascade, thereby preserving lysosomal function. More specifically, there is a hypothesized link between caspase-1 activity and alterations in lysosomal integrity—a process involving LMP that is associated with a loss of vacuolar (V-) ATPase activity and disturbances in cathepsin D maturation. The V-ATPase is critical for maintaining an acidic lysosomal environment necessary for optimal enzyme activity, including that of cathepsin D, which is involved in the degradation of photoreceptor outer segments. Some studies have shown that under conditions of inflammasome activation, RPE cells demonstrate not only an increase in cytokine secretion but also a decline in the functionality of lysosomal hydrolases, which may further compromise cell viability (Brandstetter, 2016). Although direct evidence linking VX-765 to the preservation of V-ATPase activity or cathepsin D function in RPE is currently lacking, the mechanistic framework provided by previous studies using other caspase-1 inhibitors supports the notion that caspase-1 blockade may confer lysosomal protection and attenuate pyroptotic RPE death (Brandstetter, 2016; Tseng et al., 2013).

Expected Effect:  
In the proposed in vitro and in vivo assays, VX-765 is expected to reduce the levels of active caspase-1 and consequently diminish the secretion of IL-1β and IL-18 from RPE cells stressed by inflammatory stimuli such as lipofuscin photooxidative damage. In validated ARPE-19 cell models that mimic the conditions of dry AMD—where priming with IL-1α and subsequent exposure to lysosomal stressors (for example, the photooxidative effects of lipofuscin components) lead to inflammasome activation and rapid pyroptotic cell death—treatment with caspase-1 inhibitors has been shown to reduce cell lysis and cytokine release (Brandstetter, 2016; Tseng et al., 2013). By extrapolation, VX-765 should produce similar results. Endpoints for such studies might include decreased levels of IL-1β and IL-18 measured by ELISA, preservation of cell viability as determined by metabolic assays (such as MTT or LDH assays), and a reduction in markers of pyroptosis including the cleavage of GSDMD.

Furthermore, because the hypothesis posits that caspase-1 activity contributes to inflammasome‐mediated lysosomal membrane permeabilization, VX-765 is anticipated to stabilize lysosomal integrity. This stabilization should help maintain the acid–base balance within lysosomes—reflected by sustained V-ATPase activity—which is critical for the proper maturation and function of cathepsin D. In healthy RPE cells, cathepsin D is a major lysosomal protease responsible for degrading photoreceptor outer segment debris. In the context of dry AMD, a decline in cathepsin D activity may contribute to the accumulation of undegraded material, exacerbating cellular stress and death. Although direct measurements of V-ATPase activity and cathepsin D maturation following VX-765 treatment have not yet been published, the overall expectation is that by interrupting the caspase-1–driven inflammatory signaling cascade, VX-765 can prevent the deleterious loss of lysosomal function and thereby reduce RPE cell death (Brandstetter, 2016; Gao et al., 2019; Tseng et al., 2013).

Additionally, in in vivo models of AMD (for example, those induced by Aβ₁₋₄₀ or other drusen mimetics), the inhibition of caspase-1 should theoretically reduce the incidence or severity of geographic atrophy by preserving RPE structure and function. This is of particular interest given that RPE degeneration is a critical event in the progression of dry AMD. Markers of efficacy would include improved retinal morphology on OCT imaging, better electrophysiological responses (as measured by ERG), and a decrease in inflammatory cellular infiltrates. Collectively, these anticipated outcomes support the hypothesis that VX-765’s inhibition of caspase-1 may interrupt the cascade of inflammasome activation, LMP, and lysosomal enzyme failure that leads to RPE death in dry AMD (Brandstetter, 2016; Tseng et al., 2013).

Overall Evaluation:  
Our overall evaluation of VX-765 as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD is cautiously optimistic but recognizes several critical hurdles. One of the major strengths of VX-765 is its well-characterized mechanism of action as a selective caspase-1 inhibitor, along with its demonstrated anti-inflammatory effects in non-ocular preclinical models. The clinical safety profile established in Phase I and II studies—particularly its ability to reduce circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines—lends support to its potential use in a chronic disease setting such as dry AMD (Audia et al., 2018; MacKenzie & Schipper, 2010). From a mechanistic standpoint, the centrality of caspase-1 in mediating inflammasome-driven cytokine maturation and pyroptosis is well established, and there is compelling evidence from RPE cell models that caspase-1 inhibition can reduce IL-1β secretion and potentially modify cell death pathways (Brandstetter, 2016; Tseng et al., 2013). These observations provide a strong rationale for the hypothesis that VX-765 could prevent the lysosomal destabilization and subsequent inflammatory cascade responsible for RPE degeneration.

However, there are also several weaknesses and gaps that must be addressed before VX-765 can be fully endorsed for dry AMD. Although the mechanistic data support the role of caspase-1 inhibition in reducing inflammatory cytokine release and possibly preserving lysosomal function, there is currently no direct evidence that VX-765 can stabilize lysosomal membrane integrity, preserve V-ATPase activity, or maintain proper cathepsin D function specifically in RPE cells. Most of the available literature has utilized generic caspase-1 inhibitors such as Z-YVAD-FMK as proof-of-principle tools rather than VX-765 itself in ocular models (Brandstetter, 2016; Tseng et al., 2013). Furthermore, although numerous studies have demonstrated that inflammasome activation and caspase-1–dependent pyroptosis are features of RPE injury in vitro and in some in vivo models of dry AMD, the translation of these findings into effective therapies has proven challenging. The eye’s unique anatomy and the need for adequate drug delivery to the retina raise additional concerns. For an orally administered agent like VX-765, it is not yet clear whether systemic dosing can achieve therapeutically relevant concentrations in the RPE, or whether intravitreal or alternative delivery methods would be necessary.

Another potential weakness lies in the multifactorial nature of dry AMD. In addition to inflammasome activation and lysosomal dysfunction, dry AMD pathogenesis involves complement dysregulation, oxidative stress, impaired autophagy, and other pro-inflammatory pathways. It remains possible that targeting caspase-1 alone may not be sufficient to arrest disease progression, and a combination approach might be necessary. Moreover, previous caspase inhibitor candidates (for example, pralnacasan) have encountered issues with liver toxicity or other off-target effects in long-term studies, raising concerns about the safety of chronic caspase-1 inhibition (MacKenzie & Schipper, 2010). These potential challenges underscore the need for rigorous preclinical studies in relevant dry AMD models that not only assess the anti-inflammatory and anti-pyroptotic effects of VX-765 but also directly measure lysosomal functional parameters such as V-ATPase activity and cathepsin D maturation.

In summary, while VX-765 offers a promising and mechanistically coherent approach to perturbing the detrimental caspase-1–mediated signaling cascade implicated in RPE degeneration, significant gaps remain in linking its action to improved lysosomal function and reduced RPE death in the specific context of dry AMD. The strengths of VX-765 include its established safety data, oral bioavailability, and selective inhibition of a central inflammatory enzyme; however, its application to AMD is currently supported only by indirect evidence extrapolated from non-ocular models and generic caspase-1 inhibitor studies (Audia et al., 2018; Brandstetter, 2016; MacKenzie & Schipper, 2010; Tseng et al., 2013). To move this candidate forward, we recommend that targeted preclinical studies be undertaken in sight-relevant in vitro systems (e.g., ARPE-19 or primary human RPE models) and in vivo models of dry AMD to assess whether VX-765 can indeed prevent or reverse lysosomal membrane permeabilization, sustain V-ATPase activity, and preserve cathepsin D function, in addition to reducing downstream cytokine release and pyroptotic cell death. Only with such data can we determine whether VX-765 holds true promise as a therapeutic for dry AMD.

Overall, while VX-765 has scientifically robust credentials as a caspase-1 inhibitor with proven anti-inflammatory potential in other systems, its repurposing for dry AMD will depend on demonstrating that its molecular actions translate into preservation of RPE function and structure in ocular models. The candidate is promising, but further focused studies are needed to address drug delivery, ocular pharmacokinetics, and the precise impact on lysosomal pathways within the RPE before clinical translation can be considered (Audia et al., 2018; Brandstetter, 2016; MacKenzie & Schipper, 2010; Tseng et al., 2013).
