Proposal for Fingolimod (FTY720; Selleckchem S5002)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Fingolimod (FTY720) is a synthetic, sphingoid-base mimetic that is phosphorylated in vivo by sphingosine kinase 2 (SphK2) to yield the active metabolite FTY720-phosphate (FTY720-P), which binds sphingosine-1-phosphate receptors (S1PRs) with sub-micromolar affinity comparable to endogenous S1P (primarily S1PR1, S1PR3, S1PR4, and S1PR5) (Gupta et al., 2012; Gesualdo et al., 2021). As a small-molecule modulator of S1PRs, fingolimod belongs to the class of GPCR-targeting immunomodulators that regulate lymphocyte trafficking and vascular/endothelial signaling; this class has been deployed extensively in neuroinflammatory disease (e.g., multiple sclerosis) and investigated across ocular inflammatory and vascular pathologies (Gesualdo et al., 2021; Sorenson et al., 2022). In ocular biology, endogenous S1P signaling is pleiotropic: it is detected in, and acts upon, multiple retinal cell types including RPE, endothelium, neurons, and glia, where it can protect photoreceptors from oxidative stress–induced apoptosis and modulate wound repair and barrier function, underscoring the translational appeal of S1PR modulators for retinal degenerations (Gupta et al., 2012). At the same time, the S1P axis exhibits context-dependent outcomes—anti-S1P antibodies reduce subretinal fibrosis in AMD models—so receptor-subtype selectivity, ligand presentation (e.g., apoM/HDL-bound vs free), and cell-type–specific responses will be critical to therapeutic design (Gupta et al., 2012; Jung et al., 2025).

Therapeutic History  
Fingolimod is an approved oral therapy that reduces lesion formation in multiple sclerosis through S1PR-mediated immunomodulation, providing an extensive clinical safety and pharmacology dataset for potential repurposing (Mondal & Mandal, 2019). In ocular indications, preclinical studies demonstrate that S1PR pathway manipulation alters retinal/choroidal vascular pathology: intravitreally delivered S1P chaperoned by apolipoprotein M (apoM) activates endothelial S1PR1 and suppresses choroidal neovascularization (CNV) and vascular leak, and separate approaches show that anti-S1P antibodies or S1PR1-selective agonists can suppress CNV and stabilize endothelial barriers (Jung et al., 2025; Sorenson et al., 2022). Fingolimod itself (as FTY720) reduces choroidal endothelial migration and capillary morphogenesis in vitro and mitigates CNV in vivo, indicating that systemic or intraocular S1PR modulation impacts ocular neovascular disease biology (Sorenson et al., 2022). In diabetic retinopathy models, fingolimod reduces retinal angiogenesis and VEGFA levels, aligning with its anti-angiogenic/vascular-stabilizing profile (Gesualdo et al., 2021). Importantly for the dry AMD context, none of these studies directly evaluate atrophic AMD outcomes, RPE autophagy/lysosome endpoints, or complement-driven injury after fingolimod, and there is no prior clinical use of fingolimod for dry AMD (Jung et al., 2025; Sorenson et al., 2022). A material safety consideration for retinal indications is fingolimod-associated macular edema (FAME), reported in clinical settings and relevant to dosing, route, and patient selection if repurposed for macular disease (Mondal & Mandal, 2019).

Mechanism of Action  
Receptor pharmacology of fingolimod and ocular S1P biology. Fingolimod is phosphorylated by SphK2 to FTY720-P, which behaves as a functional agonist at S1PR1, S1PR4, and S1PR5 and a partial agonist at S1PR3, triggering receptor internalization and signaling adaptation that sequesters lymphocytes and modulates vascular responses (Gupta et al., 2012; Gesualdo et al., 2021). In ocular tissues, S1PR1 is prominently expressed in multiple cell types, with experimental evidence demonstrating S1PR1 expression in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) where S1PR1 appears as the predominant S1PR subtype alongside dynamic regulation of S1PR1/S1PR3 by oxygen tension (Sorenson et al., 2022). RPE also expresses the S1P-generating enzymes SphK1 and SphK2 and can synthesize and release S1P, providing the local pathway components to respond to pharmacologic S1PR modulation (Wang et al., 2023). S1P ligands elicit cell-type–specific responses in the retinal neurovascular unit: endothelial S1PR1 activation stabilizes adherens junctions and quells sprouting angiogenesis, apoM/HDL-bound S1P reduces choroidal leakage and CNV, and S1P/S1PR signaling influences RPE barrier function and angiogenic or inflammatory outputs (Jung et al., 2025; Sorenson et al., 2022). These features render S1PR1 a biologically plausible target in RPE and choroid for modulating disease-relevant pathways in AMD, while also highlighting that the net effect of S1P pathway modulation depends on receptor subtype and ligand context (Jung et al., 2025; Sorenson et al., 2022).

RPE autophagy–lysosome biology, lysosomal acidification, and AMD. The RPE is a post-mitotic, highly phagocytic epithelium that performs daily heterophagy of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) and constitutive macroautophagy, processes that converge on lysosomal degradation; failure at the lysosomal step produces lipofuscin accumulation, proteotoxic stress, and drusen formation that characterize AMD (Sinha et al., 2016). Lysosomal acidity, maintained by the vacuolar H^+-ATPase (V-ATPase), is essential for cathepsin activity and cargo hydrolysis; AMD-relevant lipofuscin bisretinoids such as A2E elevate lysosomal pH by inhibiting V-ATPase, impairing phagolysosomal digestion of POS and promoting a feed-forward cycle of oxidative stress and lysosomal dysfunction (Sinha et al., 2016). Lysosomal signaling couples to mTORC1 at the lysosomal surface via V-ATPase–Ragulator–Rag GTPase complexes, integrating luminal amino acids and energy status to regulate autophagy; in RPE, βA3/A1-crystallin (Cryba1) binds the V0 domain of V-ATPase and its loss produces mTORC1 hyperactivation, inefficient lysosomal clearance, and AMD-like pathology, functionally tying V-ATPase activity and mTORC1–TFEB signaling to disease (Sinha et al., 2016). Autophagy protects RPE from oxidative stress, and genetic or age-related declines in macroautophagy correlate with mitochondrial dysfunction, increased ROS, inflammasome activation, and lipofuscin accumulation in human AMD eyes and animal models (Sinha et al., 2016). These data establish that increasing autophagic flux and restoring lysosomal acidification are mechanistically central interventions for RPE protection in AMD.

The AMPK–mTOR–ULK1/TFEB axis: a mechanistic route to autophagy and lysosomal biogenesis. AMPK and mTOR are canonical, opposing regulators of autophagy: AMPK stimulates autophagy by activating ULK1 and inhibiting mTORC1 (including via TSC1/2–Rheb), whereas mTORC1 phosphorylates ULK1/Atg13 to block initiation and phosphorylates Atg14 to dampen nucleation (Wang et al., 2023). TFEB/TFE3 are master transcription factors of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy; mTORC1 phosphorylation retains TFEB in the cytosol, and mTORC1 inhibition permits TFEB nuclear translocation to activate the CLEAR network of lysosomal and autophagy genes (Wang et al., 2023). AMPK-dependent phosphorylation is required for TFEB/TFE3 transcriptional activation, and AMPK activation increases V-ATPase assembly and activity under glucose starvation, providing a direct link from energy stress to lysosomal acidification machinery (Boya et al., 2023). In RPE/AMD-relevant systems, TFEB activation increases expression of V-ATPase subunits (e.g., ATP6V0A1), lysosomal proteins (LAMP2), and cathepsins (CTSB), and reduces accumulation of POS-dependent lipofuscin-like granules; trehalose- or rapamycin-mediated TFEB activation enhances autophagic flux and decreases A2E/lipofuscin load and inflammatory markers in RPE models (Falcão et al., 2025). Human AMD RPE displays increased TFEB phosphorylation and reduced nuclear TFEB, together with decreased cathepsin levels, consistent with mTORC1-mediated suppression of lysosomal function; TFEB reactivation in Cryba1-deficient RPE rescues CLEAR gene expression and degradative capacity, supporting TFEB as a viable target for lysosomal rejuvenation (Falcão et al., 2025).

Mitochondria–lysosome crosstalk, S1P, and oxidative damage. Mitochondrial quality control via PINK1/PRKN-driven mitophagy and mitochondria-derived vesicles (MDVs) is coordinated with lysosomal function; AMPK acts bidirectionally to couple mitochondrial stress to lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy competence (Boya et al., 2023). In RPE and AMD models, mitochondrial dysfunction (mtDNA damage, impaired mitophagy) increases ROS and sensitizes cells to inflammasome activation and downstream damage; restoring lysosomal/mitochondrial clearance reduces oxidative injury (Boya et al., 2023). S1P signaling can induce mitochondrial biogenesis and thereby influence immunometabolic homeostasis; combined with AMPK–TFEB activation, S1P-related pathways could theoretically enhance both mitochondrial and lysosomal resilience in stressed RPE (Boya et al., 2023).

Inflammation, complement, and autophagy–lysosome cross-talk in AMD. Autophagy negatively regulates inflammasome activation, and complement/inflammasome axes contribute to RPE pathology in AMD; for example, complement C5a primes inflammasomes, and high-risk CFH Y402H RPE exhibit swollen lysosomes with cathepsin D leakage and increased lysosomal C5b-9 deposition (Kaarniranta et al., 2023; Falcão et al., 2025). Complement-related lysosomal stress provides a plausible injury modality that could be mitigated by lysosomal stabilization and increased autophagic flux; indeed, complement modulation can restore lysosomal function in a CFH Y402H RPE model (Boya et al., 2023). Because impaired lysosome/autophagy promotes secretory autophagy of inflammatory cargo, including HMGB1 and possibly amyloid species, enhancing lysosomal degradation should also reduce extracellular pro-inflammatory signals that propagate AMD pathology (Kaarniranta et al., 2023; Sinha et al., 2016).

What is known—and unknown—about fingolimod and the AMPK/TFEB–lysosome axis. In human cell models outside the eye, fingolimod can increase LC3B-II and alter Akt signaling (dephosphorylating Akt1 at Ser473), consistent with autophagy induction; yet the same studies report TFEB downregulation and induction of an inhibitory phosphorylation mark on TFEB (Ser211), with overall interpretations suggesting potential for stalling/saturation of autophagy under some conditions, and cell-type–dependent lysosomal enlargement and membrane permeabilization have also been described (Relic et al., 2021). Across the RPE/AMD literature summarized here, there is no direct demonstration that fingolimod or S1PR agonists activate AMPK, mobilize TFEB to the nucleus, increase V-ATPase–dependent lysosomal acidification, or enhance autophagic flux in RPE; nor are there data showing that fingolimod reduces complement-driven RPE injury in dry AMD models (Boya et al., 2023; Falcão et al., 2025). These are critical gaps that must be addressed experimentally before the proposed mechanism can be accepted (Jung et al., 2025; Relic et al., 2021).

Expected Effect  
Target engagement and proximal signaling in RPE. Because S1PR1 is expressed in RPE and appears to be the predominant S1P receptor subtype in this cell type, fingolimod (as FTY720-P) should engage RPE S1PRs at pharmacologically relevant concentrations if present in the subretinal/choroidal milieu, although receptor subtype balance and receptor down-modulation dynamics will influence outcomes (Sorenson et al., 2022; Gesualdo et al., 2021). In the hypothesized AMPK→TFEB pathway, proximal readouts would include increased AMPK phosphorylation/activation, decreased mTORC1 activity (e.g., phospho-S6K/4EBP1), and enhanced nuclear localization of TFEB/TFE3, followed by transcriptional upregulation of CLEAR genes (e.g., LAMP1/2, ATP6V0A1, CTSB/D) (Boya et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023; Falcão et al., 2025). Because S1PR signaling is pleiotropic, it is critical to test whether fingolimod in RPE shifts Akt and mTORC1 activities toward a TFEB-permissive state, as opposed to inducing the inhibitory TFEB Ser211 phosphorylation and autophagy stalling seen in some non-ocular cells (Relic et al., 2021).

Lysosomal acidification, degradative capacity, and autophagic flux. If fingolimod drives AMPK activation and TFEB nuclear translocation in RPE, predicted functional outcomes include increased V-ATPase assembly and activity (acidified lysosomes), higher cathepsin activity, and increased autophagic flux measurable by LC3-II turnover in the presence of bafilomycin or chloroquine, together with decreased steady-state p62/SQSTM1 under basal degradative conditions (Boya et al., 2023; Falcão et al., 2025). TFEB activation in RPE models reduces POS-dependent lipofuscin-like granule accumulation and increases lysosome number; pharmacologic TFEB induction (e.g., with trehalose) increases lysosomal biogenesis and autophagosome–lysosome fusion, so fingolimod engagement of this axis should phenocopy these protective changes if the postulated signaling is correct (Falcão et al., 2025). Restoration of lysosomal acidity would be expected to counteract A2E- and lipofuscin-induced V-ATPase inhibition, improving phagolysosomal degradation of POS and reducing waste buildup (Sinha et al., 2016).

Oxidative stress, mitochondrial homeostasis, and inflammasome/complement outputs. By increasing autophagic and mitophagic flux, and engaging TFEB-dependent antioxidant/clearance programs, the candidate mechanism should reduce mitochondrial ROS, stabilize mitochondrial biogenesis via AMPK–PGC-1 pathways, and decrease ROS-dependent priming of inflammasomes (Boya et al., 2023; Sinha et al., 2016). Autophagy mitigates inflammasome activation, and restoring lysosome function reduces the release of DAMPs and secretory autophagy cargo (e.g., HMGB1), which together would be expected to blunt complement pathway amplification in stressed RPE; complement deposition (C5b-9) within lysosomes, especially in CFH risk-allele RPE, may be reduced if lysosomal integrity is preserved and capacity is restored (Kaarniranta et al., 2023; Falcão et al., 2025). In aggregate, if fingolimod engages the AMPK–TFEB–lysosome axis in RPE as hypothesized, one would expect improvements in lysosomal pH, cathepsin activity (including CTSD), autophagic flux, reduced lipofuscin/A2E accumulation, lower mitochondrial ROS, and decreased inflammasome/complement readouts in RPE challenged with AMD-relevant stresses (oxidants, A2E, complement components) (Falcão et al., 2025; Sinha et al., 2016; Kaarniranta et al., 2023).

Caveats and contrary expectations. Fingolimod’s effects on TFEB and autophagy are cell- and context-dependent; in human synovial fibroblasts and HEK293 cells fingolimod induced TFEB Ser211 phosphorylation and TFEB downregulation despite LC3B-II accumulation, suggesting potential autophagy stalling and negative feedback on TFEB that could counter the desired lysosomal biogenesis in RPE (Relic et al., 2021). Moreover, S1P pathway manipulation can yield opposing outcomes in different compartments (e.g., anti-S1P strategies reduce ocular fibrosis/CNV), so nonselective S1PR modulation by fingolimod may not cleanly recapitulate the endothelial-protective, S1PR1-selective phenotypes observed with apoM/HDL–S1P delivery (Jung et al., 2025; Gupta et al., 2012). Finally, retinal safety signals—particularly macular edema—have been associated with fingolimod in clinical practice and would warrant stringent dose–exposure control and route optimization (e.g., local ocular delivery) in any AMD program (Mondal & Mandal, 2019).

Assay design implications. Given the mechanistic uncertainties, rigorous RPE-focused assays must be prioritized: (1) S1PR1/3/5 expression profiling in the chosen RPE system (primary human, iPSC-RPE) and receptor occupancy by fingolimod; (2) AMPK phosphorylation and mTORC1 readouts (p-AMPK, p-S6K, p-4EBP1) after fingolimod with and without S1PR antagonists; (3) TFEB/TFE3 nuclear translocation and CLEAR target induction; (4) lysosomal pH (ratiometric LysoSensor) and V-ATPase assembly/activity; (5) autophagic flux (LC3-II turnover ± lysosomal inhibitors; p62 dynamics); (6) cathepsin D/L activity assays; (7) oxidative stress resilience (mitoROS, mtDNA damage) and mitophagy markers; and (8) complement handling (C3 turnover, C5b-9 deposition) under CFH Y402H and amyloid/oxidant stress conditions (Falcão et al., 2025; Sinha et al., 2016; Falcão et al., 2025; Kaarniranta et al., 2023). Benchmarking to validated TFEB activators (e.g., trehalose) and lysosomal acidification restorers will contextualize fingolimod’s performance on the most disease-relevant axes (Falcão et al., 2025).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
- Strong disease–mechanism alignment: Dry AMD is characterized by impaired lysosomal acidification, defective autophagic clearance, and oxidative/complement stress in RPE; AMPK–mTOR–TFEB signaling and V-ATPase activity are validated nodal controllers of lysosomal competence in RPE and AMD models, providing clear mechanistic targets that, if engaged, should be disease-modifying (Sinha et al., 2016; Sinha et al., 2016; Falcão et al., 2025; Sinha et al., 2016).  
- On-target cellular expression: S1PR1 is present in RPE and is the predominant S1PR subtype, while RPE express SphK1/2, indicating the S1P/S1PR machinery required for pharmacologic modulation exists in the target cell (Sorenson et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023).  
- Prior ocular efficacy signals via S1PR modulation: ApoM/HDL–S1P→S1PR1 suppresses CNV and leakage; FTY720 reduces choroidal endothelial angiogenic behaviors and mitigates CNV; in diabetic retinopathy, fingolimod decreases VEGF signaling—together confirming relevant ocular pharmacology and providing translational scaffolding for retinal use (Jung et al., 2025; Sorenson et al., 2022; Gesualdo et al., 2021).  
- Immunometabolic rationale: S1P signaling can promote mitochondrial biogenesis, and AMPK–TFEB signaling coordinates mitochondrial–lysosomal crosstalk; a single agent engaging the S1P–AMPK–TFEB axis might simultaneously improve mitochondrial and lysosomal resilience in oxidatively stressed RPE (Boya et al., 2023; Boya et al., 2023).  
- Repurposing tractability: Extensive human exposure, known PK/PD, and manageable formulation chemistry enable accelerated translational planning compared to de novo entities (Mondal & Mandal, 2019).

Weaknesses and risks  
- Mechanistic gap in the target cell: There is currently no direct evidence that fingolimod activates AMPK or TFEB in RPE, increases autophagic flux, or acidifies lysosomes; likewise, no data show fingolimod reduces complement-driven injury in RPE/dry AMD models, and this is the central premise of the hypothesis (Jung et al., 2025; Boya et al., 2023; Falcão et al., 2025).  
- Context dependence and potential for adverse lysosomal effects: In human non-ocular cells, fingolimod induced inhibitory TFEB phosphorylation and autophagy stalling, and lysosomal enlargement/permeabilization has been reported in some contexts—opposite to the desired lysosomal restoration—raising the possibility of deleterious effects in RPE unless carefully titrated (Relic et al., 2021).  
- Receptor- and ligand-context complexity: Beneficial vascular phenotypes are associated with endothelial S1PR1 stimulation—often via chaperone-bound S1P—while nonselective S1PR modulation or anti-S1P strategies can also be beneficial (e.g., anti-fibrotic effects in AMD), implying that fingolimod’s mixed S1PR agonism and functional antagonism could yield off-target or counterproductive outcomes in the RPE/choroid (Jung et al., 2025; Gupta et al., 2012; Sorenson et al., 2022).  
- Retinal safety concerns: Fingolimod-associated macular edema is a documented adverse effect in humans, which is particularly concerning in a macular disease; any development plan must incorporate delivery strategies and exposure limits that mitigate this risk (Mondal & Mandal, 2019).  
- Evidence base skew: Much of the directly AMD-relevant S1P/S1PR ocular literature concerns neovascular endpoints (wet AMD/CNV) or endothelium, not atrophic RPE biology; the best lysosome/autophagy data are robust but are not linked to S1PR pharmacology, necessitating bridging studies to validate the specific drug–mechanism–disease chain (Jung et al., 2025; Falcão et al., 2025).

Go/no-go perspective and experimental priorities  
On balance, fingolimod is a mechanistically plausible, but currently unvalidated, repurposing candidate for dry AMD via the proposed S1PR→AMPK→TFEB→lysosome axis. A limited, mechanism-first preclinical package in human RPE models is warranted before considering in vivo efficacy work. The minimum data package to de-risk the hypothesis should include:  
- Demonstration that fingolimod engages S1PRs on human RPE and increases AMPK activity while suppressing mTORC1, with consequent TFEB/TFE3 nuclear translocation and CLEAR gene induction; side-by-side with a S1PR1-selective agonist and apoM/HDL-S1P as positive controls for S1PR1 biology (Sorenson et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Falcão et al., 2025; Jung et al., 2025).  
- Functional lysosomal rescue: increased V-ATPase assembly/activity, lower lysosomal pH, increased CTSD/CTSL activity, improved POS digestion, reduced A2E and lipofuscin-like autofluorescence under static and diurnal POS feeding paradigms (Sinha et al., 2016; Falcão et al., 2025).  
- Flux and mitochondrial outcomes: increased autophagic flux (LC3-II turnover, p62 dynamics), enhanced mitophagy under oxidant stress (PINK1/PRKN markers), and decreased mitoROS (Boya et al., 2023; Sinha et al., 2016).  
- Inflammation/complement endpoints: reduced IL1B release after MG-132/BafA challenges, lowered NLRP3 activation, decreased lysosomal C5b-9 deposition and improved lysosomal integrity in CFH Y402H iPSC-RPE exposed to complement (Piippo, 2019; Falcão et al., 2025).  
- Safety liabilities: absence of lysosomal membrane permeabilization, cathepsin leakage, or TFEB Ser211 inhibitory phosphorylation with autophagy stalling at therapeutic exposure in RPE, in contrast to adverse signatures reported in other cell types (Relic et al., 2021).

Depending on these in vitro outcomes, a focused in vivo test in a dry AMD-relevant model (e.g., NaIO3 injury or Cryba1 cKO) with intraocular delivery and a retinal safety assessment (OCT for edema, ERG, histology) would be the logical next step (Falcão et al., 2025; Sinha et al., 2016). If the AMPK–TFEB and lysosomal endpoints are not met, pivot to alternative S1PR strategies (S1PR1-selective agonists or apoM/HDL–S1P formulations) that have more consistent endothelial/retinal protective phenotypes may be warranted (Jung et al., 2025). If proximal signaling is partially engaged but lysosomal capacity remains limiting, combination approaches with validated TFEB activators (e.g., trehalose) or NRF2 activators that cross-reinforce lysosomal and antioxidant programs could be explored with attention to oncogenicity and cell specificity (Falcão et al., 2025).

Conclusion  
Fingolimod sits at the intersection of a compelling disease mechanism (lysosomal/autophagic failure in RPE), a plausible upstream target (S1PR1 in RPE), and a therapeutic class with ocular activity (S1PR modulators), but it lacks direct evidence for engaging the AMPK–TFEB–lysosomal biogenesis/acidification axis in RPE or for mitigating complement/oxidative injury in dry AMD models (Sorenson et al., 2022; Falcão et al., 2025; Jung et al., 2025). The strength of the lysosome/autophagy rationale and the existence of RPE S1PR1 expression support a mechanism-first exploration; however, cell-type–dependent fingolimod effects on TFEB and autophagy and the clinical risk of macular edema demand caution (Relic et al., 2021; Mondal & Mandal, 2019). Proceed only with a staged validation plan centered on RPE-intrinsic endpoints of AMPK/TFEB activation, lysosomal acidification, and complement/oxidative resilience; without those data, the hypothesis remains unsubstantiated for dry AMD, and alternative S1PR strategies or direct TFEB/lysosome-targeting interventions may be more tractable (Jung et al., 2025; Falcão et al., 2025).
