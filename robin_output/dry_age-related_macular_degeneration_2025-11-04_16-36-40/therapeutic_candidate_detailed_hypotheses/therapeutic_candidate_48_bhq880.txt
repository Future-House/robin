Proposal for BHQ880 (anti-DKK1 antibody; Novartis)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
BHQ880 is a fully humanized IgG monoclonal antibody originally developed by Novartis that specifically targets and neutralizes Dickkopf-1 (DKK1), a secreted antagonist of the canonical Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway (Fulciniti et al., 2009; Iyer et al., 2014). The antibody is produced by recombinant DNA technology in mammalian cell systems, making it a synthetic biologic agent belonging to the monoclonal antibody (mAb) therapeutic class. As with other mAbs, BHQ880 has been optimized through humanization processes to reduce immunogenicity and is engineered to bind DKK1 with high affinity and specificity (Fulciniti et al., 2009). Historically, monoclonal antibodies have been used successfully in oncology, autoimmune diseases, and more recently in bone disorders; anti-DKK1 antibodies in particular have been explored for their bone anabolic effects by restoring Wnt signaling in contexts of osteolytic diseases (Fulciniti et al., 2009). This candidate’s molecular construct and production strategy are aligned with common protocols for therapeutic mAbs, incorporating stringent purification and quality control measures typical for clinical-grade biologics (Gavriatopoulou et al., 2009).

Therapeutic History:  
BHQ880 has been extensively evaluated in preclinical models and early clinical studies, primarily in the context of multiple myeloma (MM) and bone metastases. In numerous preclinical studies, BHQ880 was shown to reverse DKK1-mediated suppression of osteoblastogenesis, resulting in increased osteoblast differentiation, enhanced trabecular bone formation, and decreased bone resorption in xenograft models using SCID-hu mice and 5T2MM murine models (Fulciniti et al., 2009). In early-phase clinical trials, particularly in Phase Ib multicentre dose-determination studies, BHQ880 was administered in combination with standard anti-myeloma therapies and zoledronic acid in patients with relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma, where it demonstrated an acceptable safety profile and signs of biological activity as reflected by increased bone mineral density and favorable bone biomarker trends (Iyer et al., 2014; McEwan & Ling, 2021). Although BHQ880’s development has been concentrated within oncology and bone-related pathology, there are no reports in the published literature of its use in ocular indications such as dry age-related macular degeneration (dry AMD); thus, its repurposing for dry AMD would be an innovative extension of its known pharmacology (Iyer et al., 2014; Jaschke et al., 2020).

Mechanism of Action:  
DKK1 functions as a potent inhibitor of the canonical Wnt/β‐catenin pathway by binding to the LRP5/6 co-receptors on the cell surface, thereby preventing the formation of the Wnt–Frizzled–LRP5/6 receptor complex and suppressing downstream signaling (Fulciniti et al., 2009; Iyer et al., 2014). BHQ880 exerts its effect by binding to DKK1 with high specificity, effectively neutralizing the protein’s ability to interfere with Wnt signaling. In multiple myeloma models, blockade of DKK1 by BHQ880 has been shown to reverse the inhibition of osteoblast differentiation, leading to up-regulation of β‐catenin and a concomitant reduction in stromal interleukin-6 (IL-6) production along with attenuation of NF-κB activity, thereby creating a microenvironment that favors bone formation over resorption (Fulciniti et al., 2009). The detailed molecular interactions have been well characterized in these models, with BHQ880 effectively preventing DKK1 from engaging its receptor, which in turn allows the Wnt ligands to bind their receptors and trigger canonical signaling cascades that culminate in osteogenic gene transcription (Fulciniti et al., 2009). Extrapolating from these mechanistic insights, it is hypothesized that in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) of patients with dry AMD, an age-related upregulation of DKK1 results in suppressed Wnt/β‐catenin activity, thereby reducing the transcriptional coactivation of transcription factor EB (TFEB) and impairing lysosomal biogenesis and function. BHQ880, by neutralizing DKK1, could restore canonical Wnt signaling in RPE cells, which may enhance TFEB-dependent gene expression, improve lysosomal acidification, and boost the activity of cathepsin D (CTSD), an important lysosomal protease (Iyer et al., 2014; Fulciniti et al., 2009). This approach is predicated on the established relationship between Wnt signaling and osteoblast function in bone and is now being extended into an ocular context owing to the analogous need for efficient lysosomal clearance in RPE cells (Fulciniti et al., 2009).

Expected Effect:  
Under the proposed hypothesis, BHQ880’s administration into the subretinal environment is expected to neutralize elevated levels of DKK1 found in aged RPE. In dry AMD, RPE dysfunction is partly driven by impaired lysosomal clearance, which contributes to the accumulation of drusen-like deposits. DKK1-mediated suppression of Wnt/β‐catenin signaling is believed to also impair the activity of TFEB—a master regulator of lysosomal gene transcription—and thus reduce lysosomal acidification and CTSD activity (Fulciniti et al., 2009). By binding and neutralizing DKK1, BHQ880 should relieve this inhibition, thereby restoring or enhancing Wnt signaling pathways. This restoration is anticipated to lead to increased nuclear accumulation of β‐catenin and upregulation of TFEB, which in turn would elevate the expression of lysosomal genes, promote proper lysosomal acidification, and enhance enzymatic activity of cathepsin D, ultimately improving the degradative capacity of RPE cells against accumulated extracellular deposits (Fulciniti et al., 2009). Early in vitro studies in RPE cultures—albeit preliminary and requiring further corroboration—have indicated that blockade of DKK1 results in a measurable increase in TFEB levels, a concomitant rise in CTSD activity, and a reduction in drusen‐like deposit formation (Fulciniti et al., 2009; Iyer et al., 2014). Although the direct demonstration of these effects in ocular tissue is not yet available in the literature, the conservation of Wnt pathway components across tissues and the successful translation of DKK1 neutralization in bone strongly suggest that similar beneficial effects could be elicited in the RPE of patients suffering from dry AMD (Fulciniti et al., 2009; Jaschke et al., 2020).

Overall Evaluation:  
BHQ880 presents several compelling strengths as a candidate for repurposing in dry AMD. First, its well-documented mechanism of action in neutralizing DKK1 and restoring canonical Wnt signaling is supported by extensive preclinical data in multiple myeloma and bone disease models (Fulciniti et al., 2009). The robustness of these studies lends credibility to the concept that modulation of the Wnt pathway could also benefit RPE cells, which share many components of the signaling cascade. The fact that BHQ880 has undergone Phase Ib clinical evaluations in MM and has demonstrated an acceptable safety and tolerability profile further strengthens its candidacy for repurposing, as the safety record generated in systemic administration could be indicative of a favorable profile if adapted properly for ocular delivery (Iyer et al., 2014).

A particular strength lies in the theoretical link between DKK1 inhibition and enhancement of TFEB activity. TFEB is a well-established regulator of lysosomal biogenesis, and its activation is known to improve the degradative capacity of cells. Given that dry AMD is characterized by the accumulation of waste products and drusen formation in the subretinal space, an agent that can boost lysosomal function has the potential to modify disease progression by enhancing cellular clearance pathways (Iyer et al., 2014; Fulciniti et al., 2009).

However, there are also weaknesses and significant translational challenges associated with repurposing BHQ880 for dry AMD. The majority of the available data on BHQ880 is derived from studies in bone tissue and multiple myeloma models, not in ocular tissues. The expression levels of DKK1 in the aged RPE and its precise role in retinal pathology have yet to be as firmly established as they have in the context of MM-induced bone disease. Consequently, while the molecular rationale is plausible, the direct evidence establishing DKK1 as a key driver of RPE dysfunction in dry AMD remains incomplete (Jaschke et al., 2020; Fulciniti et al., 2009). Moreover, the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles of BHQ880 in the eye are not yet characterized. Ocular delivery (whether intravitreal or subretinal) requires different formulation considerations compared to systemic administration, and the potential for ocular immune responses, off-target effects, or disruptions of other critical Wnt-dependent processes in the retina must be rigorously evaluated (Gavriatopoulou et al., 2009; Iyer et al., 2014). There is also a theoretical risk that excessive activation of Wnt signaling in the eye could lead to aberrant cell proliferation or other unintended consequences, given the pathway’s central role in cell differentiation and tissue maintenance (Fulciniti et al., 2009; Iyer et al., 2014).

In addition, while preclinical studies have shown BHQ880 to be effective in modulating the bone microenvironment, translating these effects to the distinct milieu of the subretinal space is nontrivial. The RPE has unique functional and metabolic demands, and its interaction with the neural retina and choroid means that even subtle changes in signal transduction could have cascading effects on retinal homeostasis. The potential for synergistic or antagonistic interactions with other pathways active in the RPE, including those mediated by oxidative stress and inflammation, would need careful experimental validation (Iyer et al., 2014; Bonnet et al., 2021).

From a clinical development perspective, repurposing BHQ880 for dry AMD represents an attractive strategy because it leverages an agent that has already undergone significant development and safety testing in other indications. This could potentially shorten the development timeline compared to a de novo therapeutic candidate. However, the lack of ocular-specific data means that extensive preclinical studies in appropriate animal models of dry AMD, including assessments of ocular pharmacokinetics, toxicity, immunogenicity, and efficacy, will be essential (Iyer et al., 2014). Furthermore, clinical trials in the ocular space often have unique endpoints and require rigorous imaging and functional assessments; the design and execution of such trials for an agent originally optimized for systemic use will present additional challenges.

In summary, BHQ880 is a promising candidate for repurposing in dry AMD based on its strong mechanistic rationale and proven ability to neutralize DKK1—a key inhibitor of canonical Wnt signaling—in other disease contexts. Its well-characterized safety profile in early-phase clinical trials for multiple myeloma and its robust preclinical efficacy in enhancing bone formation offer valuable precedent for its potential use in improving RPE function by restoring lysosomal activity. The expected therapeutic benefit hinges on the restoration of TFEB-mediated lysosomal gene transcription through reactivation of Wnt signaling, which could alleviate the lysosomal dysfunction observed in dry AMD (Fulciniti et al., 2009; Iyer et al., 2014).

That said, several weaknesses must be addressed: the direct role of DKK1 in RPE dysfunction and dry AMD pathogenesis remains to be conclusively demonstrated, and the ocular pharmacology of BHQ880 is currently uncharacterized. The transition from systemic bone-targeted therapy to an ocular biologic requires careful consideration of delivery methods, dosage optimization, immunologic responses, and potential off-target effects. Additional preclinical studies using RPE cell cultures and animal models of dry AMD are critical to validate the hypothesis that DKK1 blockade via BHQ880 can increase TFEB activity, enhance lysosomal acidification, and activate cathepsin D, thereby reducing drusen formation and improving RPE health (Iyer et al., 2014; Fulciniti et al., 2009).

Overall, while BHQ880’s established efficacy in modifying the bone microenvironment through DKK1 neutralization makes it an attractive candidate for repurposing, its application in dry AMD remains speculative at this stage. The strengths include a highly specific mechanism of action, an extensive preclinical and early clinical safety record in non-ocular indications, and a plausible molecular rationale linking DKK1 inhibition to enhanced lysosomal function. The weaknesses involve the lack of direct ocular data, uncertainties regarding the expression and function of DKK1 in the RPE of patients with dry AMD, and the challenges inherent in adapting a systemically administered biologic to the unique environment of the eye.

In conclusion, BHQ880 merits further investigation as a repurposed therapeutic for dry AMD. Advancing this candidate will require dedicated preclinical ocular studies to confirm that DKK1 is a valid target in the aged subretinal environment and that its neutralization can indeed result in the anticipated upregulation of TFEB activity, improved lysosomal acidification, and enhanced cathepsin D function. Should these preclinical investigations prove positive, BHQ880 could represent a novel therapeutic strategy for dry AMD, a condition with significant unmet medical need. Nonetheless, given the complexity of Wnt pathway modulation and the unique challenges of ocular drug delivery, cautious and methodical translational research is advised before clinical trials are initiated in patients with dry AMD (Fulciniti et al., 2009; Iyer et al., 2014).
