Proposal for GSK126 (Selleckchem S7061)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
GSK126 is a small‐molecule, synthetic compound originally developed as a selective inhibitor of the enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) enzyme. As a S-adenosyl-L-methionine (SAM)-competitive inhibitor, GSK126 belongs to the class of epigenetic modulators that work by impairing the catalytic activity of EZH2, a methyltransferase that deposits H3K27me3 repressive marks on chromatin. These compounds were discovered and optimized initially for use in cancer, where aberrant EZH2 activity drives tumor progression through silencing of tumor suppressor genes. The chemical structure of GSK126 and its mode of synthesis have been well characterized in the oncology literature, and the compound has been extensively used in preclinical models and early phase clinical studies to assess its ability to relieve polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2)-mediated gene silencing (Shu et al., 2023, pp. 16–17). As such, GSK126 represents a prototypical member of a therapeutic class that has been used predominantly in oncology, with recent efforts to repurpose its epigenetic modulatory properties to other diseases where aberrant gene repression plays a role.

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, the development of EZH2 inhibitors such as GSK126 has been focused predominantly on oncological applications. Several clinical trials have evaluated EZH2 inhibitors in hematologic malignancies and solid tumors, where deregulated EZH2 activity supports cancer cell proliferation and survival. For example, while related compounds (e.g., GSK2816126) were investigated in dose escalation studies in patients with hematologic malignancies and solid tumors, GSK126 itself has been studied preclinically and in clinical contexts for its anti-proliferative effects in cancer models (GlaxoSmithKline, 2014). In addition, high-content screening initiatives in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells have identified EZH2 inhibitors among a range of epigenetic drug candidates, with studies reporting that GSK126 can upregulate p16 expression in RPE models as part of broader efforts to identify modulators of senescence (Coryell, 2019, pp. 48–53). However, there is no published record to date of GSK126 being deployed specifically for the treatment of dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) or for directly targeting lysosomal dysfunction in ocular tissues. Open Targets Platform data reveal a very weak association between EZH2 and AMD, with minimal documented evidence tying EZH2 activity directly to disease pathogenesis in the retina (Buniello et al., 2025). Thus, while extensive preclinical and clinical data substantiate the use of EZH2 inhibitors in cancer, their specific application to dry AMD remains an emerging hypothesis that has not yet been validated either in biochemical in vitro studies or in in vivo ocular models.

Mechanism of Action:  
The primary mechanism of action of GSK126 is based on its inhibition of EZH2 activity. EZH2 is the catalytic subunit of the polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) that methylates lysine 27 on histone H3 (H3K27me3), a modification generally associated with transcriptional silencing of target genes. By competitively binding to the SAM-binding pocket of EZH2, GSK126 prevents the addition of methyl groups to H3K27, thereby reducing the global levels of H3K27me3 and leading to a derepression of genes normally silenced by this epigenetic mark (Shu et al., 2023, pp. 16–17). In cancer cells, this mechanism leads to reactivation of tumor suppressor genes such as p16, and similar findings have been observed in epigenetic drug screens using RPE cells where treatment with GSK126 resulted in upregulation of p16 expression (Coryell, 2019, pp. 48–53).

The repurposing hypothesis for dry AMD suggests that similar chromatin-level mechanisms could be exploited in RPE cells to relieve the suppression of the CLEAR (Coordinated Lysosomal Expression and Regulation) network, a collection of genes regulated by the master transcription factor TFEB responsible for lysosomal biogenesis and function. Under stress conditions, the epigenetic repression mediated by EZH2 via H3K27me3 may contribute to the diminished expression of lysosomal genes, including those coding for components of the acidification machinery such as vacuolar ATPase (V-ATPase) subunits and lysosomal proteases like cathepsin D (Abokyi & Tse, 2025, pp. 2–3; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7). The rationale is that inhibition of EZH2 with GSK126 will alleviate H3K27me3 deposition at promoters of lysosomal genes, allowing TFEB to more effectively activate the transcription of these CLEAR genes. In cancer models, related observations have been made where EZH2 inhibition leads to increased expression of V-ATPase subunits and CTSD (cathepsin D) as well as enhancement of autophagy (Coryell, 2019, pp. 48–53; Shu et al., 2023, pp. 16–17, 22–23). Although most of these studies have been conducted in cancer or other non-ocular cell types, the underlying biochemical interactions between EZH2, histone modifications, and gene expression remain consistent and serve as the basis for the proposed mechanism in RPE cells. In summary, by reducing H3K27me3-mediated repression, GSK126 is expected to derepress genes that are essential for maintaining proper lysosomal acidification, proteolysis, and overall cell homeostasis, thereby addressing one potential pathogenic mechanism in dry AMD.

Expected Effect:  
The therapeutic hypothesis posits that in the context of dry AMD, RPE cells suffer from lysosomal dysfunction—manifested by aberrant acidification and insufficient activity of lysosomal proteases such as cathepsin D—that contributes to the accumulation of toxic protein aggregates and cellular debris. TFEB, a transcription factor known to drive the expression of the CLEAR network, plays a critical role in regulating lysosomal biogenesis and function. Notably, in aging retina and AMD, the activation of TFEB-mediated gene expression is impaired, leading to reduced levels of essential lysosomal enzymes and V-ATPase subunits, as well as the accumulation of lipofuscin-like material (Abokyi & Tse, 2025, pp. 9–10). The proposed effect of GSK126 in RPE cells is to inhibit EZH2 and relieve the epigenetic repression imposed by H3K27me3 at lysosomal gene promoters. This derepression should allow TFEB to more robustly activate the transcription of its target genes, thereby enhancing the expression of V-ATPase subunits and cathepsin D. Enhanced V-ATPase expression will improve lysosomal acidification—a key determinant of lysosome function—while increased levels of cathepsin D will bolster the degradative capacity of the lysosome. In other cellular contexts, such as in certain cancer cell lines, EZH2 inhibition has been shown to upregulate autophagy-related genes, including those involved in lysosomal function, which supports the plausibility of this mechanism in RPE cells (Shu et al., 2023, pp. 16–17; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 13–15).

Given that RPE cells are highly metabolically active and rely on efficient lysosomal degradation for the phagocytosis and recycling of photoreceptor outer segments, the expected outcome of GSK126 treatment would be an improvement in lysosomal clearance. This improvement could translate into a reduction of the toxic protein aggregates and lipofuscin accumulation that are hallmarks of dry AMD pathology. In vitro, one would anticipate observing an increase in TFEB nuclear translocation, upregulation of CLEAR network genes, enhanced lysosomal acidification (as measured by V-ATPase activity) and restored cathepsin D levels in treated RPE cells, thereby validating the hypothesized mechanism (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 17–18; Abokyi & Tse, 2025, pp. 3–4). In vivo, similar effects might lead to improved RPE function and a potentially slower progression of dry AMD. However, it is important to note that while there is evidence for the role of EZH2 in regulating p16 and autophagy in various models, direct evidence linking GSK126 treatment to restored lysosomal gene networks in RPE cells remains to be generated.

Overall Evaluation:  
The proposal to repurpose GSK126 for dry AMD represents an innovative application of epigenetic therapy beyond its established role in oncology. One of the major strengths of this candidate is the robust preclinical and clinical safety data that have been accumulated in cancer studies. The availability of dosing and pharmacokinetic profiles from oncology clinical trials offers a significant advantage in terms of de-risking the development timeline for repurposing efforts (GlaxoSmithKline, 2014; ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.). Moreover, the mechanistic rationale is scientifically sound: EZH2 is a well-characterized repressor of gene transcription via H3K27me3, and its inhibition could indeed lead to the derepression of genes critical for lysosomal maintenance (Abokyi & Tse, 2025, pp. 2–3; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7).

However, there are several weaknesses and uncertainties that must be addressed. First, despite the compelling mechanistic hypothesis, there is a paucity of direct evidence demonstrating that EZH2 inhibition in RPE cells leads to upregulation of lysosomal genes, specifically those involved in the acidification machinery and cathepsin D expression (Coryell, 2019, pp. 48–53). Second, comprehensive target-disease association analyses using platforms such as Open Targets reveal a very weak connection between EZH2 and AMD, which raises the possibility that EZH2’s role in the pathogenesis of dry AMD may be limited, or that other compensatory pathways might diminish the impact of EZH2 inhibition in the retinal environment (Buniello et al., 2025). Third, while studies in cancer cell lines have indicated that EZH2 inhibition can upregulate V-ATPase subunits and lysosomal enzyme expression, cancer cells and RPE cells exhibit markedly different epigenetic landscapes and stress responses; therefore, extrapolation from oncology models to the RPE must be approached with caution (Shu et al., 2023, pp. 20–21, 22–23).

Furthermore, targeting epigenetic regulators always carries the risk of off-target effects since these enzymes regulate the expression of a broad spectrum of genes. In the context of the retina—an organ with complex cellular interdependencies—the potential for unintended consequences on RPE differentiation, survival, or interactions with photoreceptors must be thoroughly evaluated prior to clinical translation (Abokyi & Tse, 2025, pp. 7–8; Ferret, 2024, pp. 171–173).

Another point of consideration is the durability of the epigenetic modification induced by EZH2 inhibition. While durable enhancement of lysosomal capacity through chromatin-level changes is an attractive prospect, the reversibility and specificity of these modifications in the context of chronic degenerative diseases like dry AMD remain uncertain. It will be crucial to determine whether the epigenetic changes induced by GSK126 are sustained over time in RPE cells and whether they translate to meaningful improvements in visual function or retinal integrity in preclinical models.

From a translational perspective, the repurposing strategy for GSK126 should include comprehensive in vitro studies using RPE cell lines and primary cultures, with endpoints that directly assess lysosomal acidification, cathepsin D activity, and clearance of photoreceptor outer segment debris. Furthermore, in vivo studies in animal models of dry AMD are needed to evaluate whether EZH2 inhibition can restore lysosomal function and slow disease progression under physiologically relevant conditions (Abokyi & Tse, 2025, pp. 2–3, 9–10). Comparative studies with other epigenetic modulators and TFEB activators could also provide context regarding the relative efficacy of GSK126 in reactivating the CLEAR network.

In conclusion, the repurposing of GSK126 for dry AMD is a promising, yet exploratory, approach that leverages a clear mechanistic hypothesis: by inhibiting EZH2, GSK126 may relieve repressive histone marks at lysosomal gene promoters, thereby promoting TFEB-driven transcription of genes essential for lysosomal acidification and proteolytic activity. This approach could conceivably ameliorate the lysosomal defects observed in RPE cells in dry AMD. The strengths of this candidate lie in its well-defined molecular target, its established clinical safety profile in oncology studies, and the potential for a durable, chromatin-level correction of lysosomal dysfunction. However, significant challenges remain, including the need for direct evidence linking EZH2 inhibition to enhancements in the CLEAR gene network within RPE cells, the potential off-target effects inherent to epigenetic therapies, and the overall weak association between EZH2 and AMD pathogenesis as indicated by current target-disease databases.

Given these considerations, while the mechanistic rationale is robust and the repurposing strategy innovative, the current level of evidence is insufficient for immediate clinical application. It is imperative that future research generate direct preclinical data in ocular models of dry AMD to confirm that GSK126 can, in fact, restore lysosomal function in RPE cells via TFEB activation and increased cathepsin D and V-ATPase expression. Further investigation should also focus on clarifying the broader gene expression changes induced by GSK126 in RPE cells to ensure that the desired effects on lysosomal pathways are achieved without detrimental off-target consequences.

The forthcoming research should aim to delineate the dose-response relationships, optimize delivery to the ocular compartment, and ensure that sustained improvements in lysosomal capacity correlate with clinical endpoints relevant to AMD progression (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 13–15; Sharif, 2015, pp. 68–72; Ferret, 2024, pp. 103–106).

In summary, while the repurposing of GSK126 for dry AMD is conceptually attractive and supported by a reasonable mechanistic framework, the endeavor is in its nascent stages. The existing literature does not yet provide direct evidence that GSK126 elicits the specific lysosomal benefits hypothesized in RPE cells. As such, this candidate represents a high-risk, high-reward opportunity that merits further in-depth investigation through rigorous preclinical studies, with a view toward eventual clinical translation if the mechanistic hypotheses are confirmed (Coryell, 2019, pp. 48–53; Sharif, 2015, pp. 68–72).
