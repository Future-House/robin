Proposal for Urolithin A (Sigma SML1791)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Urolithin A is a naturally derived small molecule that originates from the gut microbial metabolism of dietary polyphenols, particularly ellagitannins and ellagic acid, which are abundant in fruits such as pomegranates, berries, and nuts (Djedjibegović et al., 2020; Heilman et al., 2017). Unlike synthetic drug candidates, Urolithin A is generated endogenously following dietary intake, a process that relies on a healthy gut microbiome to convert these precursors into bioactive urolithins. This compound belongs to the broader class of nutraceuticals and microbiota‐derived metabolites that have been widely investigated for their capacity to modulate mitochondrial function and cellular homeostasis (Djedjibegović et al., 2020; Jayatunga et al., 2021). These compounds, which have historically been explored mostly within the context of nutritional interventions and age-related muscle decline, are now being repurposed as potential drugs for other degenerative conditions. Urolithin A, in particular, has attracted significant attention due to its ability to enhance mitochondrial quality control through the activation of pathways such as PINK1/Parkin and modulation of autophagy-related mechanisms. Its synthesis in the human body, as well as its isolation from natural sources for research-grade applications such as Sigma SML1791, underscores its dual status as both a naturally occurring metabolite and a drug candidate with therapeutic potential across a variety of disease models (Jayatunga et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2025). Moreover, as a member of this emerging class, Urolithin A has been shown to be well tolerated, which gives it an advantage over many synthetic compounds that may pose safety concerns due to their off-target toxicities (Wang & Yu, 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). The natural origin and well-characterized safety profile of Urolithin A lend support to its development as a repurposed therapeutic agent, particularly for diseases like dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD), where mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired lysosomal clearance play central roles.

Therapeutic History:  
The therapeutic history of Urolithin A spans several preclinical and clinical studies that have established its safety, bioavailability, and efficacy in improving mitochondrial function. In numerous animal studies, Urolithin A has demonstrated a high no‐observed‐adverse‐effect level (NOAEL), with doses in the range of several thousand mg/kg demonstrating a remarkable safety margin (Djedjibegović et al., 2020). Early-phase clinical trials, including randomized placebo-controlled studies in healthy elderly subjects, have corroborated its safety; these trials reported no serious adverse events even at doses as high as 2000 mg, and significant improvements were observed in mitochondrial gene expression and muscle function (Jayatunga et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2025). This safety profile has been further supported by work that shows Urolithin A’s favorable pharmacokinetic properties. For instance, following oral administration, Urolithin A is rapidly absorbed, with peak plasma concentrations reached around 6 hours post-dose and a half-life of approximately 17–22 hours for the compound and its glucuronide conjugates (Djedjibegović et al., 2020; Raimundo et al., 2021). Its systemic bioavailability is advantageous when considering repurposing for systemic degenerative conditions such as AMD.

Historically, the focus of Urolithin A research has been on its role in mitigating age-associated muscle decline by improving mitochondrial quality, a property that has been linked to its induction of mitophagy, which is the selective removal of damaged mitochondria (Jayatunga et al., 2021; Singh et al., 2019). However, growing evidence from both in vitro studies and animal models indicates that Urolithin A’s benefits extend to other tissues, particularly those that are highly dependent on mitochondrial health such as the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) in the eye (Hyttinen et al., 2018; Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024). Although direct clinical studies using Urolithin A for dry AMD are still pending, the preclinical data suggest that its ability to improve mitochondrial function by promoting mitophagy and lysosomal clearance mechanisms could be beneficial in counteracting RPE degeneration, a hallmark of dry AMD. The therapeutic candidate thus finds support from both its legacy in muscle studies and emerging evidence in neurodegenerative disease models, including studies related to Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease, where mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress are central themes (Jayatunga et al., 2021; Skeie et al., 2021). This therapeutic history, spanning several decades of research on mitochondrial modulators, positions Urolithin A as a promising candidate for repurposing toward diseases that involve similar cellular and molecular derangements.

Mechanism of Action:  
The mechanism of action of Urolithin A is multifaceted and central to its potential as a therapeutic agent for dry AMD. At a molecular level, Urolithin A functions primarily as a mitophagy enhancer. In cells experiencing mitochondrial damage or oxidative stress, the kinase PINK1 accumulates on the outer mitochondrial membrane, thereby recruiting the E3 ubiquitin ligase Parkin; together, they tag dysfunctional mitochondria for autophagic degradation (Borsky et al., 2025; Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024). This PINK1/Parkin-mediated pathway is essential for maintaining mitochondrial quality and cellular energy metabolism, particularly in high-energy demanding tissues such as the RPE where mitochondria are crucial for photoreceptor support (Hyttinen et al., 2018; Borsky et al., 2025). In addition to inducing mitophagy through PINK1/Parkin signaling, Urolithin A has been shown to activate AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), a key energy sensor in cells. AMPK activation leads to the phosphorylation and subsequent nuclear translocation of transcription factor EB (TFEB), which acts as a master regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy (Zhang et al., 2025; Borsky et al., 2025). Once translocated into the nucleus, TFEB upregulates the expression of genes encoding lysosomal proteins such as LAMP1 and cathepsin D (CTSD), thereby enhancing the capacity of cells to clear autophagic cargo through improved lysosomal degradation (D’Amico et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2025).

The biochemical interactions involved in these processes are well supported by in vitro data showing that Urolithin A not only reduces mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) but also ameliorates the disruption of autophagic flux that is often observed under oxidative stress conditions (Djedjibegović et al., 2020; Jayatunga et al., 2021). This reduction of oxidative stress is particularly relevant for the RPE because elevated oxidative damage is a significant contributor to cellular senescence and apoptosis in patients with dry AMD. Furthermore, Urolithin A has been noted to induce p62-dependent lysophagy, a specialized form of autophagy that targets lysosomal components for recycling and repair, ensuring that even when conventional lysosomal biogenesis is impaired, cellular proteostasis can be maintained (Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024). The dual activation of mitophagy and lysosomal biogenesis creates a synergistic effect, where damaged mitochondria are efficiently recognized, isolated, and degraded, and the lysosomal compartment is concurrently replenished with new, fully functional enzymes, ensuring an optimal degradative environment (Auguste, 2022; Borsky et al., 2025). Moreover, studies have shown that Urolithin A can favorably influence other signaling pathways that contribute to mitochondrial biogenesis, such as the SIRT1/PGC-1α axis, further supporting its role in restoring mitochondrial homeostasis (Borsky et al., 2025; D’Amico et al., 2021). In summary, Urolithin A’s multifaceted mechanism—which encompasses the induction of PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, activation of AMPK–TFEB signaling to enhance lysosomal biogenesis, and reduction of oxidative stress—forms a robust foundation for its proposed therapeutic application in the treatment of dry AMD.

Expected Effect:  
The therapeutic hypothesis for Urolithin A in dry AMD rests on its ability to restore mitochondrial quality and enhance lysosomal clearance in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). In the proposed assay, Urolithin A is expected to activate the PINK1/Parkin pathway in RPE cells, thus facilitating the selective removal of damaged mitochondria that contribute to oxidative stress and cellular dysfunction (Borsky et al., 2025). Concurrently, through the activation of AMPK, Urolithin A is anticipated to drive the nuclear translocation of TFEB, thereby upregulating lysosomal genes such as LAMP1 and cathepsin D. This upregulation is expected to enhance lysosomal acidification, an essential component for the proper degradation of autophagic cargo, including damaged mitochondria and photoreceptor outer segments (POS) that accumulate with age (Zhang et al., 2025; Borsky et al., 2025).

In oxidative stress RPE models, where mitochondrial ROS levels are elevated due to chronic insult, Urolithin A is projected to reduce ROS production by promoting the clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria, thereby mitigating the cascade of oxidative damage (Djedjibegović et al., 2020; Jayatunga et al., 2021). This is expected to result in the stabilization of mitochondrial membrane potential and the maintenance of energy homeostasis, both of which are crucial for the survival of RPE cells under stress. Additionally, the enhanced lysosomal function driven by TFEB activation is likely to lead to increased activity of cathepsin D, a lysosomal protease that plays a pivotal role in degrading autophagosomal substrates and in processing debris from phagocytosis of POS (D’Amico et al., 2021; Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024). The net effect of these actions would be a reduction in cellular stress markers, improved cellular clearance, and preservation of RPE cell integrity, which are fundamental in slowing down the progression of dry AMD.

The expected pharmacological outcomes are supported by prior studies demonstrating that Urolithin A can modulate key proteins known to be expressed in RPE cells. For instance, PINK1 is well documented to be present in RPE cells and plays an essential role in initiating mitophagy in response to mitochondrial damage (Hyttinen et al., 2018; García‐Villalba et al., 2020). Similarly, proteins such as LAMP1 and CTSD are integral to the function of the lysosomal system in RPE cells, and their upregulation has been associated with improved cellular clearance and resistance to stress-induced apoptosis (Zhang et al., 2025; Borsky et al., 2025). Thus, by simultaneously targeting both the mitophagic and lysosomal arms of the cellular clearance machinery, Urolithin A is expected to exert a comprehensive protective effect in the diseased state. This integrative approach is particularly relevant in dry AMD, wherein the accumulation of damaged cellular components and impaired lysosomal degradation are key pathological features (Makarov & Korkotian, 2023; Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024). Ultimately, the anticipated effects of Urolithin A administration include enhanced mitochondrial turnover, reduced oxidative stress, reinvigorated lysosomal function, and overall RPE cell survival, all of which are envisioned to culminate in a slowing or possible arrest of the degenerative processes underlying dry AMD.

Overall Evaluation:  
In evaluating Urolithin A as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD, several strengths emerge that underscore its potential while also highlighting critical areas that warrant further investigation. One of the most significant strengths of Urolithin A is its well-established safety profile. Preclinical toxicology studies have demonstrated that Urolithin A has a high NOAEL, and early-phase human clinical trials have shown that it is effectively tolerated at doses ranging from 250 to 2000 mg without significant adverse events (Jayatunga et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2025). This establishes a favorable risk–benefit ratio that is particularly advantageous when considering the chronic administration likely required for a degenerative condition such as dry AMD. Additionally, its oral bioavailability and favorable pharmacokinetic parameters, including a consistent half-life and effective systemic distribution, further support its potential for use as a repurposed drug (Zhang et al., 2025; Raimundo et al., 2021).

Mechanistically, Urolithin A addresses two core pathological processes implicated in dry AMD: mitochondrial dysfunction and lysosomal insufficiency. Its ability to trigger the PINK1/Parkin pathway ensures that damaged mitochondria are selectively removed, thereby reducing mitochondrial ROS production and mitigating oxidative injury—a process that is particularly beneficial in the energy-intensive environment of the RPE (Borsky et al., 2025). Simultaneously, the activation of AMPK and subsequent nuclear translocation of TFEB primes the lysosomal system for enhanced biogenesis and improved degradative capacity, thus tackling the accumulation of cellular debris and phagocytosed POS that are hallmarks of AMD pathology (Zhang et al., 2025; Borsky et al., 2025).

From a translational perspective, the therapeutic history of Urolithin A provides a strong foundation for its repurposing. Although much of the direct evidence pertains to skeletal muscle and neurodegenerative models, the underlying mechanisms—mitochondrial clearance and autophagic enhancement—are universally relevant to cellular systems burdened by degenerative stress, including the RPE (Hyttinen et al., 2018; Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024). However, a notable limitation remains in the current literature: there is a paucity of direct experimental data specifically utilizing RPE models or clinical cohorts with dry AMD. Although the extrapolation from other systems is promising, targeted studies are needed to validate the efficacy and optimize the dosing regimen for ocular applications (Zhang et al., 2025; Makarov & Korkotian, 2023).

Another potential challenge is the inherent variability in the bioactivation of ellagitannins to Urolithins among individuals, owing to differences in gut microbiota composition. While direct administration of Urolithin A circumvents this challenge, variability in metabolic processing and individual pharmacodynamic responses may still influence therapeutic outcomes (Wang & Yu, 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). Moreover, the translation of systemic improvements in mitochondrial function to localized effects within the retina is not entirely straightforward; the blood–retinal barrier and unique ocular microenvironment may affect drug distribution and efficacy (Makarov & Korkotian, 2023; Harper, 2023). These challenges underscore the need for comprehensive ocular pharmacokinetic studies and the development of delivery strategies that ensure adequate drug concentration in the RPE.

Despite these limitations, the overall evidence supports the advancement of Urolithin A as a candidate for treating dry AMD. Its dual mechanism of enhancing both mitophagy and lysosomal function is particularly well-suited to counteracting the complex pathophysiology of AMD, where mitochondrial decay and lysosomal failure converge to induce cellular apoptosis and dysfunction (Auguste, 2022; Borsky et al., 2025). In addition, the compound’s ability to reduce oxidative stress, a critical driver of AMD progression, further bolsters the rationale for its use (Djedjibegović et al., 2020; Jayatunga et al., 2021). Importantly, the wealth of preclinical data demonstrating Urolithin A’s beneficial effects in animal models and cell culture systems provides a compelling argument for its continued evaluation in ocular models of AMD (Hyttinen et al., 2018; Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024).

In summary, Urolithin A presents a highly promising therapeutic candidate for repurposing against dry AMD. Its natural origin, excellent safety profile, favorable pharmacokinetics, and robust mechanism of action—characterized by the activation of PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy and AMPK–TFEB-mediated lysosomal biogenesis—render it an attractive candidate for further investigation. The expected beneficial effects in reducing oxidative stress, clearing damaged mitochondria, and re-establishing lysosomal function in RPE cells align well with the therapeutic needs in dry AMD. Nevertheless, critical gaps remain in direct evidence from ocular models and in addressing potential interindividual variability in drug response. If these challenges are successfully navigated through targeted preclinical and early-phase clinical studies, Urolithin A could fill a significant unmet need in the treatment of dry AMD, offering a novel, mechanism-based intervention that targets the underlying cellular dysfunctions driving the disease (Jayatunga et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2025; Borsky et al., 2025; Hyttinen et al., 2018). Ultimately, while further research is necessary to refine dosing strategies and confirm ocular efficacy, the overall evaluation indicates that Urolithin A is a strong candidate worthy of pursuit in the quest for effective therapies for dry age-related macular degeneration.
