Proposal for Everolimus (Afinitor; Novartis)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Everolimus (Afinitor; Novartis) is a semi‐synthetic derivative of sirolimus—a macrolide originally isolated from Streptomyces hygroscopicus—as evidenced by its chemical designation as 4-O-(2-hydroxyethyl)-rapamycin (Liu et al., 2023, pp. 11–12). By introducing a 2-hydroxyethyl group at position 40, everolimus achieves greater hydrophilicity and an increased oral bioavailability (from 10% to 16% relative to sirolimus) while retaining the core macrolactone structure that is characteristic of rapalogs (Liu et al., 2023, pp. 11–12; Weber, 2015, pp. 27–28). This compound belongs to the class of mTOR inhibitors—specifically, a first‐generation rapalog that selectively inhibits the mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1) while sparing mTORC2 (Liu et al., 2023, pp. 10–11; Weber, 2015, pp. 27–28). As a synthetic derivative of a naturally occurring product, everolimus represents the successful strategy of chemical modification to optimize pharmacokinetic properties and clinical tolerability. The design parameters underpinning everolimus’ synthesis have been guided by the goal of retaining potent immunosuppressive and anti‐proliferative effects while optimizing parameters such as solubility, half‐life, and tissue penetration, with the added advantage of an oral formulation already approved for several clinical indications (Liu et al., 2023, pp. 11–12; Wang et al., 2022, pp. 6–8).

Therapeutic History:  
Everolimus has a well‐established therapeutic history as an immunosuppressant and antineoplastic agent. Clinically, it is approved for treating advanced renal cell carcinoma, progressive neuroendocrine tumors of pancreatic origin, and subependymal giant cell astrocytoma in tuberous sclerosis complex patients who are not candidates for surgery (Wang et al., 2022, pp. 6–8; Weber, 2015, pp. 27–28). In these indications, its mechanism as an mTORC1 inhibitor serves to arrest cell cycle progression and inhibit angiogenesis, which are central to its antitumor activity (Kuo et al., 2018, pp. 5–7; Weber, 2015, pp. 27–28). Moreover, everolimus has been effectively used in the context of organ transplantation to prevent allograft rejection by impairing helper T‐cell proliferation (Wang et al., 2022, pp. 6–8; Weber, 2015, pp. 27–28).

With regard to ocular applications, although everolimus has not been approved specifically for dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD), several preclinical studies have demonstrated its potential benefits in ocular cell types and models. For instance, in vitro investigations have highlighted that everolimus can inhibit the proliferation and migration of retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells and modulate mTOR signaling within retinal tissue—an effect critical to conditions such as proliferative vitreoretinopathy (PVR) (Kuo et al., 2018, pp. 5–8). Additionally, clinical trials such as the Phase 2 randomized trial for neovascular AMD (albeit focused on wet AMD) have assessed everolimus’s efficacy in combination with anti‐vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) agents (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2009), thereby providing indirect support for its ocular pharmacokinetic (PK) profile and safety profile. This background, combined with its well‐known systemic tolerability, underscores its candidacy for repurposing in nonexudative (dry) AMD—a disease area in which few pharmacologic treatments exist.

Mechanism of Action:  
Everolimus exerts its effects by binding to the intracellular receptor FKBP12, forming a complex that interacts with and inhibits mTORC1 (Liu et al., 2023, pp. 11–12; Weber, 2015, pp. 27–28). The inhibition of mTORC1 results in a downstream decrease in the phosphorylation of ribosomal protein S6 kinase (S6K1) and other substrates such as ribosomal protein S6 (RPS6), effectively suppressing cap‐dependent protein synthesis and cellular proliferation (Kuo et al., 2018, pp. 5–7; Liu et al., 2023, pp. 10–11). In the context of RPE cells, inhibition of mTORC1 plays a particularly critical role in modulating autophagic flux. Under normal conditions, mTORC1 phosphorylates and suppresses the transcription factor EB (TFEB), a master regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy. Inhibition of mTORC1 frees TFEB to translocate to the nucleus where it transactivates genes that codify for lysosomal proteins, such as the subunits of the vacuolar ATPase (V‐ATPase), and hydrolases including cathepsin D (Kuo et al., 2018, p. 7; Nieto et al., 2015, pp. 28–32). Preclinical studies have shown that in RPE culture systems, treatment with everolimus results in an increase in LC3 turnover—a marker of enhanced autophagic flux—and accelerates the maturation of cathepsin D (CTSD), which is essential for effective lysosomal degradation of photoreceptor outer segments (Kuo et al., 2018, pp. 5–8). Such modulation of the autophagy–lysosome pathway is mechanistically attractive in dry AMD because the accumulation of cellular debris and lipofuscin in RPE cells is implicated in disease pathogenesis. Furthermore, it has been reported that everolimus displays superior ocular tissue penetration compared with sirolimus in in vitro porcine corneal models, suggesting that its formulation could be optimized for direct ocular administration if required (Liu et al., 2023, pp. 11–14). Overall, the biochemical profile of everolimus supports the hypothesis that targeted mTORC1 inhibition in RPE cells could lead to enhanced TFEB activation and a subsequent restoration of lysosomal function—thereby addressing one of the key pathological features underlying dry AMD.

Expected Effect:  
The hypothesis proposes that everolimus will inhibit mTORC1 in RPE cells, leading to the activation of TFEB, a critical step in lysosomal biogenesis and function. Upon release from mTORC1‐mediated phosphorylation constraints, TFEB translocates to the nucleus where it upregulates the expression of essential lysosomal genes, including those encoding V‐ATPase subunits (which are responsible for lysosomal acidification) and cathepsin D (CTSD), a protease required for the degradation of accumulated waste products (Kuo et al., 2018, p. 7; Nieto et al., 2015, pp. 28–32). This molecular cascade is expected to improve the clearance of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) and other cellular debris that accumulate in RPE cells owing to age‐related impairment of autophagy. In vitro data support that everolimus treatment increases LC3 turnover and facilitates the maturation of CTSD, both of which are indicative of enhanced autophagic flux and lysosomal functionality (Kuo et al., 2018, pp. 5–8). Given that RPE cells naturally express components of the mTOR signaling pathway—including high levels of mTORC1—and that dysfunctional lysosomal clearance is a hallmark of dry AMD (as exemplified by the accumulation of lipofuscin and drusen formation), the expected pharmacodynamic effect of everolimus would be to restore homeostasis in these cells. Successful activation of TFEB is anticipated to lead to improved degradation of damaged organelles and protein aggregates, potentially mitigating the progression of retinal degeneration characteristic of dry AMD. Moreover, the fact that everolimus is already formulated for oral administration with a known ocular PK profile further supports the translational potential of this candidate: systemic dosing may achieve sufficient retinal concentrations to exert the desired effects in RPE cells without necessitating invasive delivery methods (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2009; Liu et al., 2023, pp. 11–12).

Overall Evaluation:  
The potential of everolimus as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD is supported by several compelling factors. First, its established clinical safety and efficacy in other indications—such as renal cell carcinoma and immunosuppression in transplantation—reduce the translational risk associated with drug repurposing (Weber, 2015, pp. 27–28; Wang et al., 2022, pp. 6–8). The fact that everolimus is an FDA‐approved agent with an oral formulation makes it an attractive candidate for repurposing, as its dosing, systemic distribution, and side‐effect profile are already well characterized. In addition, its selective inhibition of mTORC1 has a clear mechanistic rationale in the context of dry AMD. By specifically targeting the mTORC1–TFEB axis, everolimus may correct the impaired lysosomal degradation and autophagic dysfunction that underlie the accumulation of pathogenic debris in RPE cells (Kuo et al., 2018, p. 7; Nieto et al., 2015, pp. 28–32).

A major strength of everolimus is the robust preclinical evidence indicative of its capacity to modulate autophagy. In ocular cell culture systems, everolimus has been shown to enhance LC3 turnover and promote CTSD maturation—both key markers of autophagic and lysosomal efficiency (Kuo et al., 2018, pp. 5–8). Additionally, its improved ocular penetration compared to its parent compound sirolimus suggests that even systemic administration might yield therapeutically relevant levels in the retina (Liu et al., 2023, pp. 11–14). This represents a significant advantage in the context of dry AMD, where achieving effective drug delivery to deeply situated retinal tissues is a persistent challenge.

However, several weaknesses must be acknowledged. Despite a sound mechanistic rationale, direct clinical evidence for everolimus’s efficacy in dry AMD is lacking. Existing clinical studies have primarily focused on other ocular conditions such as neovascular AMD and PVR, and the particular pathophysiology of dry AMD—with its complex interplay of oxidative stress, complement dysregulation, and chronic inflammation—may require a more nuanced therapeutic approach (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2009; Kuo et al., 2018, pp. 3–5). It is also important to consider the potential systemic side effects of mTOR inhibition, which include hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, stomatitis, and, in some cases, immunosuppression (Weber, 2015, pp. 27–28, 28–29). Although these adverse events are generally manageable in oncology and transplant settings, the chronic nature of dry AMD treatment may necessitate a careful balancing of therapeutic benefits against systemic risks. Moreover, while everolimus selectively inhibits mTORC1, prolonged inhibition of this pathway could potentially interfere with homeostatic cellular processes beyond autophagy, raising concerns about long‐term retinal safety.

Another consideration relates to the multifactorial etiology of dry AMD. While lysosomal dysfunction is a critical aspect of the disease, it is accompanied by other pathological processes such as oxidative damage, chronic inflammation, and alterations in the complement cascade. Thus, monotherapy with an mTORC1 inhibitor may not be sufficiently robust to address all disease mechanisms. Combination therapies that target multiple pathological pathways might ultimately be necessary for effective disease modification. Lastly, discrepancies in the translation of preclinical findings to human disease remain a persistent challenge. For example, while certain animal models of retinal disease have demonstrated beneficial effects of mTOR inhibition, analogous results have not always been replicated in clinical settings (Kuo et al., 2018, pp. 3–5; ClinicalTrials.gov, 2009).

In summary, everolimus presents as a mechanistically intriguing candidate for repurposing in dry AMD due to its ability to inhibit mTORC1 and thereby promote TFEB‐mediated restoration of lysosomal function. Its solid record as an FDA‐approved drug with established dosing regimens and acceptable systemic safety lends additional support to its viability as a repurposing candidate. Preclinical data suggest that everolimus increases autophagic flux in RPE cells, enhancing LC3 turnover and CTSD maturation, which theoretically should facilitate the clearance of pathological debris that characterizes dry AMD (Kuo et al., 2018, pp. 5–8). The advantages of an oral formulation and favorable ocular pharmacokinetics further underpin its translational potential (Liu et al., 2023, pp. 11–12; ClinicalTrials.gov, 2009).

Nonetheless, the candidate also faces important challenges. Key limitations include the absence of direct clinical evidence for efficacy in dry AMD, potential systemic adverse effects associated with prolonged mTORC1 inhibition, and the inherent complexity of dry AMD pathology, which may require multimodal therapeutic strategies. Moreover, past experiences in ocular applications—such as studies in PVR models where everolimus did not yield a statistically significant clinical improvement—highlight the need for carefully controlled dose-finding and safety studies (Kuo et al., 2018, pp. 3–5). It will be crucial to establish that systemic dosing can achieve therapeutic retinal concentrations without eliciting unintended systemic toxicity or disrupting normal RPE physiology.

Overall, while mechanistic and preclinical data provide a strong rationale for repurposing everolimus to treat dry AMD, further rigorous preclinical studies specifically in models of dry AMD are warranted. A careful evaluation of dosing regimens, potential adverse effects, and efficacy endpoints will be essential in the translational development of everolimus for this indication. The mechanistic specificity for mTORC1 inhibition, combined with the ability to enhance autophagic clearance in RPE cells, represents a significant strength that could offer a novel approach to modifying the course of dry AMD. However, the challenges associated with systemic mTOR inhibition and the need for combination strategies or localized delivery systems must be addressed in subsequent research phases. In conclusion, everolimus is a promising, though not yet definitively validated, candidate for repurposing in dry AMD, and we recommend that further detailed studies be undertaken to fully elucidate its potential in restoring lysosomal function and ameliorating retinal degeneration in this patient population (Liu et al., 2023, pp. 11–12; ClinicalTrials.gov, 2009; Kuo et al., 2018, pp. 5–7; Weber, 2015, pp. 27–28; Wang et al., 2022, pp. 6–8; Kuo et al., 2018, pp. 7–8).
