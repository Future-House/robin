Proposal for Resveratrol (Sigma R5010)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Resveratrol is a naturally occurring polyphenolic compound that belongs to the stilbene family and is most famously isolated from grape skins and red wine. Its chemical structure features two aromatic rings linked by an ethylene bridge, which confers potent antioxidant properties. Discovered over four decades ago, resveratrol (catalog number Sigma R5010) has been extensively studied for its diverse biological activities, including anti‐inflammatory, anti‐oxidative, and anti‐aging effects. As a member of natural polyphenols, resveratrol has been used both as a dietary supplement and as a laboratory tool to probe intracellular signaling and epigenetic regulation. This compound has been explored for therapeutic applications in cardiovascular diseases, metabolic disorders, and various age‐related conditions, thereby positioning it as an attractive lead for repurposing. Its natural origin, safety profile upon oral administration, and documented bioavailability in retinal tissues support its candidature for ocular indications, particularly in mitigating dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) pathology (Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021; Delmas et al., 2021).

Therapeutic History:  
Numerous biochemical studies have established that resveratrol exerts its beneficial effects by modulating key intracellular pathways. Historically, resveratrol has been used as an anti‐oxidant in various cellular systems and has been investigated in preclinical models of neurodegeneration, cardiovascular disease, and metabolic syndrome. In the context of ophthalmology, several studies have evaluated its efficacy in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cell models. For instance, research using ARPE-19 cells, a well-established human RPE cell line, demonstrated that resveratrol activates SIRT1 to protect against oxidative stress and apoptosis induced by challenging stimuli such as sodium iodate, UVA radiation, acrolein, and A2E—a major component of lipofuscin implicated in drusen formation and AMD pathology (Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021; Li et al., 2024; Alaimo et al., 2020). Additional investigations combined resveratrol with omega-3 fatty acids in formulations like Resvega® to induce autophagy and enhance cell survival under conditions simulating AMD (Delmas et al., 2021; Koskela et al., 2016). While much of the published literature has concentrated on resveratrol’s effects in oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and autophagy promotion in RPE cells, a few reports have noted its capacity to counteract the toxic accumulation of intracellular debris that is a hallmark of dry AMD (Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021; Pinelli et al., 2025). Moreover, preliminary clinical reports have suggested that long-term oral supplementation with resveratrol-containing nutraceuticals may contribute to anatomic restoration of retinal structure and improved visual function in elderly patients, thus further underpinning its translational potential. Notably, while there is extensive preclinical evidence in vitro and in animal models, resveratrol has not yet been unequivocally validated in advanced clinical trials targeting dry AMD specifically (Abu-Amero et al., 2016).

Mechanism of Action:  
A robust body of research supports that resveratrol acts principally through the activation of SIRT1 (sirtuin 1) and the energy sensor AMPK (AMP-activated protein kinase), both of which are critical for maintaining cellular homeostasis in the aging retina. At the molecular level, resveratrol has been reported to directly and indirectly stimulate SIRT1 activity in RPE cells, leading to the deacetylation of several downstream targets such as PGC-1α (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha). This deacetylated PGC-1α, in turn, upregulates mitochondrial biogenesis and enhances cellular oxidative phosphorylation, thereby improving mitochondrial quality control and reducing the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021; Li et al., 2024; Buonfiglio et al., 2024). Concomitantly, resveratrol activates AMPK, albeit through complex mechanisms involving inhibition of phosphodiesterases and alterations in the intracellular AMP/ATP ratio. The resulting AMPK phosphorylation facilitates a myriad of downstream effects, notably the inhibition of mTOR signaling (a known suppressor of autophagy) and activation of ULK1, a serine/threonine kinase that initiates autophagosome formation (Li et al., 2024; Abu-Amero et al., 2016).

In RPE cells, the synergistic activation of SIRT1 and AMPK by resveratrol is believed to promote autophagy, as evidenced by increased LC3II/I ratios and decreased p62 expression. Autophagy is particularly critical in RPE cells to clear toxic cellular debris such as A2E and other protein aggregates that contribute to drusen accumulation in dry AMD (Alaimo et al., 2020; Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021; Pinelli et al., 2023). Furthermore, the prevailing hypothesis extends these autophagic benefits by suggesting that, downstream of AMPK/SIRT1 activation, resveratrol may induce TFEB dephosphorylation. TFEB is a transcription factor that, when dephosphorylated, translocates into the nucleus and upregulates the coordinated lysosomal expression and regulation (CLEAR) network. This results in enhanced lysosomal biogenesis, improved lysosomal acidification, and increased activation of lysosomal enzymes such as cathepsin D. Although direct experimental evidence linking resveratrol to TFEB dephosphorylation in RPE cells is not abundantly detailed in the current literature, several studies imply that agents that activate AMPK and inhibit mTOR can indirectly promote TFEB activity. In this context, resveratrol’s ability to inhibit mTOR, partly through AMPK activation, suggests a plausible mechanism for the promotion of TFEB-mediated lysosomal functions (Falcão et al., 2025; Pinelli et al., 2023). These molecular events are anticipated to bolster the autophagy-lysosomal system, thereby mitigating the drusen accumulation characteristic of dry AMD. Additionally, resveratrol exerts direct antioxidant effects by scavenging free radicals and modulating the expression of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase, which contributes further to its protective milieu in the retina (Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021; Abu-Amero et al., 2016).

Expected Effect:  
Based on its molecular pharmacology, the expected effect of resveratrol in preclinical models of dry AMD would be multifaceted. In assays utilizing ARPE-19 cells or primary human RPE cells, treatment with resveratrol is expected to enhance SIRT1 activity, as measured by increased deacetylation of SIRT1 substrates and upregulation of PGC-1α nuclear translocation. Concurrently, AMPK phosphorylation is anticipated to be elevated, leading to inhibition of the mTOR pathway; this would foster an increased autophagic flux, observable as a higher LC3II/I ratio and reduced levels of the autophagy substrate p62 (Li et al., 2024; Alaimo et al., 2020; Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021). Furthermore, the candidate is hypothesized to indirectly promote TFEB dephosphorylation, facilitating its nuclear translocation and subsequent upregulation of lysosomal genes. This could manifest as enhanced lysosomal biogenesis, improved acidification of the lysosomal compartment, and elevated activity of lysosomal proteases such as cathepsin D. The cumulative consequence of these events would be a more efficient clearance of intracellular debris, including lipofuscin and other drusen components, thereby ameliorating RPE dysfunction associated with dry AMD (Falcão et al., 2025; Pinelli et al., 2023).

It is also expected that resveratrol’s anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic actions would reduce the chronic low-grade inflammation that underpins AMD pathogenesis. In vivo, improved autophagic and lysosomal clearance should correlate with decreased accumulation of pathological deposits at the RPE-Bruch’s membrane interface, potentially reducing the progression of drusen formation and preserving retinal structure and function (Alaimo et al., 2020; Abu-Amero et al., 2016; Pinelli et al., 2025). Given that resveratrol is detectable in retinal tissue following oral administration, its systemic safety and potential for long-term use add to its attractiveness as a repurposed therapeutic agent for dry AMD. Moreover, the dual capacity to modulate both metabolic and epigenetic pathways may offer disease-modifying effects that extend beyond symptomatic relief and target the underlying cellular aging processes in the RPE (Subramani et al., 2017).

Overall Evaluation:  
Resveratrol emerges as a promising candidate for repurposing in the context of dry AMD owing to its well-documented ability to activate SIRT1 and AMPK, which are pivotal signaling nodes in the maintenance of cellular homeostasis and autophagy. One of the central strengths of resveratrol is its dual mechanism of action. By targeting SIRT1, resveratrol not only combats oxidative stress through the deacetylation of transcription factors and enhancement of mitochondrial biogenesis but also modulates anti-inflammatory pathways relevant to retinal preservation (Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021; Li et al., 2024). Simultaneously, its activation of AMPK provides additional impetus for autophagy induction through mTOR inhibition and ULK1 activation. Such molecular actions are of particular relevance to RPE cells, in which impairment of the autophagy-lysosomal system is a key pathogenetic mechanism leading to the accumulation of cellular waste products such as drusen—a hallmark of dry AMD (Alaimo et al., 2020; Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021).

Furthermore, the hypothesis that resveratrol could foster TFEB dephosphorylation is especially intriguing. Although direct evidence linking resveratrol to TFEB activation in RPE cells is currently limited, the known interactions between AMPK/mTOR signaling and TFEB activity provide a strong conceptual framework. Activation of TFEB would result in enhanced lysosomal biogenesis, acidification, and increased cathepsin D activity—mechanisms that are critical for the proper degradation of accumulated photoreceptor-derived debris and toxic aggregates (Falcão et al., 2025; Pinelli et al., 2023). This potential to bolster a declining lysosomal-autophagy system with age might address the fundamental cellular dysfunction observed in dry AMD.

On the clinical side, resveratrol demonstrates a favorable safety profile with established oral bioavailability and experimental evidence suggesting retinal tissue penetration. Combined with its history of use as an anti-oxidative and anti-inflammatory agent in other disease contexts, these features support its translational potential. Several nutraceutical formulations incorporating resveratrol (often in combination with omega-3 fatty acids, as in Resvega®) have already reached early-phase clinical evaluation for ocular indications, providing preliminary signals of efficacy in improving retinal structure and function among aged patients (Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021; Delmas et al., 2021).

Nevertheless, several weaknesses and gaps must be acknowledged. First, while there is abundant preclinical evidence of resveratrol’s activation of SIRT1/AMPK pathways and subsequent autophagy induction in RPE cells, the specific molecular events leading to TFEB dephosphorylation, enhanced lysosomal biogenesis, lysosomal acidification, and increased cathepsin D activity are not yet comprehensively elucidated. In other words, the critical mechanistic link between resveratrol treatment and lysosomal enhancement remains somewhat inferential rather than directly demonstrated in the existing literature (Falcão et al., 2025; Hyttinen et al., 2019). Second, much of the supporting data comes from in vitro studies or animal models, with limited robust clinical evidence explicitly addressing dry AMD. Although some pilot clinical studies with resveratrol-containing formulations show promise, their design, scale, and long-term outcomes are not sufficiently advanced to confirm therapeutic efficacy in a broad AMD population (Abu-Amero et al., 2016). Third, resveratrol is known to have issues with bioavailability due to rapid metabolism and low systemic concentrations after oral ingestion; therefore, optimizing its delivery to retinal tissues, perhaps via novel formulations or nanoparticle carriers, may be necessary to achieve therapeutic levels (Subramani et al., 2017; Pinelli et al., 2025). Additionally, while its pleiotropic activity is a strength in terms of targeting multiple pathogenic mechanisms, this same feature can pose challenges in ensuring specificity and avoiding off-target effects.

In summary, resveratrol presents a compelling case as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD. Its ability to activate SIRT1 and AMPK, induce autophagy, and potentially stimulate TFEB-driven lysosomal biogenesis aligns well with the current understanding of the cellular derangements underlying AMD pathogenesis. The expected outcome would be improved clearance of toxic cellular debris and drusen, preservation of RPE integrity, and amelioration of oxidative stress and inflammation in the aging retina. However, to fully validate resveratrol as a viable treatment for dry AMD, further investigations are warranted to (a) directly demonstrate its effects on TFEB dephosphorylation and lysosomal function in RPE cells, (b) optimize its ocular bioavailability, and (c) establish robust clinical endpoints in well-designed trials. Overall, while promising and supported by a substantial body of preclinical evidence, additional targeted research is required to definitively establish resveratrol’s mechanistic effects on the autophagy-lysosomal pathway and its therapeutic potential in dry AMD (Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021; Li et al., 2024; Falcão et al., 2025; Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021; Abu-Amero et al., 2016).
