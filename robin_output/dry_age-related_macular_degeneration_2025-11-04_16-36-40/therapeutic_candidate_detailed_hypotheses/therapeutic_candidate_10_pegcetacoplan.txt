Proposal for Pegcetacoplan (APL-2; Apellis)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Pegcetacoplan (APL-2, now marketed as Syfovre) is a synthetically engineered, PEGylated cyclic peptide that belongs to the family of compstatin analogs designed to inhibit complement component C3. Its origins lie in early peptide discovery via phage‐display and random peptide library screening, and successive rounds of medicinal chemistry optimization were applied to improve its binding affinity, solubility, and pharmacokinetic profile through PEG conjugation (Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023; Ong et al., 2024). The PEGylation process not only grants enhanced resistance to enzymatic degradation but also prolongs the intravitreal residence time, an essential property for ocular applications requiring sustained drug exposure with limited dosing frequency (Tzoumas et al., 2023; Mastellos & Lambris, 2024). As a second‐generation compstatin analog, pegcetacoplan employs a bivalent design that enables high specificity for human C3, blocking its proteolytic cleavage into the active fragments C3a and C3b. These fragments are potent mediators that further amplify the inflammatory cascade through the formation of C3 convertases (Lamers et al., 2022). This therapeutic candidate is embedded within a broader class of complement inhibitors that have been actively explored in inflammatory disorders, and notably in ophthalmology for dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) where aberrant complement activation plays a central pathogenic role (Lamers et al., 2022; Mastellos & Lambris, 2024). Its synthetic nature, coupled with its targeted molecular design, positions pegcetacoplan as an attractive candidate for addressing the degenerative processes in geographic atrophy (GA) associated with dry AMD by directly modulating the complement cascade (Tzoumas et al., 2023; Patel et al., 2022).

Therapeutic History  
Historically, the evolution of pegcetacoplan reflects a successful translation from bench to bedside across multiple therapeutic domains. Initially, the compstatin class was primarily developed and evaluated in systemic disorders characterized by complement dysregulation, most notably in paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH). Early clinical trials in PNH provided critical safety and efficacy data that underscored the value of targeting C3, paving the way for the repurposing of these molecules in ocular disease (Lamers et al., 2022). With the conceptual framework and preclinical data in hand, pegcetacoplan was advanced into the ophthalmology space. The pivotal Phase II FILLY trial enrolled 246 patients with GA secondary to dry AMD, and the study demonstrated that monthly intravitreal injections of pegcetacoplan resulted in a statistically significant reduction of GA lesion growth by approximately 29%, while an every-other-month dosing regimen yielded a 20% reduction relative to sham-control treatment (Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023; Patel et al., 2022). These encouraging anatomical outcomes, evidenced by the slowed progression of GA and the concurrent reduction in photoreceptor loss as evaluated by SD-OCT imaging, provided a strong rationale for subsequent Phase III clinical trials (Regillo & Heier, n.d.; Patel et al., 2022).  
In addition to its use in dry AMD, pegcetacoplan’s earlier regulatory approval for PNH helped establish it as a viable molecule with a favorable safety profile. Its journey through successive clinical phases—from Phase I safety and tolerability studies to Phase II efficacy studies and ultimately Phase III confirmation—has offered compelling evidence that targeted complement inhibition can modify the disease course in conditions characterized by chronic complement activation (Tzoumas et al., 2023). However, clinical experience with pegcetacoplan has also identified potential safety signals, including an observed increase in the incidence of new-onset exudative (wet) AMD among certain subpopulations, a factor that necessitates careful patient selection and monitoring (Lamers et al., 2022; Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023). Overall, the therapeutic history of pegcetacoplan—spanning its origin from fundamental compstatin research to its current application in treating geographic atrophy—demonstrates its potential for repurposing as a disease-modifying therapy in ocular settings (Lamers et al., 2022; Desai & Dugel, 2022).

Mechanism of Action  
At the molecular level, the pathogenic cascade in dry AMD is tightly linked to dysregulated complement activation, and pegcetacoplan intervenes at one of the central nodes of this pathway by inhibiting complement component C3. Under physiological conditions, C3 is cleaved by C3 convertases into C3a—an anaphylatoxin that drives inflammation—and C3b, which functions as an opsonin and further propagates complement amplification (Yu & Wykoff, 2021). Pegcetacoplan binds to a key epitope on C3, thereby sterically hindering the enzyme-driven cleavage that would otherwise generate these active fragments. By effectively arresting this pivotal step in the complement cascade, the drug prevents downstream events such as the assembly of terminal complement complexes and the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC) (Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023; Wu & Sun, 2019).  
The inhibition of C3 cleavage has several critical biochemical consequences. First, by lowering the concentrations of C3a and C3b, pegcetacoplan reduces the generation of pro-inflammatory mediators and the subsequent chemotactic recruitment of immune cells, which, in turn, diminishes local inflammation in the retinal environment (Regillo & Heier, n.d.; Tzoumas et al., 2023). Second, the reduction in C3b deposition directly limits the formation of MAC on retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) surfaces—a process that has been implicated in triggering lysosomal dysfunction within these critical cells (Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023; Park et al., 2021). The MAC, when deposited sublytically on RPE cells, can compromise the function of the vacuolar H⁺-ATPase (v-ATPase), the proton pump responsible for maintaining the acidic environment of lysosomes. Acidification of the lysosomal compartment is essential for optimal activity of proteolytic enzymes, such as cathepsin D, which are required to degrade photoreceptor outer segment debris and other cytotoxic by-products (Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023; Tzoumas et al., 2023).  
Thus, by preventing excessive complement activation and subsequent MAC formation, pegcetacoplan indirectly safeguards the intracellular degradative machinery of the RPE. This preservation of v-ATPase function ensures proper lysosomal acidification, which in turn maintains cathepsin D activity and reduces lysosomal overload—a key mechanism implicated in the progressive atrophy of the RPE observed in dry AMD (Mastellos & Lambris, 2024; Regillo & Heier, n.d.). Moreover, preclinical studies have demonstrated that complement blockade via agents such as pegcetacoplan lowers sub-RPE deposit formation and inflammasome priming, processes intimately linked to lysosomal dysfunction and chronic inflammation (Liao et al., 2020; Lamers et al., 2022). Collectively, the mechanism of action of pegcetacoplan encompasses both direct inhibition of complement-driven inflammatory processes and indirect protection of intracellular catabolic functions in the RPE, thereby addressing two core pathological contributors to geographic atrophy (Kassa et al., 2019; Rubner et al., 2022).

Expected Effect  
Based on its detailed mechanism of action, pegcetacoplan is expected to exert a dual beneficial effect in the management of dry AMD. First, by binding to and inhibiting complement C3, it should markedly reduce the formation of inflammatory mediators (C3a, C3b) as well as downstream terminal pathway components such as the MAC. Clinical trial data support this expectation: in the Phase II FILLY study, monthly intravitreal injections of pegcetacoplan were associated with a roughly 29% reduction in the rate of GA lesion growth relative to control, while an alternate-month regimen achieved a 20% reduction (Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023; Patel et al., 2022). These anatomical outcomes are presumed to correlate with a slowing of disease progression and a delay in the onset of irreversible retinal degeneration.  
Second, through the inhibition of MAC deposition on RPE cells, pegcetacoplan is anticipated to preserve the functional integrity of lysosomes by ensuring that v-ATPase activity is maintained and that cathepsin D retains its proteolytic function. The preservation of lysosomal function is critical not only for the proper degradation of photoreceptor outer segment debris but also for protecting RPE cells from metabolic stress and subsequent cell death (Liao et al., 2020; Desai & Dugel, 2022). Consequently, the expected biochemical outcome is that pegcetacoplan will reduce lysosomal overload and the accumulation of toxic metabolic by-products—a process thought to contribute to the formation of sub-RPE deposits that further exacerbate tissue atrophy in dry AMD (Lamers et al., 2022; Desai & Dugel, 2022).  
At the cellular level, maintaining cathepsin D activity is imperative because this protease is responsible for degrading intracellular waste products that accumulate in RPE cells, and its functionality is closely tied to the acidic environment provided by lysosomes. By preserving this aspect of lysosomal physiology, pegcetacoplan should contribute to RPE cell survival and thereby delay the progression of geographic atrophy (Patel et al., 2022). Although improvements in functional endpoints such as visual acuity have not been immediately evident in short-term clinical trials, the principal therapeutic endpoint remains the deceleration of structural degeneration. This structural preservation is expected to eventually translate into a longer period of maintained retinal function and a postponed deterioration in vision, ultimately extending the window during which patients can retain functional vision (Wu & Sun, 2019; Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023).  
Furthermore, the established dosing regimens—with monthly or alternate-month intravitreal injections—are designed to sustain local complement inhibition at therapeutic levels without causing excessive immunosuppression that might predispose patients to infections. However, some studies have raised concerns about an increased risk of conversion to exudative AMD (wet AMD) during treatment, signaling that while the inhibition of C3 can preserve RPE integrity, careful monitoring is warranted to manage potential adverse events (Regillo & Heier, n.d.; Rubner et al., 2022).

Overall Evaluation  
A comprehensive review of pegcetacoplan as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD reveals a well-substantiated rationale and a promising clinical profile, albeit with some challenges that must be addressed. One of the most compelling strengths of pegcetacoplan is its precise molecular targeting of complement component C3—a central mediator in the complement cascade. By inhibiting C3, pegcetacoplan effectively blocks the generation of both C3a and C3b, thereby reducing pro-inflammatory signaling and preventing the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC) (Yu & Wykoff, 2021). This upstream blockade provides a broad-spectrum mechanism capable of modulating all three complement activation pathways (classical, lectin, and alternative), which is particularly important in a multifactorial disease such as dry AMD (Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023; Lamers et al., 2022).  
Clinically, the results from the Phase II FILLY study are extremely encouraging, demonstrating a 29% reduction in GA lesion growth with monthly intravitreal administration and a 20% reduction with alternate-month dosing, outcomes that have been replicated in additional studies and served as the basis for progressing to Phase III evaluations (Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023; Patel et al., 2022; Desai & Dugel, 2022). These anatomical endpoints, while not immediately translating into measurable improvements in visual acuity, are critical surrogates for the long-term preservation of retinal structure and function—a key goal in the management of dry AMD (Regillo & Heier, n.d.; Patel et al., 2022).  
Biochemically, pegcetacoplan’s mechanism of action extends beyond the mere suppression of complement-mediated inflammation. Its ability to indirectly preserve lysosomal function by preventing MAC-induced dysfunction of the vacuolar H⁺-ATPase (v-ATPase) and maintaining cathepsin D activity introduces a novel paradigm in addressing the intracellular stress that accelerates RPE degeneration (Liao et al., 2020; Mastellos & Lambris, 2024). In dry AMD, the accumulation of metabolic by-products due to deficient lysosomal degradation leads to the formation of drusen and sub-RPE deposits, which further stimulate complement activation and create a vicious cycle of cellular damage. Pegcetacoplan, by protecting lysosomal integrity, offers the potential to break this cycle and slow the progression of geographic atrophy (Park et al., 2021; Kassa et al., 2019).  
There are, however, certain weaknesses and challenges that must be taken into account. One notable concern is the increased risk of new-onset exudative AMD that has been reported in some clinical studies. This adverse event appears to be dose-dependent and may reflect a complex interplay between complement inhibition and pro-angiogenic pathways in the retina (Wu & Sun, 2019; Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023). The occurrence of neovascular complications necessitates rigorous patient selection criteria, close imaging surveillance, and potentially the development of combination strategies to counterbalance this risk without compromising the therapeutic efficacy against GA (Lamers et al., 2022; Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023).  
Another challenge is the logistical and patient adherence issues related to frequent intravitreal injections. Although the PEGylation of pegcetacoplan aids in prolonging its ocular residence, the need for repeated injections every month or every other month represents a significant treatment burden. This may affect patient compliance and overall treatment outcomes, particularly when compared to therapies that require less frequent dosing or alternative delivery modalities (Piri & Kaplan, 2023; Petrukhin, 2020). Future work aimed at developing sustained-release formulations or implantable devices could potentially alleviate this drawback (Lamers et al., 2022; Patel et al., 2022).  
From a mechanistic standpoint, while the inhibition of C3 is expected to preserve lysosomal function by protecting v-ATPase activity and sustaining cathepsin D levels, further research is needed to fully elucidate the downstream biochemical consequences of long-term complement inhibition. Detailed studies evaluating biomarkers of lysosomal function—including cathepsin D activity, lysosomal pH, and the turnover of sub-RPE deposits—will be instrumental in correlating these molecular effects with clinical outcomes (Desai & Dugel, 2022; Rubner et al., 2022). Such investigations are essential not only for optimizing dosing regimens but also for refining patient selection strategies, ensuring that those most likely to benefit from complement inhibition are appropriately targeted (Rubner et al., 2022; Patel et al., 2022).  
In addition, it is worth noting that while the current clinical evidence has predominantly focused on anatomical endpoints such as GA lesion size, translating these findings into meaningful improvements in vision remains an ongoing challenge. The disconnect between structural preservation and functional outcomes is a well-recognized issue in dry AMD research and may require additional therapeutic interventions or combination approaches to achieve robust clinical benefits (Patel et al., 2022; Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023).  
Overall, the benefit–risk profile of pegcetacoplan remains favorable when considered in the context of a disease with a profound unmet medical need. Its dual-action mechanism—combining potent complement inhibition with the preservation of intracellular lysosomal function—addresses both extracellular inflammatory processes and the intracellular degradative pathways that contribute to RPE cell loss and GA progression (Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023; Mastellos & Lambris, 2024). Moreover, the clinical trial data, particularly the significant reduction in GA lesion progression observed in the FILLY study, provide a compelling rationale for further investment in pegcetacoplan’s development for dry AMD (Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023; Patel et al., 2022).  
Nevertheless, it is imperative that future studies incorporate long-term follow-up and incorporate both anatomical and functional endpoints to fully capture the therapeutic potential of pegcetacoplan. Further optimization of the dosing regimen, exploration of sustained-release formulations, and rigorous monitoring for conversion to exudative AMD are critical next steps that will determine the long-term viability of this agent in clinical practice (Tzoumas et al., 2023; Lamers et al., 2022). In addition, combination therapies targeting complementary pathogenic pathways—such as oxidative stress and dysregulated angiogenesis—may augment the efficacy of pegcetacoplan and help mitigate its potential adverse effects (Halawa et al., 2021; Qin et al., 2021).  
In summary, the body of biomedical, clinical, and biochemical evidence indicates that pegcetacoplan holds significant promise as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD. Its innovative design as a PEGylated compstatin analog ensures potent and sustained inhibition of complement C3, addressing a critical upstream mediator in the inflammatory cascade that drives RPE cell dysfunction and geographic atrophy (Tzoumas et al., 2023). Through its targeted action, pegcetacoplan not only reduces the formation of deleterious inflammatory fragments and the deposition of MAC but also indirectly preserves the lysosomal function essential for RPE homeostasis (Mastellos & Lambris, 2024).  
While challenges remain—particularly the risk of neovascular conversion and the inherent burden of frequent intravitreal administration—the overall therapeutic profile of pegcetacoplan suggests that it can significantly slow the progression of GA and potentially preserve visual function in patients with dry AMD (Lamers et al., 2022; Rubner et al., 2022). With further clinical validation and optimization of treatment protocols, pegcetacoplan is poised to redefine the standard of care for dry AMD and serve as a blueprint for the development of future complement-targeted therapies (Lamers et al., 2022; Mastellos et al., 2024).  
From an expert drug development perspective, pegcetacoplan exemplifies how targeted modulation of the complement system can address the multifactorial pathology of dry AMD. Its capacity to intersect both extracellular inflammatory processes and intracellular lysosomal homeostasis provides a mechanistically unique approach that directly addresses the underlying pathophysiology of geographic atrophy. The robust clinical efficacy data, particularly the demonstrated 29% reduction in GA lesion growth in Phase II trials, strongly justify the continued prioritization of pegcetacoplan in the development pipeline (Cruz-Pimentel & Wu, 2023; Patel et al., 2022; Lamers et al., 2022).  
In conclusion, the comprehensive literature review across biomedical, clinical, and biochemical domains confirms that pegcetacoplan is a highly promising repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD. Its synthetic origin, advanced molecular design, and demonstrated efficacy in clinical trials provide a solid foundation for its continued development. Despite potential challenges—including the risk of wet AMD conversion and the practical hurdles of repeated intravitreal administration—the advantages conferred by its targeted mechanism of action and the preservation of critical lysosomal functions in RPE cells substantiate its potential to slow geographic atrophy progression and ultimately improve patient outcomes (Park et al., 2019; Petrukhin, 2020; Regillo & Heier, n.d.). Future research should focus on long-term outcome studies, refinement of dosing strategies, and the possible integration of complementary therapies to maximize the therapeutic benefit while mitigating risks. Based on the current evidence, pegcetacoplan merits continued investment and clinical research as a lead candidate in the quest for an effective disease-modifying treatment for dry AMD (Qin et al., 2021; Rubner et al., 2022).
