Proposal for Nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN; Sigma N3501)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) is an endogenous intermediate in the mammalian NAD⁺ salvage pathway, generated by nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT) from nicotinamide (NAM) and 5-phosphoribosyl‐1‐pyrophosphate (PRPP) and converted to NAD⁺ by nicotinamide mononucleotide adenylyltransferases (NMNAT1–3) (Jadeja et al., 2020; Cimaglia et al., 2020). NMN belongs to the vitamin B3–derived NAD⁺ precursor class and is one of several precursors (others include nicotinamide [NAM], nicotinic acid [NA], and nicotinamide riboside [NR]) used to augment cellular NAD⁺ pools in aging and disease (Cimaglia et al., 2020). NMN was prioritized as a practical intervention because direct NAD⁺ delivery to tissues is not straightforward, whereas in vivo administration of NMN elevates tissue NAD⁺ and supports mitochondrial function and cellular stress defenses in multiple systems (Ebeling et al., 2020; Rahman et al., 2024). Transport and uptake mechanisms include the Slc12a8 transporter described in mouse small intestine, as well as extracellular dephosphorylation to NR followed by NR uptake via equilibrative nucleoside transporters; intracellular NR is re‐phosphorylated by NRK1 to NMN for resynthesis of NAD⁺ (Will‐Orrego, 2020a; Rahman et al., 2024). Notably for RPE models, ARPE‐19 cells have been reported to express transporters supporting NMN uptake but not NR uptake, favoring NMN over NR in this system (Will‐Orrego, 2020b). In RPE and retinal contexts, NMN and related precursors have been deployed preclinically to restore NAD⁺, improve mitochondrial function, modulate sirtuin‐dependent pathways, and mitigate features of neurodegeneration and cellular senescence (Jadeja et al., 2018; Ebeling et al., 2020; Lin, 2020).

Therapeutic History  
NAD⁺ decline with age is conserved across tissues, including the retina and RPE, and is mechanistically linked to impaired mitochondrial function, heightened oxidative stress, and increased susceptibility to degeneration (Jadeja et al., 2018; Cimaglia et al., 2020). In the outer retina, rod‐ or cone‐specific Nampt deletion produces NAD⁺ deficiency and photoreceptor degeneration; systemic NMN at 150 mg/kg i.p. restored retinal function by ERG and preserved photoreceptor structure, establishing that exogenous NMN can replenish retinal NAD⁺ and mitigate degeneration linked to impaired salvage (Lin, 2020). NMN also protected in models of acute retinal injury: daily systemic NMN reduced photoreceptor death and oxidative stress after retinal detachment and increased retinal SIRT1 and HO‐1, consistent with NAD⁺‐dependent sirtuin activation in vivo (Chen et al., 2020). In ischemia models, NMN administration protected against retinal dysfunction (Lee et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2024). In a cholesterol efflux–deficient murine model that recapitulates key dry AMD features driven by senescent subretinal macrophages (Abca1/g1‐deficient), systemic NMN reduced subretinal drusenoid deposits (SDDs), decreased lipofuscin‐positive macrophage burden, and improved electrophysiological and structural endpoints, supporting a role for NAD⁺ augmentation in reversing senescence‐driven outer retinal pathology in vivo (Terao et al., 2024). In RPE‐directed models, pharmacologic NAMPT inhibition with FK866 is widely used to mimic age‐related NAD⁺ decline; in ARPE‐19 and primary RPE, NMN co‐treatment preserved NAD⁺, SIRT1 protein, viability, and reduced senescence markers, and in vivo systemic NMN preserved RPE NAD⁺ and SIRT1 after subretinal FK866 (Jadeja et al., 2018). Oxidant injury with sodium iodate (NaIO₃) induced RPE senescence, NAD⁺ depletion, mitochondrial dysfunction, and subretinal inflammation; NMN restored RPE NAD⁺, reduced DNA damage and senescence markers, improved mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP in ARPE‐19, and reduced inflammatory cell recruitment in vivo, with intact Sirt1 signaling required for NMN benefit (Ren et al., 2022). Outside strictly RPE biology, NMN improved RPE phagocytosis and mitochondrial ultrastructure in the RCS rat model of retinal degeneration, with a mechanistic link to a NAD⁺/GAPDH axis that modulated actin/cytoskeletal phagocytosis machinery in RPE; GAPDH knockdown abrogated NMN’s benefits, supporting a causal chain from NAD⁺ repletion to enhanced RPE POS clearance via GAPDH‐dependent pathways (Zhang et al., 2024).

Clinical translation of NAD⁺ precursors has begun with nicotinamide and NR in ophthalmology-adjacent indications (e.g., glaucoma), demonstrating tolerability and retinal functional protection in mice, and documenting NAD⁺ elevation in adult humans, but direct clinical data for NMN in dry AMD are not available in the provided context (Cimaglia et al., 2020; Rahman et al., 2024). Long-term NMN treatment in mice mitigated age-associated physiological decline systemically and reportedly ameliorated eye function, but detailed ocular pharmacology and human NMN ocular safety are not established here (Rahman et al., 2024). Collectively, the preclinical record supports NMN as an NAD⁺ augmenting therapy with demonstrated efficacy in retina and RPE-relevant models of NAD⁺ deficiency, oxidative injury, and senescence; however, there is no direct published clinical trial evidence in dry AMD patients in this context set (Jadeja et al., 2018; Lin, 2020; Chen et al., 2020; Terao et al., 2024).

Mechanism of Action  
Core biochemistry  
- NAD⁺ salvage. In mammalian cells, the salvage pathway supplies most NAD⁺ (~80%), with NAMPT catalyzing NAM → NMN and NMNAT1–3 completing NMN → NAD⁺; this pool fuels redox reactions and serves as substrate for NAD⁺‐consuming enzymes including sirtuins, PARPs, and the NAD glycohydrolase CD38 (Cimaglia et al., 2020; Jadeja et al., 2020). NAMPT-mediated biosynthesis is essential for vision, and its decline in RPE with age causally links to reduced NAD⁺, reduced SIRT1 expression/activity, and enhanced cellular senescence (Jadeja et al., 2018).  
- Uptake and intracellular handling. NMN can enter cells via specific transporters (Slc12a8 shown in mouse gut) and/or after extracellular dephosphorylation to NR with re-phosphorylation by NRK1, routes that are cell type–dependent; ARPE-19 preferentially transport NMN in vitro, supporting the choice of NMN over NR in these RPE models (Will‐Orrego, 2020a; Will‐Orrego, 2020b; Rahman et al., 2024).

Downstream signaling nodes relevant to RPE and dry AMD  
- Sirtuins. NAD⁺ is an obligate co-substrate for sirtuins; increased NAD⁺ drives sirtuin deacetylase/deacylase activities across SIRT1–7 with consequences for mitochondrial function, antioxidant defenses, DNA repair, and autophagy/mitophagy (Cimaglia et al., 2020). In aging RPE, NAD⁺ and SIRT1 decline in parallel; restoring NAD⁺ with NMN rescues SIRT1 protein and activity in vitro and in vivo, and reduces senescence markers p16^INK4a and p21^Waf/Cip1 (Jadeja et al., 2018). SIRT3, the mitochondrial sirtuin, maintains mitochondrial integrity by deacetylating and activating SOD2 and coordinating antioxidant responses, mitophagy, and UPR^mt; retinal NAD⁺ deficiency impairs SIRT3, linking NAD⁺ loss to mitochondrial vulnerability (Cimaglia et al., 2020; Lin, 2020). In a retinal detachment model, systemic NMN increased SIRT1 protein and HO‐1, with mechanistic in vitro data supporting a SIRT1 → HO‐1 protective axis (Chen et al., 2020).  
- AMPK and NAMPT/SIRT1 crosstalk. AMPK upregulates NAMPT expression and thus supports NAD⁺ salvage; reciprocally, SIRT1 deacetylates/stabilizes LKB1 to activate AMPK, forming a positive feedback loop that coordinates energy sensing and catabolic responses (Luján et al., 2022). In RPE senescence induced by NaIO₃, AMPK activation occurs downstream of ATP depletion and is modulated by NMN treatment, though overactivation of Sirt1 by a direct agonist (SRT1720) produced mitochondrial hyperfusion and stress signaling, arguing for physiological NAD⁺‐driven Sirt1 activation rather than pharmacologic overdrive (Ren et al., 2022).  
- Autophagy/mitophagy and mitochondrial biogenesis. SIRT1 promotes autophagosome formation and Rab7‐dependent autophagosome–lysosome fusion; together with AMPK, it enhances expression of mitophagy effectors (PINK1, Parkin) and co-activates PGC‐1α to drive mitochondrial biogenesis programs via transcriptional partners (NRF‐1, ERRα, PPARα) (Luján et al., 2022). Reviews and preclinical observations indicate that NAD⁺ repletion can restore mitophagy, activate UPR^mt, and normalize mitochondrial metabolic profiles, including evidence that “NAD⁺ inhibits the metabolic reprogramming of RPE cells in early AMD by upregulating mitophagy,” supporting the hypothesis that NMN-driven NAD⁺ gain could favorably reprogram RPE mitochondrial quality control (Rahman et al., 2024; Jadeja et al., 2020). In primary AMD donor RPE, NMN improved mitochondrial function in vitro; the same study used mitophagy reporter assays (mKeima-mito), underscoring the feasibility of measuring NMN’s effects on mitophagy in human RPE although specific NMN → mitophagy data were not detailed in the excerpt (Fisher et al., 2022; Ebeling et al., 2020).  
- PARP and CD38 as NAD⁺ sinks. DNA damage and SASP/inflammation drive PARP and CD38 activities that deplete NAD⁺; CD38 in particular contributes to age-related NAD⁺ decline and mitochondrial dysfunction and is induced by LXR signaling and SASP, creating a self-reinforcing senescence–NAD⁺ depletion loop (Cimaglia et al., 2020; Terao et al., 2024). In photoreceptor cells, PARP inhibition partially rescues viability under NAMPT inhibition, highlighting the vulnerability of NAD⁺ pools to consumption pathways and the value of reducing NAD⁺ sinks in parallel with NMN supplementation (Lin & Apte, 2018).  
- Inflammation and inflammasome. NAD⁺ augmentation via NMN or increased NAMPT/NAD⁺ can reduce NLRP3 inflammasome activation and downstream IL-1β/IL-18 via a SIRT1‐dependent mechanism in RPE aging models, pointing to anti‐inflammatory benefits of the NAMPT/NAD⁺/SIRT1 axis (Wu et al., 2021). In the Abca1/g1 macrophage senescence model of dry AMD, NAD⁺ repletion (NMN or CD38 inhibition) eliminated senescent macrophages and reversed drusenoid pathology and RPE functional deficits, extending the anti‐senescence and anti‐inflammatory reach of NAD⁺ augmentation to subretinal immune cells central to AMD pathobiology (Terao et al., 2024).

Critically, although TFEB (a master regulator of lysosomal biogenesis) and lysosomal proteases such as cathepsin D (CTSD) are highly relevant to RPE oxidative‐debris clearance in dry AMD, the provided literature does not demonstrate direct NMN‐induced TFEB activation, lysosomal acidification, or increased CTSD activity in RPE or retina; these remain plausible but unproven links in this context set (Falcão et al., 2025; Will‐Orrego, 2020a; Ebeling et al., 2020; Cimaglia et al., 2020).

Expected Effect  
Assay-level expectations in RPE models of dry AMD  
- NAD⁺ repletion in RPE. In ARPE‐19 and primary RPE, co‐treatment with NMN restores cellular NAD⁺ reduced by NAMPT inhibition, with partial to robust recovery depending on media and dosing; in ARPE‐19, FK866 sharply lowers NAD⁺/NADH and ATP, while NMN rescues both metrics and normalizes OXPHOS/glycolysis balance measured by Seahorse assays (Will‐Orrego, 2020b, 2020c). ARPE‐19 cells express NMN but not NR transporters, predicting superior uptake and intracellular conversion for NMN in this model (Will‐Orrego, 2020b). In NaIO₃‐stressed ARPE‐19, 800–1,000 µM NMN restored NAD⁺, mitochondrial membrane potential, and ATP, reduced ROS and DNA damage, and reduced senescence markers, with Sirt1 activity required for these benefits (Ren et al., 2022).  
- Sirtuin engagement. In FK866‐inhibited RPE, NMN co‐treatment restored SIRT1 protein and activity in vitro and in vivo; in photoreceptor injury, NMN increased retinal SIRT1 and induced HO-1, suggesting a SIRT1‐dependent antioxidant arm (Jadeja et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2020). In primary AMD donor RPE, NMN improved mitochondrial function, consistent with engagement of mitochondrial sirtuins and/or PGC-1α programs, though specific sirtuin readouts were not reported in that excerpt (Ebeling et al., 2020).  
- AMPK and mitophagy. AMPK will be sensitive to changes in ATP/AMP driven by mitochondrial recovery after NMN; in RPE senescence models, AMPK activation was observed under oxidant injury and modulated by NMN, and the NAD⁺/Sirt1/AMPK network is positioned to restore mitophagy via ULK1 activation, mTORC1 attenuation, and PINK1/Parkin induction (Ren et al., 2022; Luján et al., 2022). The hypothesis that NAD⁺ supplementation restores RPE metabolic dysfunction by inducing mitophagy is supported conceptually and by limited preclinical data cited for NAD⁺ effects on RPE mitophagy, making mitophagy flux (e.g., mKeima‐mito) a reasonable expected readout under NMN treatment (Jadeja et al., 2020; Fisher et al., 2022).  
- Autophagy-lysosome system and debris clearance. TFEB governs lysosomal biogenesis and function and is a rational target in dry AMD; Sirt1 and AMPK facilitate autophagosome formation and autophagosome–lysosome fusion (Rab7) and thus could secondarily enhance lysosomal throughput after NMN (Falcão et al., 2025; Luján et al., 2022). However, direct evidence that NMN increases lysosomal number, lysosomal acidification, TFEB nuclear translocation, or CTSD activity in RPE is not provided here; these are testable but as yet unvalidated expectations (Falcão et al., 2025; Will‐Orrego, 2020a). By contrast, NMN increased RPE phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments and restored actin‐mediated phagocytic machinery in vivo in the RCS rat, revealing a complementary route by which NMN could improve outer segment turnover independent of direct TFEB activation (Zhang et al., 2024).  
- Anti‐senescence and anti‐inflammatory effects. In RPE and subretinal myeloid cells, NMN suppresses senescence phenotypes and reduces inflammatory mediators, which should reduce SASP‐driven bystander stress on RPE and photoreceptors and may indirectly lower CD38‐mediated NAD⁺ depletion in the subretinal compartment (Jadeja et al., 2018; Terao et al., 2024).

Collectively, in an RPE assay aligned to dry AMD pathophysiology, NMN is expected to: (1) restore NAD⁺ and ATP; (2) engage Sirt1/Sirt3 and normalize mitochondrial function; (3) reduce senescence and inflammatory signaling; and (4) enhance POS phagocytosis via cytoskeletal machinery, with plausible but unproven enhancement of lysosomal biogenesis/function via Sirt1/AMPK-mediated autophagy pathways and TFEB cross-talk (Will‐Orrego, 2020b; Jadeja et al., 2018; Ren et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024; Falcão et al., 2025; Luján et al., 2022).

Assay design notes  
- Use ARPE‐19 and primary human RPE including AMD donor–derived cultures for translational relevance; confirm NMN uptake advantages versus NR in each model (Will‐Orrego, 2020b; Ebeling et al., 2020).  
- Quantify NAD⁺ by enzymatic assay, SIRT1 activity fluorometrically, mitochondrial ATP production via Seahorse ATP rate assay, and senescence markers (SA‐β‐gal, p16, p21) per established protocols (Jadeja et al., 2018; Will‐Orrego, 2020b).  
- Test TFEB nuclear translocation (immunocytochemistry), lysosomal mass (LAMP1/2), acidification (LysoSensor/LysoTracker), and cathepsin D maturation/activity explicitly, recognizing these endpoints are not yet shown for NMN in RPE (Falcão et al., 2025).  
- Assay mitophagy flux (mKeima‐mito), PINK1/Parkin expression, and autophagosome–lysosome fusion (LC3‐II, p62, Rab7 localization) (Fisher et al., 2022; Luján et al., 2022).  
- Measure RPE phagocytosis of POS and handling of phototoxic bisretinoids/lipofuscin surrogates to capture clearance outcomes (Zhang et al., 2024).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
- Strong disease‐mechanism alignment. Dry AMD is characterized by RPE mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, impaired autophagy/lysosomal clearance, and chronic para‐inflammation; age‐associated NAD⁺ decline in RPE and retina connects this pathobiology to a targetable metabolic axis (NAMPT/NAD⁺), and restoring NAD⁺ is causally protective in multiple retinal models (Jadeja et al., 2018; Cimaglia et al., 2020; Lin, 2020). In vitro and in vivo, NMN replenishes NAD⁺ in RPE and retina, rescues SIRT1 and mitochondrial function, and reduces senescence and inflammation—key drivers of RPE failure in dry AMD (Jadeja et al., 2018; Ren et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2020).  
- Direct RPE benefits with functional readouts. Under NAMPT inhibition and oxidant stress, NMN restores NAD⁺/ATP, normalizes OXPHOS/glycolysis, rescues RPE gene expression, lowers ROS and DNA damage, and blunts senescence markers; in RCS rats, NMN enhances RPE POS phagocytosis through a NAD⁺/GAPDH-actin axis (Will‐Orrego, 2020c; Ren et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024).  
- Reversal of senescence-driven dry AMD phenotypes in vivo. In the Abca1/g1 model where subretinal macrophage senescence drives SDDs and RPE dysfunction, NAD⁺ augmentation by NMN reduces SDDs, lipofuscin‐positive macrophages, and functional/structural pathology, demonstrating disease‐relevant efficacy in a model that captures hallmark dry AMD features (Terao et al., 2024).  
- Mechanistic tractability and biomarkerability. The NAMPT/NMN → NAD⁺ → sirtuin/AMPK network is well studied and offers clear pharmacodynamic biomarkers (NAD⁺ levels, SIRT1 activity, mitochondrial respiration) and pathophysiological readouts (senescence markers, mitophagy flux), supporting a high‐confidence translational path (Jadeja et al., 2018; Luján et al., 2022; Ebeling et al., 2020).  
- RPE uptake feasibility. ARPE‐19 transport NMN but not NR, favoring NMN for in vitro RPE work; broader literature identifies NMN transport and NR conversion pathways that together ensure NMN bioavailability in ocular cell types (Will‐Orrego, 2020b, 2020a).

Limitations and risks  
- TFEB/lysosome claims remain unproven for NMN in RPE. While TFEB‐driven lysosomal biogenesis/function is a compelling therapeutic target in dry AMD, direct evidence that NMN activates TFEB, increases lysosomal number/acidification, or enhances cathepsin D activity in RPE is not present in the provided literature; these are critical, testable hypotheses that should not be presupposed in development plans (Falcão et al., 2025; Will‐Orrego, 2020a; Ebeling et al., 2020; Cimaglia et al., 2020).  
- Context‐dependent AMPK/Sirt1 dynamics. In NaIO₃‐induced RPE senescence, AMPK activation accompanied ATP loss and was suppressed upon NMN treatment; further, pharmacologic Sirt1 overactivation caused mitochondrial hyperfusion and stress signaling, underscoring that pathway set points matter and that NMN’s benefits likely hinge on physiologic tuning rather than maximal activation (Ren et al., 2022). This complexity argues for careful titration of NMN exposure and for avoiding assumptions about AMPK activation being uniformly beneficial in all RPE contexts.  
- Model caveats. ARPE‐19 harbor genomic abnormalities and do not recapitulate all RPE functions (e.g., missing RPE65), and media composition strongly modulates metabolic readouts; corroboration in primary human RPE (including AMD donors) and in vivo models is essential (Will‐Orrego, 2020c; Will‐Orrego, 2020b; Ebeling et al., 2020).  
- Target competition with NAD⁺ sinks. PARPs and CD38 can drain NAD⁺; in photoreceptors and macrophages these sinks materially influence disease, suggesting that combining NMN with PARP/CD38 modulation may be superior to NMN alone in some settings, and that patient subgroups with high NAD⁺ consumption (e.g., inflammatory phenotypes) may require tailored strategies (Lin & Apte, 2018; Terao et al., 2024).  
- Translation and clinical data gaps. Although nicotinamide and NR have human safety/PK data, direct human ocular PK/PD and safety data for NMN are limited here, and no clinical testing in dry AMD is documented in the provided sources (Cimaglia et al., 2020; Rahman et al., 2024). Dosing translation must reconcile effective rodent exposures (150–1,000 mg/kg/day in various models) with human tolerability and ocular exposure (Lin, 2020; Chen et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2024).

Program implications and next steps  
- Mechanistic validation in RPE. Prioritize experiments that directly test the unproven parts of the hypothesis: TFEB activation (nuclear translocation), lysosomal biogenesis (LAMP1/2), acidification (LysoSensor), and cathepsin D maturation/activity after NMN in primary human RPE from AMD and non‐AMD donors, and in iPSC‐RPE, with parallel readouts of NAD⁺, SIRT1, SIRT3, and AMPK (Falcão et al., 2025; Ebeling et al., 2020).  
- Mitochondria–lysosome crosstalk. Quantify mitophagy flux (mKeima‐mito), autophagosome–lysosome fusion (Rab7 co‐localization), and PINK1/Parkin dynamics under NMN, leveraging assays already applied in AMD donor RPE studies (Fisher et al., 2022; Luján et al., 2022).  
- Functional clearance endpoints. Test NMN effects on RPE POS phagocytosis, lipofuscin/bisretinoid handling, and oxidative debris clearance in vitro and in vivo, building on the strong RCS rat evidence for NMN‐mediated enhancement of RPE phagocytosis via a GAPDH‐actin mechanism (Zhang et al., 2024).  
- Inflammation and senescence. Extend evaluation to subretinal macrophage phenotypes and SASP factors in RPE–macrophage co‐culture and in vivo, given NMN’s ability to eliminate senescent macrophages and reverse SDDs in the Abca1/g1 model (Terao et al., 2024).  
- Dosing/PD strategy. Calibrate NMN dose–response carefully: in ARPE‐19 and primary RPE, co‐treatment with 0.05–1.0 mM NMN rescued FK866‐induced NAD⁺ loss within 72 h, while NMN alone did not elevate ATP above baseline; in NaIO₃ ARPE‐19, 800–1,000 µM was required to adequately restore NAD⁺ (Jadeja et al., 2018; Will‐Orrego, 2020b; Ren et al., 2022). In vivo, effective rodent doses ranged from 150 mg/kg i.p. to 1,000 mg/kg oral depending on model and endpoint (Lin, 2020; Zhang et al., 2024). Engage PK/PD modeling to bridge to human‐equivalent dosing and to support ocular exposure.  
- Combination hypotheses. Consider rational combinations with PARP inhibition (to reduce NAD⁺ consumption) or CD38 inhibition in inflammatory phenotypes, consistent with partial rescue by PARP inhibitors in NAMPT‐deficient contexts and CD38’s role in macrophage senescence (Lin & Apte, 2018; Terao et al., 2024). Also consider pairing with agents that more directly activate TFEB where justified, while monitoring for unintended mTORC1 perturbations (Falcão et al., 2025).

Conclusion  
NMN is a biologically credible and mechanistically tractable candidate for dry AMD focused on RPE rescue through NAD⁺ repletion and sirtuin‐centric mitochondrial restoration, with strong preclinical support in RPE senescence, oxidant injury, and phagocytosis assays, and compelling in vivo evidence in retinal injury and senescence‐driven dry‐AMD–like pathology (Jadeja et al., 2018; Ren et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024; Terao et al., 2024). The hypothesized downstream impacts on TFEB, lysosomal biogenesis, acidification, and cathepsin D activity are plausible given the Sirt1/AMPK/autophagy network architecture but remain unvalidated in RPE after NMN and should be explicitly de‐risked experimentally before clinical translation (Falcão et al., 2025; Luján et al., 2022; Ebeling et al., 2020). Strengths include a coherent disease‐mechanism target, measurable PD, multi‐compartment benefits (RPE, photoreceptors, subretinal macrophages), and favorable uptake in RPE models; weaknesses include reliance on models with limitations, the need to control for NAD⁺ sinks and pathway set points, and gaps in lysosome‐specific mechanistic evidence and human ocular PK/PD (Will‐Orrego, 2020b, 2020c; Lin & Apte, 2018; Rahman et al., 2024). Overall, NMN merits advancement to a rigorous preclinical validation package focused on lysosome‐mitophagy endpoints in primary human RPE and AMD‐relevant in vivo models, with a translational plan that integrates mitochondrial function, senescence/inflammation control, and debris clearance readouts as co‐primary pharmacology objectives (Ebeling et al., 2020; Fisher et al., 2022; Terao et al., 2024).
