Proposal for GDC-0941 (Pictilisib; Selleckchem S1066)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
GDC-0941 (pictilisib) is a synthetic, orally bioavailable, ATP-competitive, pan–class I phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) inhibitor discovered through structure-guided medicinal chemistry at Genentech as an optimization beyond early tool compounds and the dual PI3K/mTOR inhibitor PI-103, with substantially improved selectivity for class I PI3Ks and favorable pharmacokinetics (PK) suitable for systemic dosing in humans (Garces, n.d.; Zhao et al., 2017; Li et al., 2024). At the biochemical level, pictilisib exhibits low-nanomolar potency against p110α and p110δ, with activity across class I isoforms (representative IC₅₀s: α ≈ 3 nM, β ≈ 33 nM, δ ≈ 3 nM, γ ≈ 75 nM) and weak activity on mTOR (~0.58 μM), consistent with selective targeting of class I PI3Ks over mTOR kinase (Huang et al., 2023; Garces, n.d.; Josephs & Sarker, 2016; Zhao et al., 2017). Co-crystal structural analysis shows an ATP-mimetic thieno[3,2-d]pyrimidine core in which the morpholine oxygen donates a hinge hydrogen bond (Val882), the indazole engages an affinity pocket (Asp841/Tyr867), and a methanesulfonyl-piperazine tail projects toward solvent; these interactions underpin its potency and selectivity within the class I PI3K ATP site (PDB 3DBS) (Garces, n.d.). Pictilisib belongs to the pan-class I PI3K inhibitor class, a therapeutic category widely advanced in oncology to suppress the PI3K→AKT→mTOR signaling axis that governs cell growth, survival, and metabolism; multiple agents in this class have reached clinical testing or approval, providing a clear translational precedent for pathway engagement and safety management in humans (Schmitz et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024).

Therapeutic History  
The development history of pictilisib is anchored in oncology, where first-in-human Phase I trials established a recommended Phase II dose of 330 mg once daily, dose-proportional PK (Tₘₐₓ ≈ 2 h, t₁/₂ ≈ 13–24 h), and robust pharmacodynamic (PD) target engagement—up to ~90% inhibition of phosphorylated AKT (Ser473) in platelet-rich plasma within 1–3 h of dosing and suppression of p-AKT and p-S6 in tumor biopsies at sufficient exposure (AUC > 20 μM·h) (Sarker et al., 2015; Mishra et al., 2021). Across Phase I combination studies, pictilisib showed a manageable safety profile and some antitumor activity, with common adverse events including nausea, diarrhea, rash, fatigue, hyperglycemia, and cytopenias; dose-limiting toxicity in some settings was grade 3 maculopapular rash (Mishra et al., 2021; Josephs & Sarker, 2016; Sarker et al., 2015). Although pathway suppression was clear, randomized combination trials in breast and lung cancer variably met efficacy endpoints and broader development was limited by tolerability in multi-drug regimens and modest efficacy as monotherapy—experience that nonetheless provides clinically validated PK/PD tools and class-effect safety benchmarks (Mishra et al., 2021; Gao et al., 2017).

No preclinical or clinical use of pictilisib or other class I PI3K inhibitors has been reported for dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) or RPE-centered retinal disease models in the provided literature, and a recent review emphasizes that no ongoing clinical trials directly target autophagy in AMD, underscoring an evidence gap for PI3K inhibitors in this indication (Blasiak et al., 2025). By contrast, direct downstream pathway modulation in RPE—mTORC1 inhibition with rapamycin—has shown that activating the lysosome–TFEB axis reduces POS-derived autofluorescent granules in vitro in a hydrolase-dependent manner, but clinical testing in advanced AMD returned limited efficacy and adverse effects, illustrating both the mechanistic plausibility of modulating this pathway and translational challenges (Falcão et al., 2024). Collectively, the therapeutic history supports that class I PI3K inhibitors achieve systemic PI3K pathway inhibition in humans with tractable but nontrivial toxicities; however, there is no published RPE- or AMD-focused body of work with pictilisib to date in the provided context (Sarker et al., 2015; Mishra et al., 2021; Blasiak et al., 2025).

Mechanism of Action  
Class I PI3Ks phosphorylate PIP₂ to PIP₃, recruiting PH-domain effectors including PDK1 and AKT to the plasma membrane, where AKT is activated by phosphorylation at Thr308 (PDK1) and Ser473 (mTORC2) (Huang et al., 2023). Activated AKT phosphorylates multiple substrates that converge on mTORC1 activation (via TSC2/PRAS40), leading to phosphorylation of S6K1 and 4E-BP1 to promote protein synthesis and suppress autophagy, thereby integrating growth-factor status with anabolic programs (Huang et al., 2023; Castel et al., 2021). In retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), AKT/mTOR signaling is an established regulator of cell proliferation and autophagy; mTORC1 activation inhibits autophagy by blocking ULK1 signaling and autolysosomal biogenesis, whereas rapamycin-mediated mTORC1 inhibition activates autophagy and preserves photoreceptor function in preclinical models (Chang et al., 2015). PI3K inhibition upstream of AKT is thus expected to reduce PIP₃ generation, limit AKT membrane recruitment/activation, and secondarily depress mTORC1 signaling in RPE (Castel et al., 2021; Chang et al., 2015).

PI3K→AKT signaling intersects directly with TFEB/TFE3—the master transcription factors of lysosomal biogenesis and the CLEAR regulon—through both mTORC1-dependent and mTORC1-independent routes (Falcão et al., 2024; Ghosh et al., 2024). Nutrient-driven mTORC1 activity at the lysosome phosphorylates TFEB/TFE3 to retain them in the cytosol; conversely, mTORC1 inhibition permits TFEB nuclear translocation and induction of lysosomal/autophagy genes (Falcão et al., 2024). Importantly, AKT2 directly phosphorylates TFEB (at Ser467) and TFE3 (Ser321), promoting 14-3-3 binding and cytoplasmic sequestration independent of mTORC1, thereby repressing CLEAR gene expression and lysosomal programs in RPE (Ghosh et al., 2023; Ghosh et al., 2024). In AMD-relevant contexts—human dysmorphic macular RPE, CFH risk-allele iPSC-RPE, and Akt2 RPE-specific knock-in mice—AKT2 upregulation increases TFEB/TFE3 phosphorylation, reduces lysosomal hydrolases (cathepsin D/L) and CLEAR target expression, impairs autophagic flux, and shifts autophagy toward secretory pathways characterized by increased FKBP51/SNAP23, autophagosome fusion with the plasma membrane, and extracellular release of IL-1β/HMGB1 (Ghosh et al., 2023; Ghosh et al., 2024). TFEB rescue via a phospho-deficient TFEB-S467A mutant or pharmacologic TFEB activation with trehalose restores cathepsin D/L and autophagy genes, normalizes autophagosome–lysosome fusion, and improves RPE structure in AMD-like mouse models, establishing TFEB/TFE3 as actionable nodes downstream of AKT2 in RPE (Ghosh et al., 2023; Ghosh et al., 2024).

SIRT5 and mitochondrial metabolism integrate with AKT2–TFEB signaling in RPE. SIRT5 interacts with AKT2 and reduces lysine malonylation on AKT2, modulating AKT2 activity; AKT2, via inhibition of PGC-1α, downregulates SIRT5, creating a bidirectional circuit that affects TFEB-dependent lysosomal function and mitochondrial respiration (Ghosh et al., 2023). AKT2 upregulation impairs mitochondrial respiration (maximal and ATP-linked), which is rescued by SIRT5, linking mitochondrial–lysosomal crosstalk to RPE proteostasis (Ghosh et al., 2023). Functionally, failure of the AKT2/SIRT5/TFEB axis elevates secretory autophagy and likely contributes to drusen pathogenesis by exporting inflammatory cargo (Ghosh et al., 2023).

These mechanistic data converge with mTORC1→TFEB observations in POS-fed RPE models. Synchronous POS phagocytosis transiently activates mTORC1 early, with TFEB protein increasing but remaining phosphorylated during late maturation, indicating a bottleneck at TFEB activation (Falcão et al., 2024). Pharmacologic mTORC1 inhibition with rapamycin after AFGs have formed lowers POS-derived autofluorescence by ~30% in hfRPE/ARPE-19, reduces pS6, decreases the pTFEB/TFEB ratio, increases LAMP1, and requires active lysosomal hydrolases: cathepsin D knockout or protease/lipase inhibition abolishes the rapamycin effect (Falcão et al., 2024). Human AMD RPE exhibits hyperphosphorylated TFEB with decreased nuclear localization and lower cathepsin D/L protein levels and activities, consistent with impaired lysosome proteolysis; constitutively active TFEB boosts CLEAR genes, including hydrolases and V-ATPase subunits (e.g., ATP6V0A1), supporting enhanced acidification capacity (Falcão et al., 2025).

Pictilisib’s proximal pharmacology—ATP-competitive inhibition of class I PI3Ks—has been shown to produce substantial pathway suppression in human tissues (reduced pAKT, reduced pS6) and in tumor models; such upstream inhibition is expected to depress both AKT→mTORC1 signaling and AKT2-mediated TFEB phosphorylation, potentially enabling TFEB/TFE3 nuclear translocation and restoration of CLEAR programs in RPE (Sarker et al., 2015; Mishra et al., 2021; Castel et al., 2021; Ghosh et al., 2024). Two caveats are central. First, class III PI3K/VPS34 activity is required for autophagosome nucleation; thus class I–selective inhibition (as with pictilisib) is mechanistically appropriate, whereas class III inhibition would be detrimental to autophagy flux (Wang et al., 2023). Second, while AKT inhibitors (e.g., MK-2206) and TFEB activators (trehalose) have directly increased autophagy and TFEB nuclear translocation in RPE, no direct evidence of class I PI3K inhibitors reproducing these effects in RPE has been reported in the provided literature (Karim et al., 2020; Blasiak et al., 2025).

Expected Effect  
In RPE cell assays designed to model dry AMD–relevant lysosomal stress (e.g., POS feeding, A2E/oxidant exposure, or CFH risk-allele iPSC-RPE), pictilisib is expected to reduce PI3K-dependent PIP₃ generation, lowering membrane recruitment and phosphorylation of AKT, thereby secondarily suppressing mTORC1 and directly relieving AKT2-mediated phosphorylation of TFEB/TFE3 (Huang et al., 2023; Castel et al., 2021; Ghosh et al., 2024). Mechanistically, this should dephosphorylate TFEB/TFE3, promote nuclear translocation, and activate the CLEAR transcriptional program to increase components of lysosomal biogenesis and function (e.g., LAMP1/2, cathepsin D/L, and V-ATPase subunits), thereby enhancing degradative capacity and autophagic flux (Falcão et al., 2024; Falcão et al., 2025; Falcão et al., 2024).

Operationally testable expected effects include:  
• Signaling: decreased pAKT (Ser473) and pS6 (Ser235/236) within hours of dosing in RPE, mirroring PD seen in tumors and platelet-rich plasma in human trials, establishing target engagement (Sarker et al., 2015; Mishra et al., 2021).  
• TFEB/TFE3 activation: reduction in pTFEB/TFEB ratio, increased nuclear TFEB/TFE3, and induction of CLEAR genes; in RPE systems, TFEB activation correlates with increased LAMP1 and rescue of lysosomal hydrolases and autophagy mediators (e.g., ATG9B), as seen upon TFEB S467A expression or trehalose treatment (Falcão et al., 2024; Ghosh et al., 2023; Ghosh et al., 2024).  
• Lysosomal function: increased abundance and maturation of cathepsin D (pro→intermediate→mature chain) and cathepsin L, and improved degradative flux, consistent with observations that AMD RPE shows diminished cathepsin levels/activities and that hydrolase function is required for rapamycin-mediated clearance of POS-derived AFGs (Falcão et al., 2025; Falcão et al., 2024).  
• Autophagic flux: increased LC3-II turnover (± bafilomycin A₁), reduced p62/SQSTM1 accumulation, restored autophagosome–lysosome fusion (e.g., GFP-LC3 and LysoTracker/LAMP1 colocalization), and decreased accumulation of double-membrane autophagosomes on EM, reversing the AKT2-driven flux block (Ghosh et al., 2023; Ghosh et al., 2024).  
• POS-derived autofluorescence: reduction in POS-dependent autofluorescent granules in preventive and resolving paradigms, analogous to mTORC1 inhibition or TFEB activation effects; efficacy will depend on intact lysosomal hydrolase activity (Falcão et al., 2024).  
• Secretory autophagy: decreased FKBP51/SNAP23 elevation, reduced plasma-membrane fusion of autophagosomes, and lower extracellular release of IL-1β and HMGB1 from RPE, mirroring effects seen with SIRT5 co-expression or AKT2 inhibition (Ghosh et al., 2023; Ghosh et al., 2024).  
• Acidification: although lysosomal pH has not been directly measured after class I PI3K inhibition in RPE, TFEB activation increases expression of V-ATPase subunits, and improved hydrolase activities imply normalization of the acidic lumen; direct pH assays (LysoSensor/LysoTracker) should be incorporated because efficacy of rapamycin/TFEB approaches depends on intact lysosomal acid hydrolases (Falcão et al., 2025; Falcão et al., 2024).

These expectations rest on the well-documented presence and dysregulation of the AKT2→TFEB/TFE3→CLEAR axis in RPE and the dependence of POS clearance on mTOR/TFEB-governed lysosomal function (Ghosh et al., 2024; Falcão et al., 2024). AKT2 is upregulated in macular RPE from AMD donors and in AMD-like mouse models, and RPE-specific Akt2 overexpression is sufficient to induce AMD-like pathology, supporting target relevance in the cell type and disease context of interest (Ghosh et al., 2023; Ghosh et al., 2024). Because PI3K is upstream of AKT, class I PI3K inhibition should, in principle, reduce AKT2 activity and thereby favor TFEB activation; however, no direct studies in the provided literature demonstrate pictilisib (or any class I PI3K inhibitor) inducing TFEB nuclear translocation or improving cathepsin D maturation/activity in RPE, so these are testable, evidence-generating predictions rather than established facts (Blasiak et al., 2025; Chang et al., 2015; Karim et al., 2020).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
• Mechanistic alignment with disease biology: Dry AMD features impaired RPE autophagy–lysosome function, diminished cathepsin D/L levels/activities, and TFEB hyperphosphorylation with reduced nuclear TFEB, all of which converge on lysosomal insufficiency and accumulation of lipofuscin-like material and extracellular drusen; upstream PI3K inhibition is a coherent strategy to relieve AKT2- and mTORC1-mediated repression of TFEB/TFE3 and restore CLEAR-driven lysosomal biogenesis and flux (Falcão et al., 2025; Falcão et al., 2024; Ghosh et al., 2024).  
• Drug tractability and translational readiness: Pictilisib achieves robust systemic target engagement in humans (pAKT/pS6 suppression), has well-characterized PK/PD and class-effect safety, and is chemically and biophysically optimized, enabling accelerated preclinical testing and dosage/formulation exploration for ocular delivery or low systemic exposure strategies (Sarker et al., 2015; Mishra et al., 2021; Garces, n.d.).  
• Selectivity profile appropriate for autophagy enhancement: Because autophagosome nucleation requires class III PI3K/VPS34, class I selectivity of pictilisib avoids this liability while suppressing AKT→mTOR signaling and AKT2-dependent TFEB phosphorylation, in contrast to nonselective PI3K inhibition which would be deleterious (Wang et al., 2023; Castel et al., 2021).

Risks and Gaps  
• No direct RPE/AMD data with class I PI3K inhibitors: In contrast to clear benefits from rapamycin and TFEB activation, the literature presented contains no direct evidence that class I PI3K inhibition, or pictilisib specifically, improves TFEB activation/nuclear entry, lysosomal biogenesis, cathepsin D maturation/activity, lysosomal acidification, autophagy flux, or POS handling in RPE; thus, the central hypothesis remains unvalidated experimentally in the disease-relevant cell type (Blasiak et al., 2025; Chang et al., 2015; Falcão et al., 2024). By comparison, AKT inhibition (MK-2206) and trehalose have been shown to increase autophagy and nuclear TFEB in RPE, and AKT2 inhibitors reduced lipid/apoE in CFH(H/H) RPE, but these are not PI3K inhibitors and may differ in cellular selectivity and compensatory signaling (Karim et al., 2020; Ghosh et al., 2024).  
• Complexity of autophagy modulation and context dependence: Autophagy is a double-edged sword with biphasic responses to stress; overactivation or mistimed activation could exacerbate RPE stress or impair other retinal cell types, and some autophagy inhibitors display retinal toxicity (e.g., chloroquine class), emphasizing the need for precise control and safety profiling (Blasiak et al., 2025; Kaarniranta et al., 2013). Rapamycin has shown only limited clinical efficacy in advanced AMD despite mechanistic benefits in vitro, illustrating translational friction between pathway modulation and clinical outcomes (Falcão et al., 2024).  
• Safety, delivery, and exposure management: Systemic class I PI3K inhibition induces hyperglycemia, rash, and gastrointestinal toxicities; while these were generally manageable, ocular delivery strategies (intravitreal or periocular) and ocular pharmacokinetics for pictilisib are uncharacterized in the provided literature, and tissue-specific PD (e.g., drug levels at the RPE/Bruch’s complex) is likely to determine efficacy and safety (Josephs & Sarker, 2016; Sarker et al., 2015; Mishra et al., 2021). Tissue PD variability seen in tumors versus blood suggests careful intratissue PD assays are necessary (Sarker et al., 2015).  
• Isoform and cell-type specificity: AKT2 is the isoform implicated in RPE TFEB repression and secretory autophagy; upstream PI3K isoform contributions in RPE are not defined here, and pan-class I inhibition could affect neighboring retinal cells, choroidal endothelium, and immune cells, potentially altering retinal immunity and angiogenesis in unintended ways (Ghosh et al., 2024; Castel et al., 2021). Given the evidence favoring AKT2-selective inhibition or direct TFEB activation in RPE models, PI3K-level intervention may be broader than necessary and carry off-target tissue consequences (Ghosh et al., 2024; Karim et al., 2020).  
• Acidification endpoint uncertainty: The hypothesis specifically posits enhanced lysosomal acidification, yet none of the provided sources report direct lysosomal pH measurements after class I PI3K inhibition in RPE; while CLEAR induction can increase V-ATPase subunits and rapamycin-mediated clearance requires active hydrolases, direct acidification effects remain to be demonstrated (Falcão et al., 2025; Falcão et al., 2024; Blasiak et al., 2025).

Go/No-Go Evidence Plan  
Given the strong mechanistic rationale but absence of direct RPE data, a staged validation program is warranted:  
– In vitro RPE target engagement and TFEB activation: Demonstrate pictilisib decreases pAKT (Ser473) and pS6 in primary human fetal RPE, ARPE-19, and iPSC-RPE, and increases nuclear TFEB/TFE3 with induction of CLEAR genes (CTSD, CTSL, LAMP2, ATP6V0A1) assessed by Western, RT-qPCR, and ChIP-qPCR (Falcão et al., 2024; Falcão et al., 2025; Ghosh et al., 2024).  
– Lysosome function readouts: Quantify cathepsin D maturation and enzymatic activity, LAMP1/2 abundance, and lysosomal pH using ratiometric probes; include hydrolase inhibition controls to assess dependence analogous to the rapamycin data (Falcão et al., 2024).  
– Autophagic flux and modality: Assess LC3-II turnover (± bafilomycin A₁), p62 dynamics, autolysosome number (LC3–LAMP1 colocalization), and EM confirmation; evaluate secretory-autophagy markers (FKBP51, SNAP23) and extracellular cargo (HMGB1, IL-1β) to test reversal of the AKT2-driven phenotype (Ghosh et al., 2023; Ghosh et al., 2024).  
– Functional disease readouts: Test POS-dependent autofluorescence reduction in preventive and resolving paradigms and POS degradation assays; evaluate lipid/apoE accumulation in CFH(H/H) iPSC-RPE and Cryba1 cKO explants to compare with reported trehalose and AKT2 inhibitor effects (Falcão et al., 2024; Ghosh et al., 2023; Ghosh et al., 2024).  
– In vivo and delivery: Establish ocular PK and tolerability for local delivery (intravitreal/periocular) in rodents, then test efficacy in RPE-driven AMD-like models (Akt2 KI, Cryba1 cKO, NaIO₃) with TFEB activation, lysosomal and functional endpoints (ERG/SD-OCT) (Ghosh et al., 2024; Ghosh et al., 2023; Falcão et al., 2025).  
– Benchmarking: Include rapamycin (mTORC1 inhibition), MK-2206 (AKT inhibition), and trehalose (mTOR-independent TFEB activation) comparators to position PI3K inhibition within the therapeutic landscape (Falcão et al., 2024; Karim et al., 2020; Ghosh et al., 2023).

Conclusion  
Pictilisib offers a mechanistically coherent, drug-like approach to modulate an RPE pathway of clear pathogenic relevance—AKT2/mTOR-mediated repression of TFEB/TFE3 and the CLEAR network—backed by robust human PK/PD experience and a selectivity profile compatible with enhancing autophagy without impeding VPS34-dependent nucleation (Ghosh et al., 2024; Sarker et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2023). However, the absence of direct evidence that class I PI3K inhibition rectifies TFEB activity, lysosomal hydrolase function (notably cathepsin D), lysosomal acidification, autophagic flux, or POS clearance in RPE is a critical gap that precludes immediate advancement; by contrast, downstream interventions (rapamycin, TFEB activation, AKT2 inhibition) already demonstrate these benefits in RPE models (Falcão et al., 2024; Ghosh et al., 2024). On balance, pictilisib is plausible and testable but not yet justified for translational progression without focused RPE and ocular studies; an AKT2-selective inhibitor or TFEB-directed strategies may offer a higher probability of effect with narrower network perturbation, and should be run in parallel as mechanistic benchmarks (Ghosh et al., 2024; Karim et al., 2020). If the proposed RPE experiments confirm that pictilisib restores TFEB-driven lysosomal programs, improves cathepsin D maturation/activity, normalizes autophagy flux, and reduces POS-derived autofluorescence with acceptable ocular tolerability, the candidate would merit advancement to in vivo ocular efficacy testing; absent such confirmation, resources should pivot to agents with demonstrated RPE TFEB engagement (Ghosh et al., 2024; Falcão et al., 2024).
