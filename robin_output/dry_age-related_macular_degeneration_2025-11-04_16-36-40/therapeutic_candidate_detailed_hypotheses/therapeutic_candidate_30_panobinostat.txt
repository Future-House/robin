Proposal for Panobinostat (LBH589; Selleckchem S1030)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Panobinostat (LBH589) is a fully synthetic small‐molecule compound originally developed by Novartis and characterized chemically as a cinnamic hydroxamic acid analogue formulated as its anhydrous lactate salt. This molecule belongs to the hydroxamate class of histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACi), a group of compounds designed to inhibit the enzymatic activity of HDACs that regulate chromatin remodeling and transcription by catalyzing the removal of acetyl groups from histone proteins. Panobinostat’s chemical structure, defined by its hydroxamic acid moiety, is tailored to chelate the zinc ion in the catalytic domain of HDAC enzymes, thereby preventing deacetylation reactions that normally lead to a condensed chromatin state. In its initial development, panobinostat was formulated in multiple dosage forms, including oral hard capsules containing 10, 15, or 20 mg doses and earlier intravenous preparations, illustrating its versatility in administration routes for different clinical indications (Van Veggel, Westerman, & Hamberg, 2018; Libby et al., 2015; Dokmanovic, Clarke, & Marks, 2007). Compounds of this class have historically been employed in oncology, where their capacity to induce hyperacetylation of histone and non-histone substrates leads to altered gene transcription, cell cycle arrest, differentiation, and eventual apoptosis in malignant cells (Van Veggel et al., 2018; Cea et al., 2013). Moreover, the broad-spectrum inhibition of HDAC isoforms—spanning Classes I, II, and IV—distinguishes panobinostat as a “pan-HDAC inhibitor” with high potency at nanomolar concentrations, a characteristic that underlies its robust pharmacological profile and its subsequent regulatory approvals for certain cancer indications (Morabito et al., 2016).

Therapeutic History:  
Since its discovery, panobinostat has undergone extensive preclinical and clinical evaluation primarily targeting multiple myeloma and various hematologic malignancies. Early phase clinical trials established that, as a single agent, panobinostat had modest activity; however, its combination with proteasome inhibitors such as bortezomib and corticosteroids like dexamethasone produced synergistic effects that translated into significant improvements in progression‐free survival in relapsed and refractory multiple myeloma patients. This positive clinical efficacy culminated in its approval by both the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) in 2015 for the treatment of patients with multiple myeloma who had received at least two prior regimens (Van Veggel et al., 2018; Libby et al., 2015; Bondarev et al., 2021). Beyond multiple myeloma, panobinostat has been scrutinized in a variety of clinical settings—including cutaneous T-cell lymphoma, acute myeloid leukemia, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, and several solid tumor malignancies—to explore its antitumor potential in combination therapies, given its capacity to potentiate the cytotoxic effects of other agents by modulating epigenetic regulation (Cea et al., 2013; Cinek & Karalı, 2021; Libby et al., 2015). Despite its demonstrated efficacy in cancer, there is no recorded use of panobinostat in ophthalmological diseases such as dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) or in any similar degenerative condition. The extensive body of clinical research on HDAC inhibitors has predominantly focused on antineoplastic applications, and while some mechanistic parallels exist between the cellular processes targeted in cancer and those disrupted in degenerative retinal diseases, panobinostat’s utility in dry AMD remains an uncharted territory that has thus far not been explored in preclinical or clinical trials (Dokmanovic, Clarke, & Marks, 2007; Morabito et al., 2016; Libby et al., 2015).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, panobinostat exerts its biological effects primarily through the inhibition of histone deacetylase enzymes. The compound’s hydroxamic acid group binds with high affinity to the zinc ion located in the catalytic domain of HDAC enzymes, thereby blocking the deacetylation of lysine residues on histone tails as well as on several non-histone proteins. This blockade causes an accumulation of acetylated histones, which in turn results in a more open chromatin conformation that facilitates enhanced transcription of a multitude of genes, including those pivotal for cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and cellular differentiation (Benedetti, Conte, & Altucci, 2015; Morabito et al., 2016). Beyond its classical role in modifying histone tail acetylation, panobinostat’s broad inhibition across HDAC classes implies that it can modulate the acetylation status of key transcription factors, chaperone proteins (such as heat shock protein 90), and structural proteins like α-tubulin. This wide-ranging impact on protein acetylation alters the functional activity of numerous signaling pathways and cellular processes, contributing to anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic outcomes in cancer cells (Morabito et al., 2016). The pleiotropic nature of its action is further evidenced by the induction of autophagy in various tumor models, an effect thought to be mediated by the increased acetylation and transcriptional activation of master regulators of lysosomal biogenesis such as transcription factor EB (TFEB). Although the provided literature does not explicitly detail TFEB upregulation in response to panobinostat, the general tendency of HDAC inhibitors to enhance acetylation-dependent transcription supports the hypothesis that TFEB and its downstream lysosomal genes could be upregulated—thereby promoting autophagy and lysosomal clearance pathways (Cinek & Karalı, 2021; Libby et al., 2015). This complex interplay of molecular events not only underpins panobinostat’s established antitumor activity but also provides a rationale for its possible repurposing in degenerative diseases where enhanced cellular clearance mechanisms may be therapeutic (Van Veggel et al., 2018; Benedetti, Conte, & Altucci, 2015).

Expected Effect in Dry Age-Related Macular Degeneration (AMD):  
Dry age-related macular degeneration is characterized by the progressive dysfunction of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), which is responsible for the maintenance of photoreceptor health and the clearance of cellular debris. A central pathological hallmark of dry AMD is the accumulation of drusen deposits beneath the RPE, partly attributable to impaired lysosomal degradation and autophagy. In the context of this disease, the hypothesis proposes that localized ocular administration of panobinostat could inhibit HDAC activity in RPE cells, leading to a global increase in histone acetylation. This epigenetic modulation is expected to activate acetylation-dependent transcription factors such as TFEB—a master regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy—that, once activated, upregulates a network of genes involved in lysosomal acidification and cathepsin D expression (Van Veggel et al., 2018; Cinek & Karalı, 2021). Enhanced expression of these lysosomal components is anticipated to restore or improve the degradative capacity of the RPE, facilitating the clearance of drusen deposits and ameliorating the pathological buildup of cellular waste characteristic of dry AMD.

In preclinical oncology studies, panobinostat has consistently induced autophagy and promoted lysosomal function in cancer cells, leading to growth inhibition and apoptosis. If similar cellular responses occur in RPE cells, then panobinostat may stimulate a beneficial autophagic flux that targets accumulated proteins and lipids for degradation (Libby et al., 2015). The expression of cathepsin D—a lysosomal protease critical for the degradation of photoreceptor outer segments and other metabolic substrates—is well documented in RPE cells, and its activity is tightly regulated by lysosomal pH; thus, any intervention that improves lysosomal acidification is likely to have a favorable impact on RPE homeostasis (Mottamal et al., 2015; Morabito et al., 2016). These findings support the proposed mechanism whereby panobinostat’s inhibition of HDACs may indirectly enhance the transcriptional activation of TFEB, culminating in improved lysosomal biogenesis and function. Moreover, the broad HDAC inhibitory profile of panobinostat ensures that multiple redundant deacetylation pathways are targeted simultaneously, offering the potential for robust and sustained activation of the cellular clearance machinery compared to more selective agents (Morabito et al., 2016; Carafa, Nebbioso, & Altucci, 2011).

From a translational perspective, it is crucial to recognize that systemic toxicity has been an important limitation of panobinostat in oncologic settings, where adverse effects such as gastrointestinal disturbances, thrombocytopenia, and cardiotoxicity (QT interval prolongation) have been observed. However, the concept of localized ocular dosing—via intravitreal injection or topical delivery—could potentially mitigate these risks by achieving high local drug concentrations with minimal systemic exposure (Libby et al., 2015; Karol et al., 2020). In the specific context of dry AMD, where chronic, localized pathology is present in the retina, targeted delivery of panobinostat could allow for the modulation of RPE cellular processes without triggering widespread adverse reactions. This targeted approach aligns with current trends in ocular pharmacotherapy, which increasingly favor local drug delivery systems designed to maximize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity (Bondarev et al., 2021; Van Veggel et al., 2018). Ultimately, the expected outcome in the proposed assay is that panobinostat treatment will lead to measurable upregulation of lysosomal gene expression, restoration of lysosomal acidification, and enhanced activity of cathepsin D within RPE cells, thereby reducing the burden of drusen deposits and potentially slowing the progression of dry AMD (Cea et al., 2013; Libby et al., 2015).

Overall Evaluation:  
The repurposing of panobinostat for dry age-related macular degeneration presents an intriguing and biologically plausible strategy that leverages the compound’s well-documented HDAC inhibitory properties to modulate critical intracellular degradation pathways. One of the foremost strengths of this therapeutic candidate is its potent, broad-spectrum inhibition of HDAC enzymes at low nanomolar concentrations, which has been repeatedly demonstrated in various preclinical and clinical studies in oncology (Van Veggel et al., 2018; Morabito et al., 2016). The resultant hyperacetylation of histones and non-histone proteins not only promotes cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in malignant cells but also facilitates the transcriptional activation of autophagy- and lysosome-related genes—a pathway that could be harnessed to restore faithful lysosomal degradation in retinal pigment epithelial cells affected in dry AMD (Benedetti, Conte, & Altucci, 2015; Cinek & Karalı, 2021).

In terms of mechanistic specificity, the broad inhibitory profile of panobinostat could be seen as an advantage in the context of dry AMD, where multiple deacetylation pathways may contribute to the impaired clearance of cellular debris. By simultaneously inhibiting several HDAC isoforms, panobinostat is likely to achieve a more robust activation of TFEB and other transcription factors central to lysosomal biogenesis, thereby amplifying the cellular clearance mechanisms required to remove drusen deposits (Morabito et al., 2016; Carafa, Nebbioso, & Altucci, 2011). Additionally, the existing clinical experience with panobinostat in oncology provides a rich repository of pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, and safety data that can inform dose optimization and risk management strategies—albeit with the caveat that ocular tissues may respond differently to HDAC inhibition than cancer cells (Bondarev et al., 2021; Libby et al., 2015).

Nevertheless, several significant weaknesses must be acknowledged in evaluating panobinostat as a candidate for dry AMD treatment. Chief among these is the potential for systemic toxicity. Panobinostat’s clinical utilization in cancer has been limited by adverse side effects including gastrointestinal toxicity, thrombocytopenia, and cardiac complications, and although localized ocular delivery may attenuate these concerns, the safety profile of panobinostat in retinal tissues remains largely unexplored (Libby et al., 2015; Karol et al., 2020). Moreover, the pharmacokinetic behavior of panobinostat when administered locally to the eye is unknown; challenges such as drug solubility, stability, and diffusion across retinal layers must be addressed rigorously in preclinical models. There is also the possibility of off-target effects in retinal cells that could exacerbate retinal stress or contribute to unwanted inflammation, particularly given the complex interplay of epigenetic regulation in non-dividing, highly specialized cells like the RPE (Cea et al., 2013; Benedetti, Conte, & Altucci, 2015).

Another point of concern is the translational leap from oncology to ophthalmology. While the mechanistic rationale for using panobinostat to enhance autophagy and lysosomal function is supported by its effects in cancer cells, RPE cells possess distinctive metabolic and regulatory pathways that may not respond identically to HDAC inhibition. The degree to which TFEB and other autophagy or lysosomal genes are modulated by panobinostat in RPE cells has not yet been directly demonstrated, and significant differences in chromatin structure and gene expression profiles between cancer cells and differentiated RPE cells could impact both efficacy and safety outcomes (Morabito et al., 2016; Carafa, Nebbioso, & Altucci, 2011). In addition, the chronic nature of dry AMD necessitates long-term treatment, and the potential cumulative effects of continuous HDAC inhibition in ocular tissues raise concerns regarding retinal integrity, visual function, and overall ocular health over extended periods—a consideration that remains entirely speculative without targeted preclinical studies (Dokmanovic, Clarke, & Marks, 2007; Morabito et al., 2016).

From a drug development perspective, the strategy of repurposing panobinostat for dry AMD is compelling because it integrates a well-characterized molecular mode of action with an unmet clinical need. However, critical translational challenges must be overcome. These include reformulating panobinostat for localized ocular delivery, establishing appropriate dosing regimens that maximize local efficacy while minimizing toxicity, and conducting comprehensive preclinical evaluations in relevant in vitro and in vivo models of dry AMD. The potential benefit of enhancing lysosomal clearance through increased TFEB activation is a particularly attractive aspect of this proposal, as impaired autophagy and lysosomal dysfunction are recognized contributors to the pathogenesis of dry AMD (Cinek & Karalı, 2021; Morabito et al., 2016). Nonetheless, a careful balance must be maintained, as excessive activation of autophagy pathways in non-proliferative tissues may inadvertently trigger cell death or exacerbated stress responses.

In summary, while panobinostat’s established efficacy in oncology and its well-documented ability to induce broad epigenetic changes provide a strong scientific foundation for its potential repurposing, a number of critical challenges remain. On the positive side, panobinostat is among the most potent HDAC inhibitors available, capable of eliciting profound changes in gene expression that might restore lysosomal function in RPE cells and thereby clear accumulated drusen deposits. In addition, modern advances in ocular drug delivery—such as intravitreal injections, biodegradable implants, or topical formulations enhanced by nanocarrier systems—offer promising routes to achieve the necessary drug concentrations in the retina while mitigating systemic exposure and toxicity (Van Veggel et al., 2018; Morabito et al., 2016).

Conversely, the risk of adverse effects related to HDAC inhibition, the unknown ocular pharmacokinetics, and the potential differences in cellular responses between cancerous and retinal cells represent significant hurdles that must be rigorously addressed through targeted preclinical research. Furthermore, given that panobinostat’s clinical track record is largely confined to oncology, its application in a degenerative ocular disease such as dry AMD would require new, dedicated studies to validate its safety, efficacy, and optimal delivery modality in this context (Libby et al., 2015; Afifi et al., 2015).

Overall, panobinostat emerges as a promising yet challenging candidate for repurposing in dry age‐related macular degeneration. Its ability to robustly inhibit multiple HDAC isoforms and thereby potentially activate TFEB and lysosomal gene networks aligns well with the therapeutic strategy of improving autophagic clearance in RPE cells. However, substantial gaps in knowledge remain regarding its direct effects on retinal cells and the feasibility of its long-term use in an ocular setting. Consequently, while the mechanistic rationale is strong and the preclinical evidence from oncology provides useful insights, extensive additional research is needed to assess the translational potential, determine safe and efficacious ocular dosing parameters, and ultimately validate this approach in models that reliably mimic the pathophysiology of dry AMD (Prince et al., 2009; Morabito et al., 2016; Benedetti, Conte, & Altucci, 2015; Bondarev et al., 2021).

In conclusion, panobinostat’s repurposing for dry AMD is grounded in a robust understanding of epigenetic regulation and lysosomal biology. Its established clinical use in oncology offers a wealth of pharmacological data and a precedent for potent HDAC inhibition, while the hypothesis that HDAC inhibitors can reactivate latent clearance pathways in RPE cells is both innovative and scientifically plausible. The strengths of panobinostat lie in its potency, broad-spectrum activity, and documented ability to elicit autophagic responses, which collectively suggest that, under the right conditions and with a carefully optimized ocular delivery system, it could address one of the major pathophysiological mechanisms underlying dry AMD. Nonetheless, the weaknesses, including potential off-target toxicity, uncertain ocular pharmacokinetics, and the need for a comprehensive reevaluation of its effects in non-malignant, differentiated cell types, must be thoroughly investigated before advancing into clinical studies. Therefore, data from directed preclinical experiments employing RPE cell cultures and animal models of dry AMD will be essential in determining whether the benefits of panobinostat can be safely and effectively harnessed to treat this common cause of vision loss in the elderly (Srinivas, 2017; Van Veggel et al., 2018; Bondarev et al., 2021; Carafa, Nebbioso, & Altucci, 2011).
