Proposal for NSC19723 (Cathepsin D activity enhancer; custom synthesized)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
NSC19723 is presented by the team as a custom-synthesized small-molecule enhancer of cathepsin D (CTSD) activity, hypothesized to bind the zymogen (pro-CTSD) and accelerate its maturation within retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) lysosomes to restore proteolysis and debris clearance in dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD). No peer-reviewed publications or preprints describing NSC19723’s structure, discovery, selectivity, or in vivo performance are present in the provided literature corpus; accordingly, this evaluation treats NSC19723 as a theoretical representative of the drug class “CTSD activity enhancers” and assesses the therapeutic hypothesis against the current substrate-specific and disease-relevant CTSD/RPE biology (Ng et al., 2024; Sharif, 2015; Liu, 2021; Buniello et al., 2025).

The stated drug class is mechanistically distinct from upstream autophagy enhancers or lysosomal pH modulators: a CTSD activity enhancer would operate at the level of CTSD protein maturation/activity, ideally in acidic lysosomes, rather than by globally raising autophagic flux or indirectly modulating lysosomal acidification (Frost et al., 2014; Boya et al., 2023; Ng et al., 2024). CTSD itself is an aspartic endoprotease synthesized as a pre-proenzyme with an N-terminal signal peptide (for ER entry) and a propeptide region that supports folding and prevents premature activity; post-translational maturation occurs in late endosomes/lysosomes after mannose-6-phosphate (M6P)-mediated trafficking, where acidic pH supports autocatalysis and protease processing to the mature, active two-chain enzyme (Sharif, 2015). The RPE expresses CTSD at high levels, with evidence for enriched CTSD protein/activity in central/macular RPE relative to periphery, consistent with the macula’s heavy daily photoreceptor outer segment (POS) phagocytic load that relies on lysosomal proteolysis (Sharif, 2015; Pay, 2017). These features nominate CTSD as a plausible therapeutic target for restoring RPE degradative capacity; however, a specific CTSD maturation enhancer such as NSC19723 has not been characterized in the provided literature, so all compound-level conclusions must remain contingent on experimental validation (Ng et al., 2024; Buniello et al., 2025).

Therapeutic History  
No published biochemical, veterinary, or clinical use of NSC19723 is documented in the provided sources, and the name does not appear in the referenced literature; consequently, there are no prior data on this molecule in dry AMD or retinal disease within the provided corpus (Buniello et al., 2025). By contrast, there is considerable disease-relevant literature on CTSD biology in RPE and on pharmacological strategies that indirectly restore CTSD function by correcting upstream lysosomal defects. In patient-derived RPE from Stargardt disease (STGD1, ABCA4 mutations), lysosomal pH is elevated, CTSD accumulates in immature forms, and CTSD proteolytic activity is reduced; pharmacologic re-acidification of lysosomes (e.g., via P2Y₁₂ antagonism with ticagrelor) reverses these defects by lowering lysosomal pH, reducing the immature pro-CTSD pool, and restoring CTSD proteolytic activity and POS degradation in vitro and in vivo (RPE cells and Abca4−/− mice) (Ng et al., 2024). While STGD1 is not AMD, these data show that CTSD maturation/activity in human RPE is drug-modifiable and that improving CTSD function can improve degradative endpoints relevant to lipofuscin-associated disease biology, a pathophysiologic axis shared with dry AMD (Ng et al., 2024; Boya et al., 2023).

In the RPE/AMD literature, multiple lines of evidence support CTSD as a major lysosomal protease necessary for POS degradation and proteostasis. CTSD deficiency or pharmacologic inhibition impairs POS proteolysis, provokes accumulation of debris reminiscent of drusen, and is linked to retinal dysfunction in animal models, reinforcing its centrality to RPE homeostasis (Mahmud, 2019). Advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), which accumulate with age and in AMD, downregulate CTSD activity or expression in RPE and exacerbate lysosomal/autophagic failure and lipofuscin formation, further tying CTSD deficits to AMD-like phenotypes (Sharif, 2015; Mahmud, 2019). More broadly, lysosomal/autophagic insufficiency in RPE is mechanistically and pathologically linked to dry AMD: decreased degradative capacity leads to lipofuscin accumulation, lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP), DAMP release, inflammasome activation (e.g., NLRP3), and eventual RPE atrophy that drives photoreceptor degeneration; re-acidifying lysosomes and strengthening lysosomal function ameliorate several of these pathologies in vitro and in vivo (Choi, 2019; Frost et al., 2014; Boya et al., 2023).

A second therapeutic precedent relevant to “enhancing CTSD function” is enzyme augmentation. Recombinant pro-CTSD administration has been reported to correct proteolysis and autophagy defects in neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis (CLN10/CTSD deficiency), and intravitreal enzyme replacement strategies in other lysosomal storage diseases (e.g., TPP1 in CLN2) have preserved retinal structure/function in preclinical models, supporting feasibility of increasing lysosomal enzyme function in ocular tissues (Liu, 2021). These studies, while not in AMD and not small molecules, validate the general concept that augmenting CTSD can rescue lysosomal degradative failure in retina-derived tissues.

In summary, there is no therapeutic history for NSC19723 itself in the provided sources; however, there is an increasingly robust therapeutic precedent that pharmacological manipulation which restores CTSD maturation/activity in RPE improves degradative capacity and disease-relevant readouts in lipofuscin-accumulating retinal disorders, and that enzyme augmentation of lysosomal hydrolases can be effective in ocular degenerations driven by lysosomal insufficiency (Ng et al., 2024; Liu, 2021).

Mechanism of Action  
CTSD biosynthesis and maturation provide the mechanistic backdrop for a CTSD maturation enhancer. CTSD is synthesized as pre-procathepsin D with a signal peptide (for ER translocation) and a propeptide that maintains the zymogen in an inactive, folding-competent state; the protein acquires N-linked glycans and an M6P tag in the Golgi for M6P-receptor–mediated trafficking to late endosomes/lysosomes, where acidic pH facilitates removal of the propeptide, proteolytic processing, and generation of the mature, active enzyme (heavy and light chains) (Sharif, 2015). As an aspartic protease, CTSD employs two Asp residues to activate and position a water molecule for nucleophilic attack on the scissile peptide bond, a chemistry that requires acidic conditions for optimal catalysis; in neutral or alkaline environments, activity falls sharply, explaining why lysosomal alkalinization in disease states compromises both maturation and steady-state activity (Sharif, 2015; Ng et al., 2024).

In RPE disease models, immature and intermediate CTSD forms accumulate when lysosomal pH is elevated, and total CTSD protein may paradoxically rise even as proteolytic activity falls, illustrating a maturation bottleneck rather than a transcriptional deficit; pharmacological re-acidification reduces immature CTSD, increases the mature enzyme, and restores proteolytic activity in RPE (Ng et al., 2024). Functionally, impaired CTSD maturation/activity correlates with delayed degradation of internalized POS and accumulation of aggregation-prone substrates such as α-synuclein, consistent with CTSD’s principal role in RPE proteolysis of rhodopsin and POS proteins; CTSD is reported to account for the majority of rhodopsin degradation in RPE and exhibits far higher proteolytic activity than cathepsin B against POS substrates (Ng et al., 2024). The RPE’s reliance on phagolysosomal degradation of POS and basal autophagy for proteostasis means that even partial reductions in CTSD activity can precipitate progressive accumulation of lipofuscin-like material and lysosomal residual bodies, which in turn further impair lysosomes and create a feedback loop of increasing oxidative and inflammatory stress central to dry AMD pathophysiology (Choi, 2019; Frost et al., 2014; Sharif, 2015).

The proposed mechanism for NSC19723—direct binding to pro-CTSD to accelerate maturation—aligns with the above biology and would target the specific rate-limiting step observed in RPE lysosomes under stress: conversion of immature pro-CTSD to the mature, active enzyme in the acidic lumen. If achieved without broad, off-target activation of other proteases or perturbation of lysosomal pH/ion homeostasis, this mechanism would distinguish NSC19723 from autophagy inducers (e.g., rapamycin) or pH re-acidifiers (e.g., ticagrelor), while being convergent on the same functional outcome: restored CTSD proteolysis and lysosomal degradative capacity (Frost et al., 2014; Ng et al., 2024).

Mechanistic cross-talk and constraints that are relevant for a CTSD maturation enhancer include:  
- pH dependence: CTSD maturation and catalysis require acidic lysosomes; in alkaline lysosomes (e.g., due to bisretinoid accumulation), pro-CTSD processing is impaired even if total CTSD protein increases, so a maturation accelerator may be insufficient without concurrent restoration of lysosomal pH in some disease states (Ng et al., 2024).  
- Protease networks: While CTSD is an aspartic protease, lysosomal proteases co-regulate one another’s maturation and substrate turnover; for example, denatured cathepsin L can be a substrate for CTSD, and changes in cysteine cathepsins (B/L/S) alter autophagic flux and POS degradation, implying that selective enhancement of CTSD maturation must be interpreted in the context of broader protease network homeostasis (Mahmud, 2019; Sharif, 2015).  
- Endogenous inhibitors: Cystatin C potently inhibits cysteine cathepsins (B, H, L, S) and is abundant in RPE; although it does not inhibit CTSD (aspartic protease), changes in cystatin C with age and AMD suggest that protease–inhibitor balance in the lysosome and extracellular space is dynamic and may modulate the consequences of altering one protease’s activity (Myerscough, 2015; Sharif, 2015).  
- Lysosomal membrane integrity: LMP releases cathepsins to the cytosol, where they can trigger apoptosis/inflammation; any intervention that modifies lysosomal protease activity must be vetted for unintended effects on LMP susceptibility or cytosolic protease activity under stress (Boya et al., 2023; Mahmud, 2019).  
- Extracellular exposure: RPE can secrete cathepsins; most cathepsins are inactivated at neutral pH, but acidic niches or heparan/ECM interactions can stabilize activity, raising potential off-tissue effects if a compound perturbs trafficking/secretion (Sharif, 2015).

Collectively, the mechanistic plausibility that a small molecule could stabilize a conformation of pro-CTSD that accelerates propeptide removal and activation in acidic lysosomes is strong, and a favorable on-target pharmacodynamic effect can be anchored to clearly measurable, disease-relevant biochemical endpoints in RPE (Ng et al., 2024). The key risk is that without correction of lysosomal pH, pro-CTSD processing bottlenecks may persist; therefore, a CTSD maturation enhancer may be best deployed in lysosomes that are sufficiently acidic or in combination with pH-restoring strategies (Ng et al., 2024).

Expected Effect  
Dry AMD pathophysiology features RPE lysosomal-autophagic insufficiency leading to accumulation of POS-derived material, lipofuscin, and AGEs, heightened oxidative stress, inflammasome activation, and progressive RPE atrophy; restoring lysosomal proteolysis is predicted to mitigate these processes (Choi, 2019; Frost et al., 2014; Boya et al., 2023). In RPE, CTSD is a principal protease responsible for POS/rhodopsin degradation, and its activity declines with aging and under stressors relevant to AMD (AGEs, lipids/oxidation, lysosomal alkalinization), producing immature CTSD accumulation and reduced proteolytic capacity (Mahmud, 2019; Sharif, 2015; Ng et al., 2024). Therefore, a compound that accelerates pro-CTSD maturation to increase mature CTSD abundance/activity in acidic lysosomes is expected to:

- Increase CTSD catalytic activity in RPE lysosomes, measurable by fluorogenic substrate assays and activity-based probes, with corresponding decrease in immature/intermediate CTSD species and increase in mature, active CTSD on immunoblot and imaging (Ng et al., 2024).  
- Improve rate and completeness of POS degradation in RPE pulse–chase assays (e.g., rhodopsin clearance), reduce accumulation of lysosomal residual bodies, and decrease autofluorescent lipofuscin-like material formation in POS-challenged RPE under AGE/oxidative stress conditions (Ng et al., 2024; Frost et al., 2014; Sharif, 2015).  
- Reduce intracellular burden of aggregation-prone CTSD substrates (e.g., α-synuclein) and attenuate proteostasis stress, potentially normalizing vesicle trafficking defects tied to aggregate accumulation (Ng et al., 2024).  
- Rebalance autophagic flux by removing a degradative bottleneck at the lysosome, observable as improved degradation of autophagic cargo rather than simply altered LC3 abundance; careful flux assays with lysosome–autophagosome fusion inhibitors (e.g., bafilomycin A1) should be used for interpretation (Frost et al., 2014; Sharif, 2015).  
- Decrease downstream inflammatory signaling associated with lysosomal debris accumulation, including reduced NLRP3 inflammasome activation and cytokine release during chronic stress paradigms implicated in AMD pathogenesis (Choi, 2019; Boya et al., 2023).  
- With longer-term dosing, reduce subcellular and extracellular deposit biogenesis (lipofuscin, drusen-like material), preserve RPE morphology/mitochondrial health, and sustain photoreceptor function in models where lysosomal degradation is limiting, as seen when CTSD function is restored by lysosomal re-acidification (Boya et al., 2023; Ng et al., 2024).

The target is well expressed in the cell type of interest: CTSD is abundant in RPE, enriched in central/macular RPE, and directly linked to RPE’s POS degradation function; its activity is reduced by aging and AGE exposure, both highly relevant to dry AMD (Sharif, 2015; Pay, 2017; Mahmud, 2019). In lipofuscin-associated retinal disease models, restoring lysosomal acidity to rescue CTSD maturation/activity improved RPE degradative function and reduced pathological autofluorescence, underscoring the expected benefits of directly increasing CTSD maturation/activity via a selective small molecule (Ng et al., 2024).

Experimental considerations for robust readouts in RPE:  
- Biochemical: ratio of pro-/intermediate/mature CTSD by immunoblot in lysosomal fractions; CTSD activity in intact live cells and in isolated lysosomes at matched pH; selectivity profiling against other cathepsins (B/L/S) to confirm the putative specificity reported by the team; lysosomal pH measurements to control for off-target alkalinization/acidification effects (Ng et al., 2024; Sharif, 2015).  
- Functional degradative flux: POS pulse–chase assays (rhodopsin clearance kinetics), autophagy flux assays (LC3-II, p62/SQSTM1 degradation with and without bafilomycin A1), α-synuclein aggregation/clearance; quantify autofluorescence as a proxy for lipofuscin-like material (Ng et al., 2024; Frost et al., 2014; Sharif, 2015).  
- Cell health/inflammation: LMP susceptibility (galectin-3 puncta, cytosolic cathepsin immunostaining), NLRP3 inflammasome activation (caspase-1 activity), ROS and mitochondrial integrity (Boya et al., 2023; Choi, 2019).  
- Disease relevance: verify effects in hiPSC-RPE derived from dry AMD patients, which display altered lysosomal protease processing (e.g., immature CTSD increases under oxidative stress) and increased stress susceptibility; extend to macular-enriched RPE phenotypes given regional CTSD enrichment (Voisin et al., 2019; Sharif, 2015).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
- Strong target–disease mechanistic rationale: CTSD is the principal lysosomal protease for RPE POS protein degradation, with reported responsibility for the majority of rhodopsin proteolysis and markedly higher activity than cathepsin B; CTSD deficiency or inhibition impairs POS degradation and produces drusen-like debris, and genetic or pharmacologic CTSD impairment causes retinal dysfunction, aligning CTSD activation with a disease-modifying mechanism in dry AMD (Ng et al., 2024; Mahmud, 2019).  
- Convergent therapeutic precedent: Restoring lysosomal pH in patient-derived and mouse RPE models rescues CTSD maturation and activity, improves POS degradation, reduces lipofuscin-like autofluorescence, and protects photoreceptors, demonstrating that enhancing CTSD function is sufficient to improve disease-relevant outcomes in lipofuscin-associated retinal pathology (Ng et al., 2024).  
- Clear, measurable pharmacodynamic biomarkers: Mature CTSD abundance, CTSD activity, lysosomal pH, POS clearance, and lipofuscin/autofluorescence changes provide a rigorous framework for early de-risking of NSC19723 and for prioritizing compound optimization (Ng et al., 2024).  
- Disease context supports target engagement benefits: RPE lysosomal dysfunction, autophagy/heterophagy deficits, lipofuscin/AGE accumulation, inflammasome activation, and progressive atrophy are central features of dry AMD; a CTSD maturation enhancer directly addresses the degradative bottleneck at the lysosome (Choi, 2019; Frost et al., 2014; Boya et al., 2023).

Risks and gaps  
- Absence of public data on NSC19723: No structural, selectivity, potency, PK/PD, ocular disposition, or toxicology data are available in the provided corpus; thus, claims of pro-CTSD binding, selectivity for CTSD over other cathepsins, and in vivo rescue in CTSD-deficient retina remain unverified by the sources provided (Buniello et al., 2025). This is the major program risk at present.  
- Lysosomal pH constraint: If lysosomal alkalinization is a primary driver of immature CTSD accumulation (as in STGD1 and arguably in stressed AMD RPE), a maturation accelerator may not overcome the pH-dependent processing barrier; efficacy may require sufficient lysosomal acidity or combination with pH-restoring interventions (Ng et al., 2024).  
- Protease network and safety considerations: Enhancing lysosomal protease activity can exacerbate LMP-driven cytotoxicity under oxidative or lipofuscin stress; careful LMP and inflammasome assessments are required, alongside confirmation that NSC19723 does not perturb trafficking/secretion to increase extracellular protease activity where pH can be locally acidic (Boya et al., 2023; Sharif, 2015).  
- Disease heterogeneity and target linkage: Although CTSD biology is compelling in RPE and in lipofuscin-associated pathology, Open Targets curates only a low-confidence literature-based association between CTSD and AMD, reflecting limited direct genetic or clinical targeting data in AMD; translational success will depend on demonstrating benefit in dry AMD–relevant human RPE models and in vivo models that recapitulate AMD’s lysosomal/autophagic deficits (Buniello et al., 2025; Inana et al., 2018; Frost et al., 2014).  
- Off-target and network effects: Crosstalk between cathepsins (e.g., CTSD processing of denatured cathepsin L, altered cathepsin L/C3/NF-κB signaling), and modulation by AGEs/oxidative stress and endogenous inhibitors necessitate comprehensive off-target profiling and immune readouts to ensure that CTSD-specific maturation enhancement does not dysregulate broader proteostasis/inflammatory networks in RPE or at the RPE–Bruch’s membrane (BrM) interface (Sharif, 2015; Mahmud, 2019).

Program implications and recommended next steps  
Given the robust mechanistic case for CTSD restoration in RPE and the successful pharmacologic rescue of CTSD maturation/activity via lysosomal re-acidification in human patient–derived RPE, a selective CTSD maturation enhancer is a rational, high-potential approach for dry AMD—provided the compound is real, selective, and effective in acidic lysosomes (Ng et al., 2024). To advance NSC19723, the following de-risking package is critical:  

- Biochemical validation: Demonstrate pro-CTSD binding and acceleration of maturation at acidic pH using purified pro-CTSD and lysosomal-mimetic conditions; quantify catalytic activation and processing kinetics; confirm no activation or inhibition of cathepsins B/L/S and other lysosomal hydrolases (Sharif, 2015).  
- Cellular validation in RPE: In primary human RPE/hiPSC-RPE (including dry AMD lines), show increased mature CTSD and activity without altering lysosomal pH, improved POS degradation and reduced autofluorescence under chronic POS/AGE stress, restored clearance of α-synuclein aggregates, and normalization of autophagic flux using proper flux assays (Frost et al., 2014; Ng et al., 2024; Sharif, 2015).  
- Safety pharmacology in RPE: Demonstrate no increase in LMP susceptibility or NLRP3 activation under oxidative/bisretinoid stress; characterize secretion/trafficking effects and exclude extracellular protease activation under BrM-like conditions (Boya et al., 2023; Sharif, 2015).  
- In vivo proof of concept: In models featuring RPE lysosomal dysfunction and lipofuscin burden, measure retinal/RPE CTSD maturation/activity, RPE autofluorescence, drusen-like material, photoreceptor survival, and visual function; compare with or add to pH-reacidifying strategies (Ng et al., 2024).

Overall, while a CTSD maturation enhancer is scientifically well grounded for RPE-centered degenerations and could represent a differentiated, direct-restoration modality versus pH modulators or broad autophagy inducers, the current file lacks any external validation for NSC19723. Progressing NSC19723 is justified only with rapid, rigorous confirmation of selectivity, on-target activity in RPE lysosomes, and favorable safety in stressed RPE systems that model dry AMD’s lysosomal/autophagic failure (Frost et al., 2014; Boya et al., 2023; Choi, 2019).

In summary, the therapeutic concept—enhancing CTSD maturation/activity to restore RPE lysosomal proteolysis—has high mechanistic plausibility and encouraging precedent from pharmacologic re-acidification studies, but the specific candidate NSC19723 remains unsubstantiated in the provided literature and requires foundational biochemical and cellular validation to warrant preclinical development in dry AMD (Ng et al., 2024; Buniello et al., 2025).
