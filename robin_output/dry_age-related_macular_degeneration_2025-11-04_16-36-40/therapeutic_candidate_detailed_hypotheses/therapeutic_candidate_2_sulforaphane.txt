Proposal for Sulforaphane (Sigma S4441)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Sulforaphane (SFN) is a naturally occurring isothiocyanate derived from glucoraphanin in cruciferous vegetables via myrosinase-mediated hydrolysis; as an electrophilic small molecule, SFN was identified through its unusually potent induction of phase II detoxification enzymes and antioxidant defenses in mammalian tissues via the KEAP1–NFE2L2/NRF2 stress-response axis (classically termed “phase 2 induction”) (Qin, 2007; Hyttinen et al., 2019). Isothiocyanates and related electrophiles covalently modify reactive cysteine residues on KEAP1, disrupting the KEAP1–NRF2 “hinge-and-latch” ubiquitin ligase complex and stabilizing NRF2 to drive ARE-dependent transcription of cytoprotective enzymes including NQO1, GCLM, HO-1, and glutathione pathway genes (Hyttinen et al., 2019; Qin, 2007). Beyond canonical NRF2 activation, SFN is now recognized to engage lysosome-centered adaptive transcription by inducing nuclear translocation and activation of TFEB/TFE3 through a mild, ROS-triggered Ca²⁺–calcineurin pathway that is largely MTOR-independent, thereby upregulating CLEAR-network genes controlling lysosome and autophagy biogenesis (Li et al., 2021). In ocular contexts, SFN has been used experimentally as a cytoprotective agent in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) models subjected to oxidative or phototoxic stress, and as a modulator of autophagy markers, situating SFN within a dual-modality class that can activate antioxidant defenses and augment lysosome-autophagy function (Yang et al., 2021; Li et al., 2021).

Therapeutic History  
SFN and other NRF2 activators have been widely studied as cytoprotective chemopreventive agents and as protectants in oxidative-stress paradigms across tissues; in RPE models, SFN has repeatedly demonstrated NRF2-dependent protection and modulation of stress and quality-control pathways, although direct clinical testing in dry AMD is not reported in the provided literature (Qin, 2007; Liu et al., 2016). In ARPE-19 cells exposed to blue light, SFN promoted NRF2 nuclear translocation, induced HO-1 and glutathione production, reduced oxidative stress and inflammation, and its cytoprotective effect was blocked by the NRF2 inhibitor ML385, indicating a mechanistic dependence on NRF2; SFN also increased LC3B-II and p62, and the autophagy inhibitor 3-MA blunted its protective action, linking SFN’s benefit to autophagy engagement in this RPE stress model (Yang et al., 2021). In a separate ARPE-19 inflammatory model (TGF-β), SFN (alone or combined with vitamin D) lowered ROS and VEGF and restored epithelial junction markers; in that system, the authors did not detect NRF2 pathway modulation and instead implicated ERK1/2 downregulation, underscoring context-dependence of the upstream signaling mediating protection (Bergandi et al., 2024). Reviews and experimental summaries further report that SFN protects retinal/epithelial tissues and can recover cone function in an NRF2-dependent manner in mice undergoing RPE oxidative stress, although primary data for those specific in vivo outcomes are not detailed in the provided text (Falcão et al., 2025). Mechanistically adjacent precedents in RPE include robust protection by the electrophilic NRF2 activator RTA 408 in primary human RPE subjected to H₂O₂, with genetic evidence for NRF2 dependence; while not SFN, this validates the KEAP1–NRF2 axis as druggable and protective in human RPE (Liu et al., 2016). With respect to lysosome-autophagy axes central to dry AMD, TFEB activation (via rapamycin or constitutively active TFEB) reduces photoreceptor outer segment–derived autofluorescent granules in RPE POS-feeding models, and TFEB-directed interventions (including trehalose or constitutively nuclear TFEB) restore cathepsin D/L abundance and activity and increase LAMP1 in AMD-relevant human RPE models, establishing the lysosome/transcriptional arm as therapeutically tractable in RPE; however, these TFEB-driven rescues have not been directly demonstrated with SFN in RPE in the supplied literature (Falcão et al., 2024; Ghosh et al., 2024).

Mechanism of Action  
- NRF2 engagement via KEAP1 cysteine sensing: SFN, as an electrophile, reacts with specific redox-sensitive cysteines on KEAP1 (notably Cys151 among others), impairing KEAP1-mediated ubiquitination of NRF2 and permitting NRF2 stabilization, nuclear accumulation, and heterodimerization with small Maf proteins to bind AREs and induce a large gene battery of antioxidant, detoxification, and proteostasis regulators (e.g., NQO1, HO-1, GCLM, GSTs), increasing GSH and related defenses (Hyttinen et al., 2019; Qin, 2007). In RPE and ocular tissues, SFN and related NRF2 activators have induced phase II enzymes and HO-1 and conferred resistance to photooxidative injury, with biochemical readouts that include increased NQO1, thioredoxin, GSH, and reduced ROS (Qin, 2007; Zhang et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2021). Aging impairs inducible NRF2 signaling in RPE under oxidative challenges (e.g., sodium iodate), supporting the rationale for exogenous NRF2 activation in older RPE (Sachdeva et al., 2014).

- TFEB/TFE3 activation and lysosome-autophagy biogenesis: SFN provokes a mild increase in ROS that sensitizes lysosomal MCOLN1/TRPML1 channels to agonist-evoked Ca²⁺ release; resultant cytosolic Ca²⁺ engages calcineurin to dephosphorylate TFEB, enabling its nuclear translocation and activation of CLEAR-network transcription independently of acute MTORC1 inhibition (TFEB activation is blocked by antioxidants, BAPTA-AM, and attenuated by FK506) (Li et al., 2021). The SFN-induced TFEB program upregulates autophagy and lysosome genes (SQSTM1, ULK1, LC3) and multiple lysosomal components including LAMP1, v-ATPase subunits (e.g., ATP6V1H), hydrolases (CTSB, TPP1), and MCOLN1, and increases LysoTracker and LAMP1 signals, consistent with enhanced lysosomal mass and acidotropic probe accumulation (Li et al., 2021). Critically, TFEB is necessary for SFN-induced upregulation of NFE2L2/NRF2 and for SFN-mediated suppression of ROS in mitochondrial and lysosomal stress models, documenting a TFEB→NRF2 dependency in SFN’s antioxidant protection (Li et al., 2021). Reviews synthesizing TFEB–NRF2 crosstalk add that TFEB can directly or indirectly promote NRF2 by repressing its E3 ligase (DCAF11) and via p62-mediated KEAP1 sequestration; reciprocally, NRF2 activation can induce TFEB-related gene sets and promote autophagosome–lysosome fusion, indicating bidirectional reinforcement (Falcão et al., 2025).

- RPE-specific TFEB/lysosome biology relevant to AMD: In human RPE-derived ARPE-19 cells, TFEB controls lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy; nutrient deprivation and lysosomal stress increase TFEB and MITF and induce CLEAR genes including LAMP1, CTSD, MCOLN1, and autophagy factors (BECN1), with calcineurin-mediated dephosphorylation regulating nuclear translocation (Pan et al., 2019). In AMD-relevant RPE models, TFEB activation (via mTORC1 inhibition or constitutively active TFEB) reduces POS-dependent lipofuscin-like autofluorescent granules through a hydrolase-dependent mechanism, and inhibition or genetic loss of CTSD increases autofluorescent burden and abrogates rapamycin’s benefit, tying TFEB-driven rescue to functional lysosomal proteolysis (Falcão et al., 2024). In human AMD donor RPE and CFH Y402H iPSC-RPE, elevated p-TFEB/p-TFE3 associates with reduced CTSD/CTSL abundance and activity; interventions that restore TFEB nuclear activity (AAV2-TFEB-S467A) or TFEB activators (trehalose) rescue CTSD/CTSL levels and activities and increase LAMP1, while AKT2 inhibition counters secretory-autophagy bias and improves degradative flux (Ghosh et al., 2024; Ghosh et al., 2023).

- Cathepsin D biochemistry and lysosomal pH: Cathepsin D (CTSD) is an abundant lysosomal aspartyl protease synthesized as an inactive proenzyme that undergoes maturation and activation in the acidic late endosome/lysosome; cathepsins generally require a reducing, mildly acidic lumen and lose activity at neutral pH, linking lysosomal acidification (v-ATPase function) tightly to CTSD proteolysis (Sharif, 2015). In AMD, lysosomal dysfunction (including LMP, impaired proton-pump function, and hydrolase inhibition by lipofuscin-derived oxidized products) is prominent, and CTSD leakage and reduced hydrolase activity are reported in AMD RPE, mechanistically supporting the therapeutic objective to normalize lysosomal pH and restore cathepsin activity (Falcão et al., 2025).

- Safety and pathway caveats: Constitutive or excessive activation of NRF2 or TFEB can carry oncogenic or maladaptive risks, and chronic KEAP1–NRF2 perturbation can yield accumulation of non-degradative organelles, emphasizing the need for cell-specific, dose-titrated modulation and rigorous safety assessment in the AMD context (Falcão et al., 2025).

Taken together, SFN stands out for concurrent engagement of NRF2 antioxidant programs and TFEB-driven lysosome/autophagy biogenesis—mechanisms that align with central oxidative and degradative defects in dry AMD RPE—yet it is important to note that direct biochemical evidence demonstrating SFN-mediated normalization of lysosomal pH and restoration of CTSD maturation/activity in RPE under AMD-relevant oxidative stress is not present in the supplied literature (Falcão et al., 2025; Li et al., 2021).

Expected Effect  
Under the stated hypothesis—SFN engages NRF2 and TFEB in oxidatively stressed RPE to normalize lysosomal pH and restore CTSD proteolysis—the expected proximate and downstream effects in rigorously designed assays are:  
- Antioxidant response and ROS control: SFN should increase NRF2 nuclear accumulation and ARE target expression (HO-1, NQO1, GCLM, Trx1, GSH biosynthesis enzymes) in RPE cells subjected to oxidative paradigms (e.g., blue light, sodium iodate, hydroquinone, or POS feeding with photooxidative stress), lowering ROS burden and oxidative damage; this is strongly supported in ARPE-19 under blue light (and in other epithelial systems), and the effect shows functional dependence on NRF2 (ML385-sensitive) (Yang et al., 2021; Qin, 2007). Aging-associated defects in inducible NRF2 signaling suggest exogenous NRF2 activation may be especially beneficial in aged RPE, further motivating this outcome measure (Sachdeva et al., 2014).

- TFEB activation and lysosome-autophagy program: SFN should induce TFEB nuclear translocation through ROS→Ca²⁺→calcineurin signaling, upregulate CLEAR-network transcripts (including LAMP1, CTSD, ATP6V1H, MCOLN1, ULK1, SQSTM1, LC3), increase lysosomal biogenesis and LysoTracker staining, and enhance autophagic flux; these outcomes are well documented for SFN in multiple human cell models and provide mechanistic plausibility for analogous effects in RPE (Li et al., 2021). In RPE specifically, TFEB is inducible and drives LAMP1 and CTSD expression under stress, while TFEB activation improves degradative capacity in AMD-relevant settings, supporting the assay logic to monitor TFEB localization and CLEAR targets in RPE (Pan et al., 2019; Falcão et al., 2024).

- Lysosomal acidification and CTSD proteolysis: If SFN increases v-ATPase subunit expression and lysosomal mass (as in other cells), one would expect improved lysosomal acidification (lower pH) and more efficient CTSD maturation (pro- to intermediate- to mature forms) and activity, read out by calibrated pH probes (e.g., LysoSensor or ratiometric genetically encoded sensors), Western blots for CTSD processing, and fluorometric CTSD activity assays. This is biologically plausible given SFN-induced lysosomal gene expression and LysoTracker increases in vitro, and given that TFEB activation rescues hydrolase-dependent POS processing in RPE; however, direct evidence that SFN normalizes lysosomal pH and increases CTSD maturation/activity in RPE under oxidative stress is not shown in the provided literature and must be generated (Li et al., 2021; Falcão et al., 2024; Falcão et al., 2025).

- Functional RPE outcomes: Improved degradative clearance of POS-derived material and reduced accumulation of autofluorescent granules (lipofuscin-like AFG) are expected functional correlates. Rapamycin and constitutively active TFEB reduce AFG in RPE POS-feeding models in a hydrolase-dependent manner, and TFEB activation or AKT2 inhibition restores CTSD/CTSL activity and LAMP1 levels in human AMD RPE models; SFN could converge on these endpoints via TFEB activation, but this remains to be established experimentally (Falcão et al., 2024; Ghosh et al., 2024).

Assay implementation considerations:  
- Cell systems and paradigms: iPSC-derived human RPE (including CFH Y402H risk background) and ARPE-19, subjected to blue light, hydroquinone/cigarette smoke mimetics, sodium iodate, POS feeding, and/or complement challenge, to model oxidative and degradative stress relevant to dry AMD (Yang et al., 2021; Ghosh et al., 2024; Falcão et al., 2024).

- Readouts: NRF2 nuclear translocation and ARE target induction; TFEB nuclear translocation and phosphorylation state; CLEAR-network gene expression (LAMP1, CTSD, ATP6V1H, MCOLN1, ULK1, SQSTM1); lysosomal pH (calibrated probes); lysosomal mass (LAMP1 immunostaining, LysoTracker); CTSD processing (pro/mature Western blots) and enzymatic activity; autophagic flux (LC3 turnover with and without lysosomal inhibitors); POS processing kinetics and AFG quantification (Li et al., 2021; Pan et al., 2019; Falcão et al., 2024).

- Controls and mechanism validation: Antioxidant blockade (NAC, catalase) to test ROS-dependence of TFEB activation; Ca²⁺ chelation (BAPTA-AM) and calcineurin inhibition (FK506) to probe TFEB activation pathway; NRF2 inhibitor (ML385) to test NRF2 dependence; TFEB loss-of-function (siRNA/CRISPR) to test TFEB dependence; rapamycin/trehalose/AAV-TFEB as positive controls for lysosomal biogenesis; cathepsin inhibitors and CTSD KO/knockdown to test hydrolase dependence; use of AKT2 pathway modulators to deconvolve TFEB repression in AMD-relevant models (Li et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2021; Ghosh et al., 2024).

- Dosing: Based on mechanistic studies, SFN at 5–15 µM for 4–9 h is effective for TFEB activation in several cell lines; in ARPE-19 blue-light studies, SFN modulated NRF2 and autophagy markers, and in TGF-β models, SFN was tested at 1–10 µM with ERK pathway modulation observed (Li et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2021; Bergandi et al., 2024).

- Interpretation caveats: In TGF-β–treated RPE, investigators did not detect NRF2 modulation with SFN, implying model-specific pathway engagement; this further justifies running multiple oxidative-stress paradigms and confirming pathway activation per condition (Bergandi et al., 2024).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
- Mechanistic alignment with AMD pathobiology: SFN targets two validated, convergent axes implicated in non-neovascular AMD—NRF2-mediated antioxidant defense and TFEB-driven lysosomal-autophagy competence—addressing both oxidative injury and impaired degradative capacity in RPE (Pinelli et al., 2020; Pan et al., 2019; Falcão et al., 2024). The KEAP1–NRF2 pathway is protective in RPE and becomes inducibly impaired with age, and TFEB activation restores lysosomal hydrolase function and reduces POS-derived autofluorescence in RPE models (Sachdeva et al., 2014; Ghosh et al., 2024).

- Robust cellular mechanism for dual-pathway engagement: High-quality mechanistic studies show SFN activates TFEB via ROS→Ca²⁺→calcineurin independent of acute MTORC1 inhibition, induces CLEAR genes, increases lysosomal mass and LysoTracker staining, and requires TFEB to drive NFE2L2 transcription and antioxidant protection; this dual activation positions SFN to coordinate detoxification and lysosomal biogenesis (Li et al., 2021).

- RPE-relevant efficacy signals: In ARPE-19 blue-light stress, SFN confers NRF2-dependent cytoprotection and requires autophagy; SFN also reduces ROS and preserves epithelial integrity in an inflammatory RPE model (albeit via ERK1/2 modulation), indicating protective breadth across RPE-relevant stressors (Yang et al., 2021; Bergandi et al., 2024).

- Translational plausibility: SFN (and class analogs) have measurable bioavailability in humans and have long been explored as chemopreventive agents, supporting feasibility of repurposing if ocular exposure and dosing can be optimized; as a comparator, RTA 408’s efficacy in primary RPE confirms the druggability of KEAP1–NRF2 in human RPE (Qin, 2007; Liu et al., 2016).

Weaknesses and Gaps  
- No direct evidence for lysosomal pH normalization or CTSD rescue by SFN in RPE: Although SFN upregulates lysosomal genes and LysoTracker signal in other cell types, the literature provided does not report calibrated lysosomal pH measurements, CTSD maturation patterns, or CTSD enzymatic activity in RPE after SFN under AMD-relevant oxidative stress; this is the critical gap relative to the stated hypothesis (Falcão et al., 2025; Li et al., 2021).

- Model-dependent pathway engagement: In TGF-β ARPE-19, SFN protection tracked with ERK1/2 modulation rather than detectable NRF2 activation, underscoring that the upstream route by which SFN confers benefit can vary with the stressor and cellular context; robust pathway biomarker panels will be required in each model (Bergandi et al., 2024).

- Safety and liability considerations: Persistent activation of NRF2 or TFEB may carry oncogenic or maladaptive risks; KEAP1–NRF2 dysregulation can drive accumulation of non-degradative organelles, and TFEB activation must be titrated to avoid excessive catabolic or proliferative shifts; this risk profile necessitates careful dose finding, exposure control, and long-term safety assessment (Falcão et al., 2025).

- Ocular delivery/exposure unknowns: The provided literature notes systemic bioavailability with detection in non-ocular tissues; however, data establishing therapeutic concentrations in the RPE/choroid following systemic or local administration are absent here and will need to be generated to support translational feasibility (Qin, 2007).

- Disease-network complexity: In AMD-relevant RPE, AKT2 upregulation suppresses TFEB/TFE3 and reduces cathepsin expression/activity, while complement dysregulation, iron overload, and lipofuscin-associated proton-pump/hydrolase inhibition further stress lysosomes; whether SFN’s TFEB activation suffices under these multi-hit conditions, or whether combination with AKT2/MTOR modulators is required, remains to be determined (Ghosh et al., 2024; Falcão et al., 2025; Ghosh et al., 2023).

Programmatic recommendation  
- Proceed to focused preclinical validation with a decision gate on lysosomal biochemical rescue. The mechanistic case for SFN’s dual NRF2/TFEB action is strong, and RPE protection data exist; however, the program’s value hinges on demonstrating that SFN normalizes lysosomal acidity and restores CTSD maturation/activity in stressed human RPE—outcomes not yet shown (Li et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2021; Falcão et al., 2025).

- Experimental plan (key go/no-go criteria):  
  1) Pathway engagement: SFN dose–time response for NRF2 and TFEB activation in iPSC-RPE (±CFH Y402H) under blue light, hydroquinone, sodium iodate, POS feeding, and complement challenge; use ML385, FK506, BAPTA-AM, and TFEB LOF to assign mechanism (Yang et al., 2021; Li et al., 2021; Falcão et al., 2024).  
  2) Lysosomal function: Calibrated lysosomal pH; CTSD pro/mature processing and activity; LAMP1 and LysoTracker; autophagic flux; POS degradation kinetics and AFG load; requirement for CTSD confirmed by CTSD knockdown and cathepsin inhibitors (Falcão et al., 2024; Sharif, 2015).  
  3) Comparative controls: Rapamycin, trehalose, and AAV2-TFEB-S467A as positive controls for lysosomal rescue; AKT2 inhibition as a disease-relevant comparator; RTA 408 or DMF as NRF2-pathway comparators (Ghosh et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2016).  
  4) In vivo signal-seeking: Acute oxidative RPE injury (e.g., sodium iodate) and chronic stress models to test functional endpoints (ERG, OCT) and histology with RPE lysosomal markers and CTSD activity ex vivo; assess whether SFN improves cone/RPE function in an NRF2-dependent manner as suggested by prior reports (Sachdeva et al., 2014; Falcão et al., 2025).  
  5) Safety/tolerability: Proliferation, TFEB/NRF2 target overactivation, lysosomal membrane integrity (LMP markers), and non-degradative organelle accumulation with extended SFN exposure; aim for intermittent dosing to minimize chronic pathway hyperactivation (Falcão et al., 2025).

If SFN demonstrates robust TFEB/NRF2 engagement in human RPE, normalizes lysosomal pH, and restores CTSD maturation/activity with concomitant functional improvements in POS clearance and AFG reduction, it would represent a differentiated candidate addressing both oxidative and degradative lesions central to geographic atrophy; absent those biochemical rescues, SFN should be deprioritized in favor of TFEB-directed agents (e.g., trehalose or gene-based TFEB activation) or combinations that address AKT2–TFEB repression and complement-driven lysosomal injury (Ghosh et al., 2024; Falcão et al., 2024; Ghosh et al., 2023).

In summary, sulforaphane is a scientifically credible, mechanistically dual-action candidate for dry AMD based on strong evidence of NRF2 activation in RPE and TFEB-driven lysosome-autophagy induction in multiple cell systems, with supportive RPE protection data; yet the decisive evidence that SFN normalizes lysosomal acidity and rescues cathepsin D proteolysis in oxidatively stressed human RPE is currently lacking in the provided literature and must be established to justify advancement (Yang et al., 2021; Li et al., 2021; Falcão et al., 2025).
