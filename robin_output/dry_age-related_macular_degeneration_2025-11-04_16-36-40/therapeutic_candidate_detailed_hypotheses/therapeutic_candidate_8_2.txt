Proposal for 2-Hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (HPβCD; Sigma C0926)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
2-Hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (HPβCD) is a chemically modified derivative of natural β-cyclodextrin—a cyclic oligosaccharide consisting of glucose units linked via α-1,4 glycosidic bonds. This modification, achieved by the introduction of hydroxypropyl groups, improves its solubility and reduces its inherent cytotoxicity relative to unmodified cyclodextrins. HPβCD belongs to the cyclodextrin class, molecules that have been widely used in pharmaceutical formulations as solubilizing agents and excipients due to their ability to encapsulate hydrophobic molecules within their lipophilic interior, thereby increasing bioavailability. Over the years, these agents have gained attention as cholesterol-binding molecules, and HPβCD is no exception, being repurposed for diseases characterized by pathological cholesterol accumulation such as Niemann–Pick type C1 (NPC1). Its structural properties permit the formation of inclusion complexes with cholesterol and other lipids, facilitating their mobilization and removal from cells. Given its established role in NPC1 disease and emerging evidence supporting its ability to cross the blood–retina barrier, HPβCD is now being evaluated as a therapeutic candidate for dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD), specifically targeting drusen-associated cholesterol accumulation in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells (Calias, 2018; Roney, 2020).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, HPβCD has been extensively investigated as a therapeutic intervention for lysosomal storage diseases, with a particular focus on Niemann–Pick disease type C (NPC), a condition characterized by the pathological accumulation of cholesterol and glycosphingolipids in cells. Preclinical studies in animal models have demonstrated that systemic administration of HPβCD can lower intracellular cholesterol levels, thereby ameliorating disease-related cellular dysfunction. However, its pharmacokinetic limitations—most notably, its minimal penetration of the blood–brain barrier (BBB) when administered intravenously—have necessitated alternative routes of delivery, such as intrathecal administration, to achieve sufficient central nervous system (CNS) exposure (Calias, 2018). In the context of retinal diseases, several studies have investigated the effects of HPβCD in animal models of retinal cholesterol dyshomeostasis. In these models, HPβCD treatment has been associated with significant reductions in cholesterol crystals, diminished lipid deposition in structures such as Bruch’s membrane, and decreased activation of retinal macrophages and microglial cells (El-Darzi et al., 2023). Although HPβCD has been repurposed from its application in NPC to target retinal pathologies, a focused investigation into its use for dry AMD remains to be performed clinically, as systematic searches on ClinicalTrials.gov indicate that no registered trials have specifically addressed cyclodextrin use in AMD (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, HPβCD acts primarily as a cholesterol extractor by leveraging its toroidal structure, which is characterized by a hydrophilic exterior and a relatively hydrophobic interior cavity. This unique architecture enables HPβCD to form inclusion complexes with cholesterol, effectively sequestering the lipid from cellular membranes. In the setting of RPE cells, where lysosomal cholesterol accumulation is implicated in the pathogenesis of dry AMD, HPβCD is hypothesized to mobilize cholesterol from lysosomal membranes. Excess cholesterol in these membranes disrupts the lipid microdomains necessary for the optimal assembly and functioning of protein complexes such as the vacuolar-type H⁺-ATPase (v-ATPase), which is essential for maintaining lysosomal acidity (Nociari, Kiss, & Rodriguez-Boulan, 2017). By extracting cholesterol, HPβCD is expected to restore these lipid microdomains, thereby facilitating the proper assembly and function of the v-ATPase complex. This normalization of v-ATPase assembly is critical for reestablishing an acidic environment within lysosomes, which in turn is necessary for the optimal activity of lysosomal hydrolases such as cathepsin D (CTSD).

In neurons and other cell types, studies have demonstrated that HPβCD treatment leads to a reduction in both luminal and membrane-associated cholesterol levels, a process that not only improves lysosomal trafficking but also induces lysosomal exocytosis under certain conditions (Roney, 2020). Furthermore, the improvement in lysosomal acidification following HPβCD-mediated cholesterol extraction has been correlated with enhanced autophagic flux due to increased CTSD-mediated proteolysis. Although direct evidence for this mechanism in RPE cells is still emerging, it is reasonable to extrapolate that similar molecular events would occur in retinal cells. The restoration of lysosomal pH and the subsequent improvement in autophagy are critical because they directly contribute to the clearance of intracellular debris, including drusen lipids, thereby potentially reducing the lipid burden that characterizes the progression of dry AMD (Nociari et al., 2017; Roney, 2020).

Expected Effect:  
The central hypothesis driving the repurposing of HPβCD for dry AMD is that it will mobilize cholesterol from RPE lysosomal membranes, thereby restoring the integrity of lipid microdomains that are essential for v-ATPase assembly. In practical terms, treatment with HPβCD is expected to yield several interrelated outcomes. First, the reduction in membrane cholesterol is anticipated to alleviate the inhibitory effect of cholesterol excess on v-ATPase assembly, leading to improved proton pump activity and a consequent normalization of lysosomal pH. Given that the activity of CTSD is pH-dependent—with optimal function occurring under acidic conditions—the normalization of lysosomal pH should enable enhanced CTSD activity and improved autophagic degradation of lipid aggregates (Nociari et al., 2017; Roney, 2020).

Second, by promoting the removal of excess cholesterol, HPβCD should reduce the formation of cholesterol crystals, a pathological hallmark associated with retinal lipid dyshomeostasis and drusen formation. Animal studies have demonstrated that HPβCD treatment leads to a significant reduction in the number of cholesterol crystals per unit area in retinal tissues, which is strongly correlated with improvements in retinal lipid processing and decreased inflammatory responses mediated by macrophage and microglial activation (El-Darzi et al., 2023).

Third, in laboratory assays using RPE cell cultures, one would expect to observe a decrease in the proportion of lipid-laden lysosomes, accompanied by measurable improvements in lysosomal acidification—parameters that serve as surrogate markers for enhanced autophagic capacity. Additionally, given the evidence that HPβCD can cross the blood–retina barrier when administered via systemic routes, there is a strong rationale for expecting that appropriate dosing regimens will yield sufficient retinal tissue concentrations to exert these beneficial effects (El-Darzi et al., 2023). Ultimately, these molecular and cellular changes are anticipated to culminate in the reduction of drusen lipid volumes, thereby slowing or potentially halting the progression of dry AMD (El-Darzi et al., 2023).

Overall Evaluation:  
The repurposing of HPβCD for the treatment of dry AMD is underpinned by a robust mechanistic rationale, a substantial preclinical history, and an acceptable clinical safety profile observed in other indications. One of the greatest strengths of HPβCD is its dual functionality: the ability to form effective inclusion complexes with cholesterol and the resultant improvement in lysosomal function. In several animal models of retinal cholesterol dyshomeostasis, HPβCD has yielded promising results by lowering total, unesterified, and esterified cholesterol levels, reducing the number of cholesterol crystals, and mitigating inflammatory responses in the retina (El-Darzi et al., 2023). These data provide a compelling basis for its evaluation in conditions such as dry AMD where drusen formation is closely linked to aberrant cholesterol metabolism. Furthermore, its prior use in the treatment of NPC—a condition that shares certain pathogenic features with AMD in terms of lysosomal dysfunction—adds credence to the hypothesis that HPβCD may exert similar therapeutic effects in RPE cells (Calias, 2018).

A significant advantage of HPβCD is its established safety record in clinical studies for NPC. Although challenges remain regarding optimal dosing—especially given the compound’s limited ability to cross the BBB when administered systemically—the retinal application appears more favorable, as preclinical studies suggest that HPβCD is capable of traversing the blood–retina barrier and achieving therapeutic concentrations in retinal tissues (El-Darzi et al., 2023). However, it should be noted that the safety profile in the context of chronic ocular exposure remains to be fully characterized. Notably, clinical studies in NPC have observed adverse effects such as hearing loss with certain routes of administration, and while these effects may not directly translate to ocular toxicity, rigorous ocular safety studies will be essential to ensure that any repurposing for dry AMD is both efficacious and safe (Calias, 2018).

Another potential strength lies in the specificity of the lipid‐targeting strategy. Unlike therapies that broadly modulate inflammatory or oxidative stress pathways, HPβCD acts directly on the cholesterol burden—the underlying driver of drusen lipid accumulation in dry AMD. This mechanistic specificity has the potential to result in a more direct impact on the disease process, rather than merely modifying secondary symptoms. Mechanistically, the extraction of cholesterol by HPβCD is anticipated to restore the lipid environment within RPE lysosomal membranes, thereby re-enabling the proper assembly of v-ATPase complexes. This, in turn, can lead to improved lysosomal acidification, enhanced cathepsin D activity, and accelerated autophagic clearance of damaged cellular components—a cascade of events that would address one of the root causes of retinal degeneration in AMD (Nociari et al., 2017; Roney, 2020).

Despite these strengths, several weaknesses and uncertainties must be acknowledged. First, while the preclinical data in animal models are promising, there remains a lack of clinical trial data specifically focusing on the use of HPβCD in dry AMD. A search using the relevant keywords indicates a paucity of clinical evidence in this particular indication (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.), which means that translational hurdles remain before HPβCD can be confidently recommended for dry AMD therapy. Second, although the mechanism by which HPβCD affects v-ATPase assembly and CTSD activity is supported by data in neuronal models, direct evidence of these molecular events in RPE cells is still emerging, leaving some uncertainty regarding the depth of its effects on lysosomal autophagy in the retinal environment (Nociari et al., 2017). Third, formulation and administration routes will be critical considerations. While systemic dosing has been used in other indications, the optimal approach for targeting the retina might require localized delivery techniques—such as intravitreal injection or the development of a sustained-release ocular formulation—to maximize retinal exposure while minimizing systemic exposure and off-target effects. Finally, the potential for adverse events, particularly those related to over-extraction of cholesterol or unintended disruption of lipid homeostasis, must be carefully evaluated in the context of chronic ocular treatment.

In summary, HPβCD is a well-characterized compound with a history of successful application in disorders of cholesterol accumulation, notably NPC. Its ability to extract cholesterol from cellular membranes, restore lipid microdomains, and thereby normalize lysosomal function forms a strong mechanistic basis for its evaluation as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD. Preclinical studies in animal models provide encouraging evidence that HPβCD can reduce retinal cholesterol accumulation, lower the number of cholesterol crystals, and improve markers of lysosomal function in retinal tissues. This directly aligns with the therapeutic hypothesis that the mobilization of cholesterol from RPE lysosomal membranes will restore v-ATPase activity, normalize lysosomal pH, enhance cathepsin D activity, and ultimately reduce the drusen lipid burden characteristic of dry AMD (Calias, 2018; El-Darzi et al., 2023; Nociari et al., 2017; Roney, 2020).

However, the translation of these preclinical findings into a clinically effective therapy for dry AMD will require addressing several critical challenges. These include the absence of direct clinical data in AMD, the need for detailed mechanistic studies in RPE cells to confirm that the cholesterol-lowering effects observed in other tissues are recapitulated in the retina, and the optimization of dosing and delivery methods to ensure that therapeutic concentrations are achieved safely in the target tissue. Additionally, any potential side effects—such as those noted in NPC patients, including auditory toxicity—must be rigorously monitored in ocular studies to ensure that treatment remains safe over the long term (Calias, 2018).

Overall, the repurposing of HPβCD for dry AMD is scientifically compelling due to its targeted mechanism of action and its established clinical history in related indications. Its ability to mobilize cholesterol and restore lysosomal function addresses fundamental pathogenic processes implicated in dry AMD, and the preclinical evidence supports its potential efficacy. Nevertheless, the absence of clinical trials in this particular indication, combined with uncertainties regarding the precise molecular interactions in RPE cells and the optimization of drug delivery to the retina, suggests that further preclinical and early-stage clinical studies are warranted before HPβCD can be advanced as a treatment for dry AMD. Continued research should focus on elucidating the detailed molecular mechanisms within RPE cells, assessing long-term safety in ocular models, and exploring various delivery modalities to maximize efficacy while minimizing potential risks. In conclusion, while HPβCD demonstrates substantial promise as a repurposed drug candidate for dry AMD, a measured, stepwise approach in future research is essential to address outstanding questions and to establish its full therapeutic potential (Calias, 2018; El-Darzi et al., 2023; Nociari et al., 2017; Roney, 2020).
