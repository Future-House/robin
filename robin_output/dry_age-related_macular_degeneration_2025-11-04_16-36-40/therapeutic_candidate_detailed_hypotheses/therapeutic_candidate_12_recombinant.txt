Proposal for Recombinant human pro-cathepsin D (R&D Systems 1869-CD)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Recombinant human pro-cathepsin D (rhCTSD), as exemplified by R&D Systems product 1869-CD, is a bioengineered form of a naturally occurring lysosomal aspartyl protease that is synthesized as an inactive precursor (pro-cathepsin D) (Sharif, 2015, pp. 60–64). This therapeutic candidate belongs to the enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) class, a category of biotherapeutics developed to supplement or correct deficient lysosomal enzymes in various disorders. The natural cathepsin D enzyme is normally produced and processed intracellularly through the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and Golgi apparatus, undergoing critical post-translational modifications—including the removal of the N-terminal signal peptide, glycosylation, and addition of a mannose-6-phosphate (M6P) tag—that are essential for its subsequent targeting to the lysosome (Sharif, 2015, pp. 60–64). The recombinant version is produced under conditions aimed at ensuring high purity and biological activity conforming to GMP standards, thus making it an attractive candidate for repurposing in conditions where lysosomal proteolytic capacity is compromised. The candidate has been designed in the context of neurodegenerative and lysosomal storage disorders, where enzyme supplementation has been shown to restore proteolytic flux by directly replacing a rate-limiting hydrolase (Bassal et al., 2021; Marques et al., 2020).

Therapeutic History:  
Enzyme replacement therapies have been a cornerstone in the management of several lysosomal storage disorders since the early 1990s, with the first approved treatment for Gaucher’s disease setting a precedent for this therapeutic strategy (Saftig, 2005; Lerussi et al., 2025). In the specific case of cathepsin D, recombinant forms have been employed in preclinical models of neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis (NCL) and other neurodegenerative lysosomal storage disorders, where the administration of exogenous pro-cathepsin D was able to rescue functional deficits associated with genetic CTSD deficiency (Bassal et al., 2021; Marques et al., 2020). Animal studies using CTSD-deficient mouse models have demonstrated that both intracranial and localized injections of recombinant CTSD can correct defects in proteolysis and autophagy, leading to improvements in neuropathological markers and even extending lifespan in treated animals (Bassal et al., 2021; Liu, 2021; Marques et al., 2020). Although most of these studies have focused on neurodegenerative conditions of the central nervous system, similar principles of lysosomal dysfunction apply to retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells, which are critical for maintaining retinal homeostasis in the context of age-related macular degeneration (AMD) (Klettner et al., 2013). Notably, the literature suggests that cathepsin D is highly expressed in RPE cells and plays a vital role in the degradation of photoreceptor outer segments (POS), with its deficiency or reduced function being implicated in the accumulation of toxic deposits and drusen formation—a hallmark of dry AMD (Klettner et al., 2013; Sharif, 2015). Despite the absence of direct clinical trials in dry AMD using rhCTSD, the extensive preclinical evidence from both neurodegenerative and retinal disease models provides a compelling rationale for exploring the repurposing of this enzyme replacement strategy in dry AMD (Liu, 2021; Marques et al., 2020).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, the mechanism of action of recombinant human pro-cathepsin D is rooted in its ability to restore lysosomal proteolytic function, which is often impaired in disease states such as dry AMD. Under physiological conditions, pro-cathepsin D is synthesized in the rough ER as an inactive precursor that undergoes several post-translational modifications, including the cleavage of an N-terminal signal peptide, N-linked glycosylation, and critical mannose-6-phosphate (M6P) tagging in the Golgi apparatus (Sharif, 2015). The M6P tag facilitates the binding of pro-cathepsin D to M6P receptors within the trans-Golgi network (TGN), directing the enzyme to late endosomes and, eventually, to lysosomes where the acidic environment promotes its maturation into its active form (Sharif, 2015). Once active, cathepsin D contributes to the degradation of a wide range of substrates, including the photoreceptor outer segments in RPE cells, thereby playing a crucial role in maintaining cellular homeostasis and preventing the accumulation of deleterious debris that can lead to drusen formation (Klettner et al., 2013). The proposed therapeutic strategy involves subretinal delivery of the recombinant enzyme to bypass systemic limitations such as the blood-brain barrier and to achieve localized uptake into RPE cells via the M6P receptor-mediated endocytosis pathway (Liu, 2021; Marques et al., 2020; Sharif, 2015). Once internalized, the enzyme can replenish endogenous levels of cathepsin D, thereby enhancing autophagic flux and proteolytic degradation processes that are essential for the health and function of the RPE (Klettner et al., 2013; Sharif, 2015). This targeted mechanism is a direct, molecularly specific approach that aims not only to compensate for maturation defects in the enzyme but also to overcome upstream acidification abnormalities that often hinder proper activation of endogenously produced cathepsin D in aging RPE cells (Liu, 2021; Marques et al., 2020; Sharif, 2015).

Expected Effect:  
The anticipated outcome of employing recombinant human pro-cathepsin D for the treatment of dry AMD is multifaceted. First, the subretinal administration of the enzyme is expected to lead to efficient uptake by RPE cells via the M6P receptor, thereby supplementing or restoring the lysosomal pool of active cathepsin D where it is most critically needed (Sharif, 2015). This direct enzyme replacement should result in improved degradation of cellular waste such as spent photoreceptor outer segments, mitigating the build-up of lipofuscin and drusen that are characteristic of dry AMD pathology (Klettner et al., 2013). In animal models of CTSD deficiency—specifically those that mimic aspects of neurodegenerative lysosomal storage disorders—the administration of exogenous pro-cathepsin D has yielded significant improvements in proteolytic function, reduction of pathological markers, and even extensions in lifespan (Bassal et al., 2021; Marques et al., 2020), which suggest that similar therapeutic benefits might be achievable in the context of dry AMD. Enhancing the proteolytic flux within the lysosomes of RPE cells is expected to restore autophagy and maintain cellular homeostasis, thereby reducing the incidence of apoptotic cell death and preserving retinal structure (Klettner et al., 2013; Sharif, 2015). Additionally, by compensating for potential maturation deficits and bypassing defective acidification processes through localized delivery, the therapeutic candidate offers a targeted approach that is likely to be both efficacious and specific compared to more indirect modulators of lysosomal function (Liu, 2021; Marques et al., 2020; Sharif, 2015). Ultimately, the expected effect is an overall improvement in RPE health, which should translate into slowed progression of dry AMD, decreased drusen accumulation, and preserved visual function.

Overall Evaluation:  
In evaluating recombinant human pro-cathepsin D as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD, several strengths emerge from the literature, though some challenges remain. One of the primary strengths of this candidate lies in its specificity; unlike indirect modulators of lysosomal function, the exogenous delivery of a rate-limiting hydrolase such as CTSD offers a mechanistically driven, targeted replacement therapy that directly addresses a known biochemical deficit in the retinal pigment epithelium (Klettner et al., 2013; Liu, 2021). The robust body of preclinical evidence supporting the efficacy of recombinant pro-cathepsin D in models of neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis, where similar lysosomal impairments are present, lends further credence to its potential applicability in AMD (Bassal et al., 2021; Marques et al., 2020). Moreover, the formulation's GMP-compatibility and prior demonstration of safety in the context of genetic CTSD deficiency are significant advantages that can accelerate its translational potential (Marques et al., 2020; Liu, 2021).

The enzyme’s molecular mechanism, which involves uptake via mannose-6-phosphate receptor-mediated endocytosis and subsequent activation in the lysosomal compartment, is well-characterized and aligns with the pathological requirements for restoring RPE function in dry AMD (Sharif, 2015). This targeted approach provides a direct means to enhance proteolytic activity and autophagic flux, crucial processes that are disrupted in the aging retina and contribute to drusen accumulation (Klettner et al., 2013). The ability of the exogenously delivered pro-cathepsin D to overcome defects in lysosomal maturation and acidification further reinforces its potential role in reversing or mitigating the deleterious effects of the disease (Liu, 2021; Marques et al., 2020).

On the other hand, several weaknesses or challenges must be acknowledged. Although the mechanistic rationale is robust, the translation of preclinical successes in neurodegenerative and lysosomal storage disorder models to the complex, multifactorial pathology of dry AMD remains uncertain (Benes et al., 2008; Liu, 2021). Dry AMD is influenced by a host of factors—including oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and genetic predispositions—that may not be fully addressed by simply supplementing CTSD activity. Therefore, while the normalization of proteolytic flux in RPE cells is a critical component, it may not suffice on its own in the context of the broader pathophysiological milieu present in AMD (Klettner et al., 2013; Sharif, 2015).

Furthermore, delivery challenges inherent to subretinal injections must be considered. Although localized delivery methods help bypass systemic barriers such as the blood-brain barrier, subretinal injections are technically demanding, carry inherent risks of infection, and may provoke local immune responses due to repeated interventions (Marques et al., 2020; Liu, 2021). The emergence of neutralizing antibodies, as observed in some ERT studies when alternative routes of administration were explored, could compromise the long-term efficacy of the therapy if systemic leakage occurs or if repeated dosing is required (Marques et al., 2020; Liu, 2021). Additionally, the stability and persistence of the enzyme in the subretinal space represent important pharmacokinetic questions that must be addressed through rigorous preclinical pharmacodynamic studies.

Another potential shortcoming is the specificity of the enzyme’s action. While cathepsin D is indeed a critical protease in RPE cells, there is an intricate interplay between cathepsin D and other lysosomal enzymes such as cathepsins B, L, and S, as well as regulatory proteins like cystatin C, which modulate proteolytic activity (Sharif, 2015). This interdependence implies that augmenting CTSD activity alone might lead to compensatory changes in other proteases that could either enhance the therapeutic effect or, conversely, result in unforeseen off-target consequences. Moreover, given the complexity of the RPE cell’s lysosomal network and the multifactorial nature of dry AMD pathogenesis, combination approaches targeting several aspects of lysosomal dysfunction might be necessary to achieve optimal clinical outcomes.

In summary, recombinant human pro-cathepsin D presents a therapeutically attractive and mechanistically rational candidate for the treatment of dry age-related macular degeneration. Its origins as a naturally occurring lysosomal precursor that, when supplemented exogenously, has been shown to restore enzyme activity in preclinical models of lysosomal dysfunction (Bassal et al., 2021; Marques et al., 2020) underscores its potential for addressing the proteolytic deficits seen in RPE cells affected by dry AMD (Klettner et al., 2013). The candidate’s well-defined molecular processing pathway—from synthesis as an inactive proenzyme to activation within the acidic environment of the lysosome—ensures that it is both functionally and biologically suited to compensate for endogenous maturation deficits (Sharif, 2015). This detailed understanding of its biochemistry and cellular uptake mechanisms via the mannose-6-phosphate receptor supports the hypothesis that subretinal delivery of rhCTSD could enhance proteolytic flux, reduce drusen accumulation, and ultimately contribute to the preservation of retinal function in patients with dry AMD (Liu, 2021; Marques et al., 2020).

Despite these promising attributes, the candidate’s clinical translation will require addressing several key challenges. Future studies must rigorously evaluate the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles of subretinally delivered rhCTSD, with particular emphasis on its stability, local retention time in the subretinal space, and potential immunogenicity. It will also be critical to determine whether augmenting CTSD activity alone is sufficient to counteract the multi-factorial processes underlying dry AMD or if the therapeutic strategy should be integrated into a broader, combination-based treatment regimen. Additionally, long-term safety studies are necessary to monitor for potential adverse effects, such as immune-mediated clearance or detrimental off-target proteolytic activity that might impair retinal structure and function (Marques et al., 2020; Thiessen, 2023).

Ultimately, the overall evaluation is cautiously optimistic. The mechanistic rationale is strong—supported by extensive preclinical data from related neurodegenerative and lysosomal storage disorder models—and the therapeutic candidate is produced under rigorous, GMP-compliant conditions, offering a level of quality assurance that is imperative for clinical advancement (Marques et al., 2020; Liu, 2021). The specificity of recombinant pro-cathepsin D in directly addressing a rate-limiting step in lysosomal degradation makes it an especially compelling candidate in a disease setting where impaired autophagy and proteolysis are central to pathology (Klettner et al., 2013). However, challenges related to effective subretinal delivery, the potential for immune responses, and the need to integrate its action within the broader context of AMD pathogenesis must be rigorously addressed in subsequent preclinical and clinical studies before a definitive therapeutic benefit can be confirmed (Marques et al., 2020; Benes et al., 2008). In light of these considerations, recombinant human pro-cathepsin D stands out as a promising avenue for therapeutic intervention in dry AMD, meriting further investigation to delineate its efficacy, safety, and long-term benefits in the appropriate animal models and ultimately in clinical trials.
