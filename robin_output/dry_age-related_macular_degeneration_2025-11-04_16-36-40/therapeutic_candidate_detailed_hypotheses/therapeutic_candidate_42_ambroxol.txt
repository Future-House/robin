Proposal for Ambroxol (Sigma A6407)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Ambroxol is a small-molecule mucolytic historically used to thin airway mucus and has been repurposed as a pharmacological chaperone for the lysosomal enzyme glucocerebrosidase (GCase) in Gaucher disease, where it selectively binds misfolded GCase to aid folding and trafficking to lysosomes for substrate degradation (Alhowyan & Harisa, 2025, pp. 17–18). Pharmacological chaperones constitute a class of small molecules that stabilize specific mutant lysosomal enzymes conformationally in the endoplasmic reticulum, enabling correct processing and lysosomal delivery; a paradigmatic example is migalastat, an alpha-galactosidase A chaperone approved for Fabry disease with amenable variants (Alhowyan & Harisa, 2025, pp. 17–18). Ambroxol is further characterized by its ability to cross the blood–brain barrier, a property relevant for neuronopathic lysosomal storage disorders and attractive for central nervous system or ocular indications where access is otherwise constrained (Alhowyan & Harisa, 2025, pp. 17–18). Within the broader landscape of lysosomal therapeutics, small molecules also act as proteostasis regulators and modulators of lysosomal signaling and biogenesis; however, off-target actions remain a central limitation of small-molecule therapy in this domain (Alhowyan & Harisa, 2025, pp. 17–18).

The therapeutic hypothesis for dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) posits that ambroxol could act as a pharmacological chaperone for lysosomal enzymes in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), stabilize or enhance vacuolar H+-ATPase (V-ATPase) function, improve lysosomal acidification, and thereby augment cathepsin D (CTSD) activity to clear lipofuscin/drusen components characteristic of dry AMD (Kaarniranta et al., 2023, pp. 2–3; Si et al., 2023, pp. 4–7). This rationale is grounded in the centrality of lysosomal function to RPE homeostasis and AMD pathogenesis: RPE lysosomes sustain heavy heterophagic and autophagic loads from daily photoreceptor outer-segment (POS) digestion, and impaired lysosomal acidification and hydrolase function contribute to lipofuscin accumulation, exosome-driven drusen formation, and progressive degeneration (Kaarniranta et al., 2023, pp. 2–3; Si et al., 2023, pp. 2–4, 5–7; Sharif, 2015, pp. 199–202). Notably, in AMD models and donor RPE, TFEB—the master transcriptional driver of lysosomal/autophagy biogenesis—is dysregulated, V-ATPase-related transcripts (e.g., ATP6VOA1) and cathepsin abundance/activity are linked to lysosomal competence, and TFEB activation or mTOR inhibition reduces toxic storage material such as A2E and POS-derived autofluorescent granules (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7).

Therapeutic History  
Conventional use: Ambroxol is primarily used clinically as a mucolytic agent to reduce mucus viscosity in respiratory disease (Alhowyan & Harisa, 2025, pp. 17–18). Beyond its mucolytic effects, ambroxol has been investigated in lysosomal storage diseases as a pharmacological chaperone for glucocerebrosidase; it binds misfolded GCase, promotes its proper folding/trafficking to lysosomes, and facilitates substrate clearance, with reported neurological benefits in neuronopathic Gaucher disease and demonstrated blood–brain barrier penetration (Alhowyan & Harisa, 2025, pp. 17–18). Clinically observed improvements in neurological symptoms such as myoclonus and seizures have been reported in pilot studies, and reviews summarize safety/efficacy observations in Gaucher populations, albeit with the caveat of potential off-target toxicities innate to small-molecule approaches (Alhowyan & Harisa, 2025, pp. 17–18, 33–35).

Use in ophthalmology and AMD: A targeted search of ClinicalTrials.gov for ambroxol in macular degeneration, AMD, or retinal conditions returned zero registered trials, indicating a lack of clinical testing for ambroxol in AMD or retina-specific indications within that registry (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.). The provided literature excerpts contain no preclinical or clinical data demonstrating ambroxol use in RPE, retinal degeneration models, or AMD, and they do not report ocular distribution or ocular pharmacokinetics for ambroxol (Alhowyan & Harisa, 2025, pp. 17–18; Si et al., 2023, pp. 2–4; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7). In contrast, other small-molecule interventions that modulate lysosomal/autophagy function in RPE have been reported: mTOR inhibition with rapamycin decreases A2E and lipofuscin-like granules and reduces inflammatory/angiogenic proteins; trehalose increases autophagy markers and flux and exhibits cytoprotective effects, although limitations in clinical efficacy and adverse effects are noted for mTOR inhibitors (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7). More broadly, pharmacologic restoration of lysosomal pH and regulatory control of V-ATPase–mTORC1 signaling in RPE have been explored mechanistically, underscoring the translational target plausibility but not implicating ambroxol specifically (Kaarniranta et al., 2023, pp. 2–3; Markitantova & Simirskii, 2025, pp. 24–25).

Mechanism of Action  
Ambroxol as a pharmacological chaperone: Ambroxol binds selectively to misfolded glucocerebrosidase (GCase), stabilizing its conformation in the biosynthetic pathway and enabling correct trafficking to lysosomes, where it restores enzymatic degradation of GCase substrates (Alhowyan & Harisa, 2025, pp. 17–18). This canonical chaperone mechanism increases lysosomal GCase activity and substrate turnover and has been linked to functional improvements in Gaucher disease models and patient reports, with additional indications that ambroxol can enhance mitochondrial function in patient-derived cells (Alhowyan & Harisa, 2025, pp. 17–18). The drug’s small-molecule nature and CNS penetrance differentiate it from enzyme-replacement approaches and position it within a class of proteostasis-modulating agents where target-specific engagement can produce organelle-level functional rescue but also carries risk of off-target interactions (Alhowyan & Harisa, 2025, pp. 17–18).

RPE lysosomal biology relevant to the hypothesis: In the RPE, lysosomal acidification is primarily established by the V-ATPase proton pump and is essential for optimal activity of luminal hydrolases including cathepsins; most lysosomal enzymes are exquisitely pH-dependent and lose activity when lysosomal pH is elevated (Markitantova & Simirskii, 2025, pp. 9–11; Kaarniranta et al., 2023, pp. 2–3). RPE lysosomes operate at the nexus of two degradative streams: heterophagy of POS (requiring efficient phagosome–lysosome fusion and acidic hydrolase activity) and macroautophagy/CMA for damaged proteins and organelles, both of which are dysregulated with aging and AMD-relevant stresses such as oxidative damage and advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) (Si et al., 2023, pp. 4–5; Kaarniranta et al., 2023, pp. 2–3; Sharif, 2015, pp. 199–202). CTSD is a key RPE lysosomal aspartic protease and the principal POS-degrading enzyme; CTSD loss causes retinal degeneration in mice, underscoring its nonredundant role (Si et al., 2023, pp. 4–5). In AMD, human donor RPE show decreased CTSD and CTSL levels/activities, increased TFEB phosphorylation with reduced nuclear localization, and impaired degradative capacity with accumulation of POS-dependent lipofuscin-like granules; activating TFEB in RPE increases CLEAR network gene expression including ATP6VOA1 (a V-ATPase subunit) and reduces POS-derived lipofuscin-like granules (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7). Oxidized bisretinoids such as A2E, a major lipofuscin component, inhibit V-ATPase and raise lysosomal pH in RPE, which decreases lysosomal enzyme function and impairs cargo hydrolysis—a direct mechanism linking storage material to acidification defects (Wang et al., 2023, pp. 11–13). Lipofuscin and its photoproducts can further inhibit lysosomal proton pumps or hydrolases and promote lysosomal membrane permeabilization, coupling storage to organelle damage and propagating autophagy-lysosomal dysfunction and inflammatory signaling (Si et al., 2023, pp. 5–7; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 2–4).

Signal control of lysosomal biogenesis and acidification in RPE: The V-ATPase–mTORC1–TFEB axis is central in RPE lysosomal homeostasis; CRYBA1/βA3/A1-crystallin directly regulates the V-ATPase and signals to mTORC1 at the lysosomal membrane, where mTORC1 activity controls TFEB phosphorylation and localization (phosphorylated TFEB is cytosolic; dephosphorylation permits nuclear translocation and activation of CLEAR network genes) (Kaarniranta et al., 2023, pp. 2–3; Markitantova & Simirskii, 2025, pp. 9–11; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7). Pharmacologic mTOR inhibition (e.g., rapamycin) shifts TFEB toward an active dephosphorylated state, increases autophagic flux, reduces A2E, and decreases lipofuscin-like granules in RPE and POS-fed models, with the reduction in storage material being dependent on active lysosomal enzymes and TFEB activation (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7). Trehalose also augments autophagy markers and flux with cytoprotective effects in RPE insult models, supporting the druggability of lysosome–autophagy axes (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7).

What is known and unknown about ambroxol in RPE/AMD: The provided literature contains no experimental data showing that ambroxol binds, stabilizes, or otherwise directly modulates RPE cathepsin D or V-ATPase, nor does it report ambroxol’s effects on lysosomal pH, TFEB activity, or CLEAR network gene expression in RPE models (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7; Markitantova & Simirskii, 2025, pp. 9–11). While small-molecule interventions can restore lysosomal acidity (e.g., acidic nanoparticles) or, conversely, deacidify lysosomes (e.g., chloroquine, quinacrine), these examples do not involve ambroxol and serve primarily to illustrate the malleability of lysosomal pH by pharmacological means (Alhowyan & Harisa, 2025, pp. 12–15). Importantly, antimalarial lysosomotropic agents that raise lysosomal pH carry ocular toxicity risks, providing a cautionary context for any lysosome-acting small molecule in ophthalmology, although ambroxol’s lysosomotropism and ocular toxicity profile are not defined in the provided excerpts (Alhowyan & Harisa, 2025, pp. 14–15). The context explicitly notes that no small-molecule pharmacological chaperones targeting cathepsin D deficiency or V-ATPase impairment in RPE/AMD models are presented, and there is no direct evidence described for ambroxol enhancing cathepsin D activity or drusen clearance in dry AMD (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7).

Expected Effect  
Pathway-level expectation if the hypothesis holds: If ambroxol acts as a pharmacological chaperone for lysosomal enzymes expressed in RPE and indirectly improves V-ATPase-dependent acidification, one would expect increased lysosomal hydrolase maturation/activity (including CTSD), restoration of acidic lysosomal pH, improved autophagosome–lysosome fusion and autophagic flux, and enhanced clearance of POS-derived material and lipofuscin components (Si et al., 2023, pp. 4–7; Markitantova & Simirskii, 2025, pp. 9–11). In RPE cells subjected to AMD-relevant stressors (e.g., A2E loading, AGEs-modified substrates), improved acidification and hydrolase function should reduce accumulation of autofluorescent granules and mitigate the need for exosome-mediated egress of undegraded material implicated in drusen formation (Sharif, 2015, pp. 68–72, 199–202; Grilo, 2014, pp. 42–46). Because TFEB activation robustly upregulates lysosomal biogenesis and can decrease lipofuscin-like material, intersecting ambroxol effects with TFEB–mTORC1 signaling and V-ATPase subunit expression (e.g., ATP6VOA1) would be logical endpoints for mechanistic alignment, even though ambroxol has not been shown to modulate TFEB in RPE (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7).

Assay predictions and measurable readouts: In RPE-like cells (ARPE-19, primary human RPE), relevant readouts to substantiate the hypothesis include:  
- Lysosomal pH measurements showing acidification relative to stressed controls; for comparison, A2E raises lysosomal pH via V-ATPase inhibition in RPE (Wang et al., 2023, pp. 11–13).  
- CTSD maturation (pro-CTSD to mature CTSD) and enzymatic activity assays, given RPE dependence on CTSD for POS degradation and its documented reduction in AMD RPE (Si et al., 2023, pp. 4–5; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7).  
- V-ATPase function and expression, including ATP6VOA1 transcript/protein, as TFEB target and acidification determinant (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7).  
- TFEB localization and CLEAR network gene expression, to assess upstream biogenesis signaling that correlates with lysosomal competence (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7).  
- POS degradation capacity and lipofuscin-like autofluorescent granule burden after POS feeding, a disease-relevant functional output sensitive to lysosomal hydrolase and acidification status (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7; Sharif, 2015, pp. 189–192).  
- Drusen-relevant surrogate readouts such as exosome marker release (CD63, CD81) and extracellular debris deposition under conditions of impaired lysosomal flux versus pharmacologic rescue (Grilo, 2014, pp. 42–46; Sharif, 2015, pp. 199–202).  
- In genetic/stress backgrounds that exacerbate lysosomal dysfunction (e.g., CFH Y402H), examine CTSD leakage and lysosomal fragility as phenotypes that might be normalized by improved acidification/hydrolase function (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 4–5).

Importantly, these expectations derive from RPE lysosome–autophagy biology and the mechanistic success of TFEB activation or mTOR inhibition in reducing storage material, not from any demonstrated ambroxol activity in RPE models; thus, ambroxol-specific efficacy remains speculative in this context (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7).

Evidence gaps and comparators: The literature does not report ambroxol enhancing lysosomal acidification, V-ATPase stability, or CTSD activity in RPE; preclinical evaluation would therefore require rigorous head-to-head comparisons with pathway-validating controls such as rapamycin (mTOR/TFEB activation) and trehalose (autophagy enhancer) that have shown reductions in A2E and lipofuscin-like granules or increased autophagy markers in RPE models (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7). Given the sensitivity of RPE hydrolases to lysosomal pH and the pathogenic role of A2E-mediated V-ATPase inhibition, an explicit demonstration that ambroxol reverses A2E-induced deacidification and restores hydrolase activity is a critical bar for translational plausibility (Wang et al., 2023, pp. 11–13; Markitantova & Simirskii, 2025, pp. 9–11).

Overall Evaluation  
Strength of biological rationale: The centrality of lysosomal dysfunction to RPE degeneration in dry AMD is well-supported: lysosomal enzyme activity declines with aging and stress, lipofuscin accumulates and inhibits proton pumps/hydrolases, lysosomal acidification is critical for hydrolase function, and impaired lysosome–autophagy flux contributes to intracellular overload, exosome-mediated debris export, drusen formation, and inflammation (Kaarniranta et al., 2023, pp. 2–5; Si et al., 2023, pp. 5–7; Sharif, 2015, pp. 199–202). CTSD is a principal POS-degrading protease in RPE and is reduced in human AMD donor tissue, making strategies that restore CTSD maturation/activity and lysosomal acidification compelling (Si et al., 2023, pp. 4–5; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7). The V-ATPase–mTORC1–TFEB axis offers convergent control over lysosomal biogenesis and acidification, and experimental activation of TFEB or mTOR inhibition in RPE produces the desired phenotypes—elevated lysosomal gene expression (including ATP6VOA1), increased autophagic capacity, reduced A2E, and decreased lipofuscin-like granules—validating this biology as druggable in principle (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7). Against this backdrop, small-molecule chaperones are an attractive modality because they can enhance endogenous lysosomal enzyme folding/trafficking and potentially boost organelle function without gene transfer; ambroxol exemplifies this approach in GCase biology, with CNS penetration and clinical observations supporting feasibility in lysosomal disease (Alhowyan & Harisa, 2025, pp. 17–18).

Evidence weaknesses specific to ambroxol in AMD: Despite the strong organelle-level rationale, the ambroxol-specific data package for AMD is essentially absent in the provided record: no RPE cell data, no measurements of lysosomal pH or V-ATPase stabilization, no CTSD maturation/activity rescue, no POS degradation assays, no lipofuscin/A2E clearance, and no in vivo retinal outcomes are reported here (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7; Markitantova & Simirskii, 2025, pp. 9–11). There are no registered clinical trials for ambroxol in macular degeneration or retinal indications on ClinicalTrials.gov per the specified query, and the excerpts contain no ocular distribution or pharmacokinetics information for ambroxol (Alhowyan & Harisa, 2025, pp. 17–18; ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.). The mechanistic extrapolation—ambroxol chaperoning broadly for lysosomal enzymes in RPE and stabilizing V-ATPase—is unsubstantiated; ambroxol’s demonstrated chaperone target is GCase, not CTSD or V-ATPase, and the literature provided explicitly notes the lack of small-molecule pharmacological chaperones targeting CTSD deficiency or V-ATPase impairment in RPE/AMD models (Alhowyan & Harisa, 2025, pp. 17–18; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7). Furthermore, while small molecules can manipulate lysosomal pH in either direction (e.g., chloroquine deacidifies; acidic nanoparticles re-acidify), these findings emphasize the need for target-specific validation and caution regarding retinal toxicity from lysosomotropic agents; none of these pharmacologies have been tied to ambroxol in RPE (Alhowyan & Harisa, 2025, pp. 14–15).

Translational considerations and risk: The absence of ocular PK and target engagement data is a major liability for advancing ambroxol toward AMD; lysosomal pharmacology in post-mitotic RPE under chronic oxidative and lipofuscin stress likely differs from LSD contexts, and direct evidence of RPE target engagement is needed to justify escalation (Si et al., 2023, pp. 2–4; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 2–4). It is also noteworthy that mTOR inhibitors—which validated TFEB-mediated lysosomal rescue in RPE models—have shown limited efficacy and adverse effects in clinical settings, highlighting the translational friction even when mechanisms are strong (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7). Given that CTSD is pH-dependent and V-ATPase inhibition by lipofuscin bisretinoids is a proximal defect in AMD RPE, any candidate must demonstrate re-acidification and hydrolase restoration under A2E load to be credible; absent such data for ambroxol, the mechanistic chain remains hypothetical (Wang et al., 2023, pp. 11–13; Markitantova & Simirskii, 2025, pp. 9–11). Finally, because impaired lysosomal clearance increases exosome release and contributes to drusen, measuring exosome markers and extracellular deposit proxies will be important to link intracellular rescue to extracellular AMD hallmarks, but ambroxol lacks such evidence here (Sharif, 2015, pp. 199–202; Grilo, 2014, pp. 42–46).

Programmatic recommendation and experimental path: At this stage, ambroxol should not advance directly to in vivo retinal studies or clinical exploration for dry AMD without foundational RPE data demonstrating lysosomal acidification rescue and CTSD activity enhancement under AMD-relevant stressors (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.). A rigorous, staged preclinical plan is warranted:  
- In vitro RPE assays under A2E and POS-fed conditions to quantify lysosomal pH, V-ATPase subunit expression (ATP6VOA1), TFEB nuclear translocation/CLEAR activation, CTSD maturation/activity, POS degradation kinetics, and lipofuscin-like autofluorescence, with rapamycin and trehalose as mechanistically validated comparators (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7; Wang et al., 2023, pp. 11–13).  
- Lysosomal membrane integrity tests and CTSD leakage assessment in CFH Y402H RPE models to probe stabilization of lysosomal compartments under genetic risk backgrounds (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 4–5).  
- Exosome marker release and extracellular deposit assays to link intracellular lysosomal rescue to drusen-relevant phenotypes (Grilo, 2014, pp. 42–46).  
- Ocular exposure assessment to confirm ambroxol reaches RPE at pharmacologically active concentrations using appropriate delivery routes; absent ocular PK data in the provided literature, this becomes a gating requirement (Alhowyan & Harisa, 2025, pp. 17–18).

Overall, ambroxol’s demonstrated chaperone activity for GCase, BBB penetration, and clinical observations in Gaucher disease establish it as a plausible proteostasis-modulating scaffold, but the lack of any RPE/AMD-directed mechanistic or efficacy evidence and the absence of ocular PK data create a high translational risk profile for dry AMD (Alhowyan & Harisa, 2025, pp. 17–18; ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.). By contrast, interventions that directly activate TFEB or modulate mTOR have documented efficacy in RPE models, though clinical translation hurdles remain, and this suggests that ambroxol should be benchmarked against these pathways in a preclinical setting before any programmatic commitment (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7).
