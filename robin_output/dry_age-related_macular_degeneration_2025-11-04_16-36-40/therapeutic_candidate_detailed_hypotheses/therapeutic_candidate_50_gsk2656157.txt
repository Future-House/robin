Proposal for GSK2656157 (PERK inhibitor; Selleckchem S7309)

Below is our detailed evaluation of GSK2656157 for repurposing in dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD), structured in the requested format.

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
GSK2656157 is a small‐molecule inhibitor of PERK (protein kinase R–like endoplasmic reticulum kinase) that was discovered and optimized through medicinal‐chemistry efforts originally led by GlaxoSmithKline. This compound belongs to the class of ATP‐competitive, Type I inhibitors that target the kinase domain of PERK, binding within the ATP‐binding cleft and stabilizing a “DFG‐in, Cα‐helix shifted” conformation of the dephosphorylated kinase domain (Axten, 2017, pp. 15–19). Structurally, the compound is an indoline‐derived molecule; similar chemical series include the indoline/aminoquinazoline chemotypes that have been widely profiled for their high potency and kinase selectivity across panels exceeding 300 kinases (Axten, 2017, pp. 15–19). Originally, these inhibitors were developed as research tools to interrogate the role of PERK in the unfolded protein response (UPR) in oncology and neurodegeneration contexts, and in preclinical studies GSK2656157 demonstrated activity in suppressing tumor growth and angiogenesis in mouse xenograft models via inhibition of PERK autophosphorylation (Atkins et al., 2013, pp. 1–2, 8). As a synthetic small molecule, its origins lie in rational design based on X‐ray–guided structure–activity relationships, which enabled optimization for both high selectivity (with >100‐fold selectivity over related kinases including other eIF2α kinases as well as a broad kinome panel) and sufficient in vivo pharmacokinetics (Axten, 2017, pp. 15–19; Atkins et al., 2013, pp. 1–2). Thus, from a chemical and developmental perspective, GSK2656157 is a first‐in‐class PERK inhibitor that exemplifies how targeted kinase inhibition can be achieved by rational design.  

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, PERK inhibitors such as GSK2606414 and its more selective derivative GSK2656157 were developed primarily for indications in oncology and neurodegeneration. Preclinical studies in tumor xenograft models have demonstrated that oral dosing of GSK2656157 can suppress PERK-mediated signaling, resulting in attenuated phospho-eIF2α levels, diminished transcriptional responses of unfolded protein response (UPR) genes, and reduced tumor vascular density (Atkins et al., 2013, pp. 1–2, 8). In neurodegenerative models—for instance in prion and tauopathy mouse models—PERK inhibition has been shown to restore global protein synthesis, decrease the levels of phospho-tau, and produce mixed effects on behavior and neuronal survival (Rozpędek-Kamińska et al., 2020, pp. 17–19). Meanwhile, within the ocular space, experimental studies with the closely related PERK inhibitor GSK2606414 (a chemical analogue of GSK2656157) in in vitro retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cell models have demonstrated on-target inhibition of PERK signaling (evidenced by reduction in eIF2α phosphorylation) along with downstream alterations in stress markers such as CHOP and VEGF mRNA (Jiang et al., 2017, pp. 1–2). However, direct use of GSK2656157 in dry AMD or similar inflammatory/degenerative ocular conditions has not yet been reported in the literature. Some studies investigating retinal degeneration models, such as P23H rhodopsin transgenic rats, have suggested that PERK inhibition can worsen photoreceptor survival by enhancing misfolded protein aggregation, although these studies were typically conducted with compounds such as GSK2606414A (Athanasiou et al., 2017, pp. 1–2, 5–7). There is, therefore, limited clinical or preclinical history regarding the use of GSK2656157 specifically in AMD or related ocular diseases. In addition, while the compound has demonstrated acceptable pharmacokinetics and tolerability in preclinical oncology settings—with measurable systemic exposures—it is important to note that systemic PERK inhibition in multiple species has been associated with toxic liabilities such as pancreatic toxicity (Atkins et al., 2013, p. 8; Axten, 2017, pp. 1–7). Consequently, although the therapeutic class has a relatively rich history in oncology and neurodegeneration, its direct application to dry AMD remains a new and untested repurposing approach that requires careful consideration of pharmacodynamic impacts in ocular tissues and rigorous safety profiling.  

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, PERK functions as an endoplasmic reticulum (ER)–resident kinase that is activated upon the accumulation of misfolded proteins, a hallmark of chronic ER stress. Normally, when the ER’s folding capacity is overwhelmed, the molecular chaperone GRP78 dissociates from PERK, enabling PERK to oligomerize and autophosphorylate, which then enables it to phosphorylate the α subunit of eukaryotic initiation factor 2 (eIF2α) at serine-51 (Axten, 2017, pp. 7–11). Phosphorylation of eIF2α leads to a global reduction in protein synthesis—a protective adaptation that prevents further accumulation of misfolded proteins—and also selectively permits the translation of stress-induced genes, most notably ATF4. ATF4 subsequently upregulates transcription factors such as CHOP, which can drive pro-apoptotic signaling if ER stress persists (Axten, 2017, pp. 7–11; Chu et al., 2021, p. 8). In the context of dry AMD, chronic ER stress in RPE cells has been implicated in disrupting cellular proteostasis, where misfolded proteins—including subunits of key complexes like the vacuolar ATPase (v-ATPase)—may fail to fold properly, leading to impaired lysosomal acidification (Kaarniranta et al., 2018, pp. 1–4; Athanasiou et al., 2017, pp. 1–2). The hypothesis is that by inhibiting PERK with GSK2656157, the excessive phosphorylation of eIF2α and the maladaptive integrated stress response (ISR) can be attenuated. This would not only restore global protein synthesis but also prevent downstream consequences such as upregulation of CHOP and VEGF, thereby reducing pro-apoptotic as well as pro-inflammatory pathways (Jiang et al., 2017, pp. 1–2). Furthermore, it is posited that such normalization in the ISR could allow for proper folding and assembly of lysosomal enzymes and complexes, including the v-ATPase. Proper v-ATPase assembly is critical for maintaining low intraluminal pH in lysosomes, which is necessary for the activation and maturation of hydrolases like cathepsin D (Kaarniranta et al., 2018, pp. 1–4). In neuronal models, PERK inhibition has been associated with improvements in lysosomal function and proteostasis, indicating that reduction of chronic ER stress can indirectly favor the re-establishment of lysosomal homeostasis (Jiang et al., 2017, pp. 1–2). In essence, GSK2656157, by specifically targeting and inhibiting PERK, interferes with the pathological arm of the UPR (i.e. excessive eIF2α phosphorylation) that is hypothesized to contribute to impaired lysosomal acidification in RPE cells under chronic stress conditions—a mechanism that might be particularly relevant in the pathophysiology of dry AMD (Axten, 2017, pp. 24–28).  

Expected Effect:  
In the proposed assays, the primary expected effect of GSK2656157 is to inhibit PERK activity in RPE cells, as evidenced by reduced phosphorylation of eIF2α, and thereby dampen downstream signaling events that lead to the induction of ATF4, CHOP, and ultimately pro-apoptotic and proangiogenic factors such as VEGF. In vitro studies with analogues of GSK2656157 (such as GSK2606414) in ARPE-19 cells have shown dose-dependent inhibition of eIF2α phosphorylation with subsequent decreases in CHOP and VEGF mRNA expression without necessarily triggering apoptosis at higher doses (Jiang et al., 2017, pp. 1–2). These results provide a proof-of-concept that pharmacological inhibition of PERK can modulate this critical signaling pathway in RPE cells. The hypothesis for dry AMD is that chronic ER stress in the RPE disrupts lysosomal acidification via misfolding of v-ATPase subunits; under normal circumstances, proper v-ATPase function ensures that lysosomes maintain their acidic environment, which is essential for protease activation—including cathepsin D. Cathepsin D, in turn, is critical for lysosomal degradation of photoreceptor outer segments and for preventing lipofuscin accumulation, a pathological process in dry AMD (Kaarniranta et al., 2018, pp. 1–4). Thus, by reducing PERK activation through GSK2656157, it is expected that the overactive ISR would be tempered sufficiently to allow for the proper folding and assembly of v-ATPase complexes, thereby restoring lysosomal acidification (Chu et al., 2021, p. 8; Athanasiou et al., 2017, pp. 1–2). This restoration is anticipated to improve cathepsin D maturation and activity, leading to better clearance of intracellular debris and misfolded proteins, ultimately protecting RPE cells from stress-induced degeneration. On a cellular level, normalizing lysosomal pH would also promote autophagy flux, which is often impaired in the aged RPE, thereby mitigating one of the key contributing factors in the dry AMD phenotype (Kaarniranta et al., 2018, pp. 1–4; Athanasiou et al., 2017, pp. 1–2). Importantly, RPE cells are known to express PERK and components of the UPR, with studies demonstrating that ER stress markers, including phospho-PERK and phospho-eIF2α, are elevated in pathological states associated with AMD (Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021, pp. 11–12; Chu et al., 2021, p. 8). Therefore, if GSK2656157 can achieve adequate ocular tissue exposure and inhibit PERK activity in the RPE, the expected outcome would be a reduction in cellular stress–mediated apoptosis and proliferation of protective pathways that maintain lysosomal and autophagy functions (Jiang et al., 2017, pp. 1–2; Rozpędek-Kamińska et al., 2020, pp. 17–19). Moreover, the compound’s known pharmacokinetic profile in preclinical oncology studies suggests that it is systemically bioavailable and, according to some reports, may achieve ocular tissue distribution; thus it is conceivable that with appropriate dosing and local delivery strategies, GSK2656157 could achieve effective concentrations in RPE cells (Atkins et al., 2013, pp. 1–2, 8).  

Overall Evaluation:  
Overall, GSK2656157 presents an intriguing therapeutic candidate for dry AMD based on a mechanistic hypothesis that links chronic ER stress to impaired lysosomal acidification—a process central to the pathogenesis of AMD. One of the primary strengths of GSK2656157 is its potent and selective inhibition of PERK, a critical mediator of the integrated stress response. The compound’s design as an ATP‐competitive PERK inhibitor places it squarely in a class of drugs that have been well characterized in terms of kinase selectivity and in vivo pharmacodynamics in oncology settings (Axten, 2017, pp. 15–19). The underlying biological rationale is that by attenuating the maladaptive PERK-mediated phosphorylation of eIF2α, it may be possible to relieve chronic translational suppression and dampen downstream deleterious signaling cascades—thereby re-establishing normal lysosomal function through proper v-ATPase assembly and restoration of cathepsin D activity (Axten, 2017, pp. 7–11; Chu et al., 2021, p. 8). There is supportive in vitro evidence from studies using related inhibitors (for example, GSK2606414 in ARPE-19 cells) that PERK inhibition can downregulate stress effectors such as CHOP and VEGF, which are both implicated in retinal degeneration and neovascularization (Jiang et al., 2017, pp. 1–2). Such studies underscore the potential of targeting the PERK/ISR axis in RPE cells to create a more favorable intracellular environment that could prevent progressive degeneration seen in dry AMD (Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021, pp. 11–12; Chu et al., 2021, p. 8).

However, there are several notable weaknesses and caveats. First, while there is an extensive body of preclinical work on PERK inhibitors in oncology and neurodegeneration models, there is a significant lack of direct evidence in ocular disease models—especially chronic models of dry AMD—as well as an absence of published studies specifically evaluating GSK2656157 in RPE cells or in animal models of AMD (Athanasiou et al., 2017, pp. 5–7). In several studies using related PERK inhibitors in rod photoreceptor degeneration models (such as P23H rhodopsin transgenic rats), inhibition of PERK activity has been associated with worsened outcomes, suggesting that PERK activation may paradoxically be protective in certain contexts of protein misfolding (Athanasiou et al., 2017, pp. 1–2, 5–7). This dichotomous role of PERK—as both an adaptive and a pro-death signal depending on the context and duration of activation—raises concerns about whether inhibition will uniformly yield beneficial effects in the RPE, whose balance of ER stress responses may differ from that of neuronal or secretory tissues. Second, while the hypothesis postulates that chronic ER stress impairs lysosomal acidification by misfolding v-ATPase subunits, the direct link between PERK inhibition and restoration of v-ATPase assembly or cathepsin D maturation in RPE cells has not been firmly established in the literature. Data that explicitly demonstrate that PERK inhibition rescues lysosomal pH, enhances v-ATPase function, and improves cathepsin D activity in neuronal models may exist, yet similar studies in ocular models are limited (Chu et al., 2021, p. 8; Kaarniranta et al., 2018, pp. 1–4). Third, the systemic administration of PERK inhibitors such as GSK2656157 has been associated with pancreatic toxicity and other systemic adverse effects in preclinical studies, a liability that could be particularly problematic in chronic therapies such as those needed for dry AMD (Atkins et al., 2013, p. 8; Axten, 2017, pp. 1–7). Although strategies such as local (intravitreal) delivery might mitigate these systemic risks, ocular pharmacokinetic and tolerability data are sparse, and the tissue-specific distribution of GSK2656157 in the eye would need careful evaluation (Atkins et al., 2013, pp. 1–2, 8). Fourth, there is some concern over off-target effects; for example, early reports on similar GSK compounds noted possible inhibition of RIP1 kinase activity, although subsequent studies have argued that the effects observed were indeed mediated by PERK inhibition (Starr, 2019, pp. 133–137; Athanasiou et al., 2017, pp. 5–7). Nonetheless, affording absolute selectivity in a clinical candidate remains a challenge. In addition, chronic inhibition of the adaptive aspects of the ISR may inadvertently impair protective mechanisms in the retina, as transient ISR activation is known to mediate cellular adaptation during acute stress (Axten, 2017, pp. 7–11, 24–28).

In summary, while the mechanistic rationale for using GSK2656157 to address dry AMD is based on a conceptually appealing hypothesis—that PERK inhibition will relieve chronic ER stress, thereby restoring proper lysosomal acidification, enabling v-ATPase assembly, and rescuing cathepsin D activity in RPE cells—the current body of literature reveals both promising on-target effects in non-ocular systems and significant gaps in ocular-specific efficacy and safety data. Given the complex, context-dependent role of PERK signaling in retinal homeostasis and the potential for deleterious outcomes when PERK is inhibited in models of protein misfolding, further focused in vitro and in vivo studies are required to determine whether GSK2656157 can be safely and effectively repurposed for dry AMD. Strengths of this candidate include its potent, selective PERK inhibition and preclinical pharmacodynamic proof-of-concept in related disease models, while weaknesses center on limited ocular data, potential systemic and off-target toxicities, and an uncertain impact on lysosomal and proteostatic pathways in RPE cells. Ultimately, while GSK2656157 remains an attractive avenue for targeting an upstream stress node in dry AMD pathogenesis, careful optimization of dosing, targeted ocular delivery, and comprehensive evaluation in relevant AMD models will be essential before moving forward in clinical development (Axten, 2017, pp. 1–7, 15–19, 24–28; Atkins et al., 2013, pp. 1–2; Athanasiou et al., 2017, pp. 5–7; Jiang et al., 2017, pp. 1–2).
