Proposal for Bexarotene (Sigma B1269)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Bexarotene, commercially known as Targretin and referenced here as Sigma B1269, is a fully synthetic rexinoid that was originally designed as a selective agonist for the retinoid X receptor (RXR). It is structurally distinct from naturally occurring retinoids such as 9‐cis‐retinoic acid; its chemical structure consists of a lipophilic tetramethyltetralin moiety connected through an ethynyl linker to a benzoic acid group, a design that confers high binding affinity (nanomolar EC50 values) and specificity for RXR subtypes (de Almeida & Conda‐Sheridan, 2019, pp. 8–9; Jurutka et al., 2022, pp. 2–3). As a member of the rexinoid class, bexarotene belongs to a group of therapeutic compounds that were developed to overcome the limitations of pan‐retinoid receptor activation by offering a more targeted means of modulating nuclear receptor signaling. RXRs have the unique ability to form either homodimers or heterodimers with a wide range of nuclear receptors—including peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptors (PPARs), liver X receptors (LXRs), thyroid hormone receptors (TRs), and vitamin D receptors (VDRs)—thereby influencing diverse gene expression programs that control cell growth, differentiation, and lipid metabolism (de Almeida & Conda‐Sheridan, 2019, pp. 8–9; Heck et al., 2016, pp. 1–2; Lalloyer et al., 2009, pp. 1–2). The synthetic origin of bexarotene, achieved through sophisticated organic synthesis that optimizes its lipophilic and acidic domains, enables it to engage conserved ligand‐binding residues (for example, key arginine and hydrophobic amino acids) in the RXR ligand‐binding domain, ensuring potent activation of the receptor (Chitranshi et al., 2019, p. 9; de Almeida & Conda‐Sheridan, 2019, pp. 11–13). This profile not only underlies its efficacy in its original oncologic indications but also supports its potential for repurposing in other diseases where RXR signaling is of therapeutic interest.

Therapeutic History:  
Bexarotene is well established in clinical practice as the first FDA-approved RXR-selective agonist, primarily indicated for the treatment of refractory cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (CTCL) in patients who have failed at least one systemic therapy (de Almeida & Conda‐Sheridan, 2019, pp. 8–9; Jurutka et al., 2022, pp. 2–3). Over the years, extensive clinical experience with bexarotene has been accrued in oncology, with studies demonstrating its capacity to induce cancer cell arrest, differentiation, and apoptosis by modulating the transcriptional landscape via RXR-mediated gene regulation (Heck et al., 2016, pp. 1–2, 9–10). Beyond its original role in oncology, bexarotene has been investigated in various other contexts; preclinical studies have explored its effects in non-small cell lung cancer, acute myeloid leukemia, and even in some hormone-sensitive malignancies—owing to its ability to interfere with proliferative signaling pathways (Marshall et al., 2015, pp. 2–3; Dheer et al., 2018, pp. 1–2). Notably, recent investigations have also assessed bexarotene in neurodegenerative disease models, particularly Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease, where its RXR-mediated upregulation of apolipoprotein E (ApoE) and cholesterol transporters such as ABCA1 have been postulated to facilitate amyloid-beta clearance and neuronal protection (Marshall et al., 2015, pp. 2–3; Mallick et al., 2021, pp. 1–2). Although the compound has not been directly tested in clinical trials for dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD), its mechanism of action—centered on modulating lipid metabolism and cellular clearance pathways—suggests that its repurposing for dry AMD is scientifically plausible, especially given the involvement of dysregulated lipid homeostasis and lysosomal dysfunction in this condition (Chitranshi et al., 2019, pp. 1–2; de Almeida & Conda‐Sheridan, 2019, pp. 8–9). Furthermore, RXR signaling is known to be critical in maintaining retinal homeostasis, and studies in animal models have shown that disruption of RXR function can lead to retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) abnormalities, providing an indirect rationale for evaluating RXR agonists like bexarotene in ocular applications (Chitranshi et al., 2019, pp. 1–2; Lalloyer et al., 2009, pp. 1–2).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, bexarotene exerts its effects by binding selectively to the ligand-binding domain of RXRs with high affinity, thereby promoting conformational changes that favor the release of co-repressor proteins and the recruitment of co-activators containing LxxLL motifs. This binding event induces RXR to function either as a homodimer or, more importantly, as a heterodimer with other nuclear receptors such as LXR and PPAR, which are central to the regulation of lipid homeostasis and inflammatory responses (de Almeida & Conda‐Sheridan, 2019, pp. 8–9; Marshall et al., 2015, pp. 2–3). In its role as an RXR agonist, bexarotene has been shown to markedly upregulate the transcription of genes involved in cholesterol efflux, including those coding for ATP-binding cassette transporters like ABCA1 and for apolipoproteins such as ApoE; these factors are critical for mobilizing and clearing excess cholesterol in various tissues (Marshall et al., 2015, pp. 2–3; Mallick et al., 2021, pp. 2–3). Furthermore, RXR agonism by bexarotene can stimulate the activity of permissive heterodimers—such as RXR/PPARα—which are known to play pivotal roles in fatty acid oxidation and energy metabolism. This activation is hypothesized to extend to downstream transcriptional regulators like TFEB, the master regulator of lysosomal biogenesis, though direct evidence in retinal cells is still emerging (Marshall et al., 2015, pp. 2–3; Dheer et al., 2018, pp. 1–2). In several preclinical models, bexarotene has been associated with the modulation of lysosomal pathways; for instance, it can alleviate endoplasmic reticulum stress and prevent the activation of pro-apoptotic proteins, which are important contributors to intracellular lipid accumulation and cellular dysfunction (Farol & Hymes, 2004, pp. 1–2; Lalloyer et al., 2009, pp. 1–2). Moreover, its selective RXR activity circumvents the adverse effects associated with pan-retinoid receptor activation, particularly the hypercalcemic and other toxicities seen with non-selective retinoids, by limiting cross-reactivity with RARs (Jurutka et al., 2022, pp. 2–3; Mallick et al., 2021, pp. 2–3). In summary, bexarotene’s multifaceted mechanism includes (1) robust activation of RXR-mediated transcription of lipid transporter genes, (2) modulation of heterodimer partners such as PPARα and LXR that indirectly regulate lipid metabolism and energy balance, and (3) potential upregulation of lysosomal biogenesis and function via TFEB-associated pathways—collectively contributing to improved cellular lipid clearance and metabolic homeostasis (Marshall et al., 2015, pp. 2–3; Mallick et al., 2021, pp. 1–2; Heck et al., 2016, pp. 7–9).

Expected Effect in Dry Age-related Macular Degeneration:  
The central hypothesis driving the evaluation of bexarotene for dry AMD posits that activation of RXR in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells will lead to beneficial metabolic remodeling, specifically through enhancing cholesterol efflux and promoting lysosomal function. In the context of dry AMD, pathologic drusen formation is largely a consequence of chronic accumulation of lipids and misprocessed extracellular debris under the RPE. Through the activation of RXR, bexarotene is expected to upregulate key mediators of cholesterol transport such as ABCA1 and ApoE, thereby facilitating the removal of excess cholesterol and lipid deposits from the RPE (Marshall et al., 2015, pp. 2–3; Chitranshi et al., 2019, pp. 1–2). In addition, RXR activation, particularly when partnered with PPARα, has been associated with the induction of a lysosomal program that involves enhanced expression of genes regulating lysosomal acidification—potentially via the upregulation of vacuolar ATPase (V-ATPase) subunits—and the activation of lysosomal enzymes such as cathepsin D (CTSD) (Marshall et al., 2015, pp. 2–3; Mallick et al., 2021, pp. 2–3). Although direct demonstrations of TFEB activation in RPE models with bexarotene treatment are limited in the provided literature, the established role of RXR agonists in modulating lipid-rich, lysosomal compartments in other cell types supports the likelihood that a similar upregulation would occur in RPE cells (Marshall et al., 2015, pp. 2–3; Dheer et al., 2018, pp. 1–2). Given that TFEB is a master regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and function, its coactivation alongside PPARα could enhance the degradative capacity of the cell, resulting in improved clearance of drusen-associated lipid aggregates. This dual action—promoting cholesterol efflux while simultaneously enhancing lysosomal catabolism—addresses two of the key pathogenic mechanisms underlying dry AMD (Marshall et al., 2015, pp. 2–3; Mallick et al., 2021, pp. 1–2). In vitro, one might anticipate that bexarotene treatment of cultured RPE cells will lead to increased transcript and protein levels of ABC transporters, elevated markers of lysosomal acidification such as V-ATPase subunits, and enhanced activity of cathepsin D, collectively contributing to the reduction of lipid overload at a cellular level (Marshall et al., 2015, pp. 2–3; de Almeida & Conda‐Sheridan, 2019, pp. 8–9). Moreover, given its extensive clinical use in oncology with a tolerable ocular safety profile, bexarotene is well situated for repurposing, provided that its systemic adverse effects (e.g., hyperlipidemia, hypothyroidism) can be managed or mitigated through controlled dosing strategies or targeted delivery approaches (de Almeida & Conda‐Sheridan, 2019, pp. 8–9; Marshall et al., 2015, pp. 2–3).

Overall Evaluation:  
Bexarotene emerges as a promising therapeutic candidate for dry AMD based on its well-characterized pharmacological profile and multifaceted mechanism of action. One of its primary strengths lies in its selective activation of RXR, which has been shown to trigger transcriptional programs that enhance cholesterol efflux via the upregulation of key transporters such as ABCA1 and ApoE—pathways that are directly relevant to the clearance of drusen-associated lipids in dry AMD (Marshall et al., 2015, pp. 2–3; Mallick et al., 2021, pp. 1–2). In addition, the potential for RXR agonism to engage PPARα and, by extension, coactivate TFEB offers a novel approach to correcting lysosomal dysfunction—a critical factor in the pathogenesis of dry AMD. This dual action addresses both the extracellular accumulation of lipids and the intracellular deficits in degradative capacity that contribute to RPE dysfunction. The extensive clinical experience with bexarotene in cancer therapy provides an added advantage, as its pharmacokinetics, dosing regimens, and safety profiles are well documented, thereby facilitating a more rapid transition into repurposing studies. However, several challenges and weaknesses need to be acknowledged. Bexarotene is known to cause significant metabolic side effects, including hyperlipidemia and hypothyroidism, which may necessitate adjunctive treatments (such as lipid-lowering agents) or the development of targeted ocular delivery systems to minimize systemic exposure (de Almeida & Conda‐Sheridan, 2019, pp. 8–9; Mallick et al., 2021, pp. 2–3). Furthermore, although preclinical data in neurodegenerative models have demonstrated beneficial effects on lipid metabolism and lysosomal pathways, direct evidence in retinal pigment epithelial models is still limited. This gap underscores the need for rigorous in vitro and in vivo studies specifically designed to evaluate bexarotene’s efficacy in RPE cells and animal models of dry AMD (Chitranshi et al., 2019, pp. 1–2; Lalloyer et al., 2009, pp. 1–2). Another potential risk involves the compound’s polypharmacological nature; while its ability to interact with multiple nuclear receptors can be advantageous for targeting complex diseases, it also raises the possibility of off-target effects, such as its documented antagonism at PPARγ in certain contexts, which could have unforeseen impacts on retinal cell physiology (Marciano et al., 2015, pp. 1–2; Heck et al., 2016, pp. 7–9). The balance between therapeutic benefits and adverse effects will need to be carefully managed, perhaps by optimizing dosing regimens or by designing analogues with improved selectivity and reduced side effect profiles. In summary, the repurposing of bexarotene for dry AMD is supported by its robust activation of RXR-driven pathways related to lipid efflux and lysosomal function, as well as by its safe clinical history in ocular settings when used topically or in controlled systemic regimens. Nonetheless, additional preclinical work is essential to directly demonstrate its efficacy in RPE models and to delineate any ocular-specific pharmacodynamic parameters. Overall, the candidate shows promise as a multi‐pathway therapeutic approach for dry AMD, with strengths including its defined mechanism of action and extensive clinical history, and weaknesses largely centered on potential systemic side effects and the need for targeted delivery strategies (de Almeida & Conda‐Sheridan, 2019, pp. 8–9; Marshall et al., 2015, pp. 2–3; Chitranshi et al., 2019, pp. 1–2; Mallick et al., 2021, pp. 1–2; Marciano et al., 2015, pp. 1–2).
