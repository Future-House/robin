Proposal for Temsirolimus (Torisel; Pfizer)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Temsirolimus (CCI-779; Torisel) is a semi-synthetic ester prodrug of sirolimus (rapamycin) discovered during rapamycin medicinal chemistry optimization to improve formulation properties and pharmacokinetics; it is a first-generation “rapalog” that inhibits mTOR by forming an intracellular complex with FKBP12 and binding the FRB domain of mTOR, thereby allosterically inhibiting mTORC1 catalytic signaling (and only indirectly affecting mTORC2) (Wang et al., 2022). As a class, rapalogs are derived from the natural macrolide rapamycin originally isolated from Streptomyces hygroscopicus; they have been used systemically as immunosuppressants and anti-proliferative/anti-angiogenic agents in oncology and transplant medicine by suppressing mTORC1-dependent growth and inflammatory programs (Wang et al., 2022; Johnson, 2013). mTORC1 is a lysosome-proximal signaling hub that integrates amino acid sufficiency via the Ragulator–Rag GTPase machinery and growth/energy signals via Rheb/AMPK, coordinating anabolism (protein synthesis via S6K and 4E-BP1), and repressing catabolism (autophagy and lysosomal biogenesis) (Johnson, 2013). In ocular biology, temsirolimus has been evaluated preclinically in human retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC), consistently demonstrating dose-dependent inhibition of proliferation and migration and decreased VEGF/PDGF expression through mTOR pathway inhibition, supporting target engagement in retinal cell types relevant to AMD pathogenesis (Liegl et al., 2014). Compared with rapamycin, temsirolimus has higher aqueous solubility and stability but is administered intravenously in approved oncology indications; adverse events include metabolic and mucosal toxicities typical of systemic mTOR inhibition (Wang et al., 2022).

Therapeutic History  
Rapamycin/sirolimus and rapalogs have a long clinical history across transplantation and oncology; in ophthalmology, mechanistic and translational work has targeted mTOR for AMD and other retinal diseases because mTORC1 suppresses autophagy/lysosomal programs and promotes inflammatory and angiogenic signaling (Wang et al., 2022; Nita & Grzybowski, 2023). In RPE models that recapitulate daily phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments (POS), mTORC1 activity is dynamically regulated: POS phagocytosis transiently activates mTORC1, followed by inhibition during phagosome maturation; however, in aged RPE and AMD, mTORC1 activity is relatively elevated and TFEB is retained in the phosphorylated cytosolic state, impairing lysosomal responses and favoring lipofuscin-like autofluorescent granule (AFG) accumulation (Falcão et al., 2024; Falcão et al., 2025). In these systems, rapamycin reduces POS-dependent AFGs by about 30% while increasing TFEB activity and lysosomal biogenesis markers (e.g., LAMP1) in ARPE-19 and human fetal RPE cells; efficacy generalizes to other mTOR pathway inhibitors (e.g., INK-128), and constitutively active TFEB provides larger effects, supporting a TFEB-centric mechanism (Falcão et al., 2024). These effects require intact lysosomal proteolysis; pharmacologic blockade of lysosomal acid lipase, cathepsin inhibition, or cathepsin D (CTSD) knockout abrogates rapamycin’s benefit, highlighting a dependence on functional lysosomes (Falcão et al., 2024). Although these data do not directly test temsirolimus in lysosome-TFEB endpoints, temsirolimus is mechanistically a rapalog mTORC1 inhibitor, and in RPE it engages mTOR biology by suppressing growth factor signaling outputs (VEGF/PDGF) and cell motility/proliferation, thereby validating target engagement in the relevant cell type (Liegl et al., 2014).

Clinically, multiple sirolimus trials have evaluated mTOR inhibition in geographic atrophy (GA), a late stage of dry AMD, with disappointing outcomes. Subconjunctival sirolimus (440 µg every 3 months for 24 months) in a bilateral-eye, fellow-eye-control study did not slow GA enlargement and was associated with a treatment-associated decrease in visual acuity relative to untreated fellow eyes, despite an acceptable safety profile in that study (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2008; Wang et al., 2022). An intravitreal sirolimus Phase I/II study in bilateral GA was discontinued early, and separate trials reported ocular adverse events including accelerated retinal thinning, abnormal perilesional autofluorescence, and sterile endophthalmitis; overall, no structural or functional benefit was demonstrated across these programs (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2011; Wang et al., 2022). These clinical observations raise concern that chronic mTOR inhibition in established GA may be ineffective or even detrimental, potentially due to disease stage–specific network effects of autophagy and survival signaling in degenerating retina (Wang et al., 2022). In contrast, preclinical data in other retinal contexts (e.g., glaucoma) show neuroprotective correlates of mTOR inhibition, underscoring the context- and stage-dependence of therapeutic effects (Wang et al., 2022). For wet AMD and retinal vascular pathology, temsirolimus (and rapamycin) exert anti-angiogenic actions in vitro and in animal models by downregulating VEGF/PDGF and endothelial proliferation, which are less directly relevant to GA biology but establish ocular pharmacology for the class (Liegl et al., 2014; Johnson, 2013). Drug delivery innovations such as synthetic HDL nanoparticles carrying rapamycin have been explored preclinically to optimize ocular delivery, suggesting format-dependent translatability may be important for this class (Falcão et al., 2024).

Mechanism of Action  
Temsirolimus acts as a prototypical rapalog: it binds FKBP12 to form a gain-of-function complex that docks to the FKBP12-rapamycin binding (FRB) domain of mTOR and selectively inhibits mTORC1 signaling; mTORC2 is generally spared acutely, with potential indirect effects chronically depending on cellular context (Wang et al., 2022). mTORC1 normally phosphorylates TFEB on multiple serine residues, retaining it in the cytosol and preventing nuclear entry; when mTORC1 is inhibited, TFEB becomes dephosphorylated, translocates to the nucleus, and drives the CLEAR network that upregulates autophagy and lysosomal biogenesis genes (e.g., LAMP1), enhancing degradative capacity and organelle biogenesis (Falcão et al., 2025; Falcão et al., 2024). In RPE cells, POS phagocytosis engages mTORC1 transiently, but aged/AMD RPE exhibit enhanced mTORC1 activity that suppresses appropriate TFEB activation; rapamycin reverses this, promoting TFEB dephosphorylation, nuclear entry, GADD34 upregulation, and increased lysosomal mass, collectively reducing AFGs by approximately 30% in POS-challenged RPE (Falcão et al., 2024). The TFEB dependency has been functionally validated via TFEB gain-of-function, which markedly decreases AFGs in both preventive and “therapeutic” paradigms after lipofuscin-like material has already formed (Falcão et al., 2024). Pharmacologic relevance to mTOR pathway modulation beyond rapamycin was supported using INK-128 and other mTOR inhibitors, which replicated the AFG reduction and TFEB-lysosome signature (Falcão et al., 2024).

At the biochemical level, mTORC1 integrates amino acid signaling at the lysosomal surface via Ragulator-Rag GTPases and growth/energy cues via Rheb/AMPK, coordinating downstream phosphorylation of p70S6K and 4E-BP1 to activate translation while repressing catabolic programs including initiation of autophagy (partly by phosphorylating ULK1 at Ser757 and by blocking TFEB) (Johnson, 2013; Chang et al., 2022). In retinal tissues, mTOR inhibition is expected to relieve brakes on autophagy and lysosomal biogenesis; in diabetic retinopathy models, mTOR inhibition with rapamycin restored impaired lysosomal proteolysis by normalizing cathepsin L activity and autophagic flux, mechanistically consistent with TFEB-driven CLEAR network activation and improved lysosomal protease capacity (Wang et al., 2022). Although direct evidence for temsirolimus effects on RPE TFEB phosphorylation state, lysosomal acidification, or CTSD maturation is not available, the shared mechanism of FKBP12-dependent mTORC1 inhibition and the RPE-cell mTOR engagement demonstrated by temsirolimus (antiproliferative/anti-migratory, VEGF/PDGF suppression) support pathway perturbation in the correct cellular context (Liegl et al., 2014).

In the context of AMD pathophysiology, RPE lysosomal dysfunction and impaired clearance of POS-derived material drive lipofuscin accumulation and downstream toxicity; TFEB has emerged as a master regulator to restore lysosomal capacity, while AMD donor RPE show reduced cathepsin D and L levels/activities, highlighting protease insufficiency as a plausible node of therapeutic rescue (Falcão et al., 2025). Importantly, in POS-fed RPE, the rapamycin benefit is abolished by lysosomal protease inhibition or CTSD knockout, indicating that mTORC1 inhibition enhances clearance only when lysosomal protease function is preserved, and suggesting that concomitant strategies to support lysosomal acidification/protease maturation could be synergistic (Falcão et al., 2024). Finally, some reports have suggested low immunoreactivity of mTORC1/2 components in RPE, but this observation contrasts with multiple functional studies demonstrating mTORC1 signaling in RPE during POS handling and aging; taken together, functional responsiveness to mTOR inhibitors in RPE likely depends on model system and detection sensitivity (Wang et al., 2022; Falcão et al., 2024).

Expected Effect  
In a POS-pulse/chase AFG assay using ARPE-19 or human fetal RPE, temsirolimus is expected to inhibit mTORC1 (decreased pS6 phosphorylation), dephosphorylate TFEB, increase TFEB nuclear localization, upregulate CLEAR network/lysosomal biogenesis genes (e.g., GADD34, LAMP1), and reduce AFG burden by enhancing degradation of phagocytosed material; this mirrors rapamycin’s effects (~30% AFG reduction) and those of other mTOR inhibitors in these models (Falcão et al., 2024). To confirm on-target activity and pathway alignment, assays should include pS6/S6 and pTFEB/TFEB immunoblotting at 2–24 h, TFEB nuclear translocation imaging, LAMP1 immunostaining for lysosomal mass, and flow cytometry quantification of AFGs after 24–72 h chase, consistent with established methodologies (Falcão et al., 2024). Because rapamycin’s benefits are abolished by lysosomal protease blockade and in CTSD-deficient backgrounds, temsirolimus-dependent AFG reduction is predicted to require functional cathepsin D and lipases; co-assaying CTSD maturation (pro-/mature CTSD ratio), lysosomal pH, and acid lipase activity is therefore crucial to mechanistic validation (Falcão et al., 2024). In parallel, transcriptomic or targeted RT-qPCR panels for CLEAR genes will validate TFEB program engagement (Falcão et al., 2024).

Regarding lysosomal acidification and protease activity, there is mechanistic plausibility but no direct demonstration that temsirolimus enhances lysosomal pH or CTSD maturation in RPE; nevertheless, the TFEB-centric model predicts improved lysosomal proteostasis, and related retinal models show that mTOR inhibition can restore impaired lysosomal proteolysis (cathepsin L) and autophagic flux, supporting the hypothesis that temsirolimus could favorably influence protease maturation/activity in RPE (Wang et al., 2022; Falcão et al., 2025). Notably, independent approaches in RPE can acidify lysosomes and reduce AFGs, underscoring the tractability of the acidification axis, though this was not via mTOR inhibition; combining mTORC1-TFEB activation with direct acidification strategies could be additive (Falcão et al., 2024). The expectation for disease modification in GA derives from the idea that sustained enhancement of lysosomal clearance will reduce lipofuscin burden, preserve RPE function, and slow atrophic expansion; however, prior GA trials with sirolimus showed no benefit and potential harm, implying that either the disease stage, delivery/exposure, or network consequences of chronic mTORC1 inhibition negate the anticipated benefit in late disease (Wang et al., 2022).

Temsirolimus target expression and pharmacology in RPE are supported by in vitro data showing dose-dependent inhibition of RPE proliferation and migration and suppression of hypoxia-induced VEGF/PDGF increases without overt cytotoxicity at low micromolar to millimolar nominal concentrations; these endpoints corroborate mTOR pathway engagement in RPE and provide pharmacodynamic markers (e.g., VEGF suppression) that can be measured alongside TFEB-lysosomal readouts in AFG assays (Liegl et al., 2014). The drug class’s ocular delivery options range from systemic dosing (with significant class-typical adverse events) to local delivery; sirolimus trials used subconjunctival or intravitreal administration but encountered safety/tolerability signals including sterile endophthalmitis and accelerated retinal thinning with no efficacy in GA, emphasizing the need for alternative delivery technologies (e.g., nanoparticles) and stage-appropriate patient selection if pursuing this mechanism (Wang et al., 2022; Falcão et al., 2024). Finally, given AMD donor RPE deficiencies in cathepsins D/L, co-therapies or genetic activation strategies that more directly drive TFEB or augment lysosomal protease capacity could be required to realize the full benefit of mTORC1 inhibition in human disease (Falcão et al., 2025).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
- Mechanism-based rationale with cell-type congruence: RPE lysosomal dysfunction is central to AMD, and mTORC1 directly represses TFEB and lysosomal programs; in POS-fed RPE, mTORC1 inhibition with rapamycin reduces AFG burden by ~30%, increases lysosomal biogenesis (LAMP1), and requires intact lysosomal proteases, aligning precisely with the hypothesized MoA (Falcão et al., 2024). The effect generalizes across mTOR inhibitors and is amplified by TFEB gain-of-function, strengthening the pathway argument (Falcão et al., 2024).  
- Direct evidence that temsirolimus engages mTOR signaling in RPE: temsirolimus suppresses proliferation/migration and reduces VEGF/PDGF in primary human RPE, indicating pharmacodynamic activity and target engagement in the disease-relevant cell type (Liegl et al., 2014).  
- Broad mechanistic plausibility for enhancing lysosomal proteolysis: mTORC1 inhibition activates TFEB; retinal disease models show restoration of lysosomal protease activity (cathepsin L) and autophagic flux with rapamycin, consistent with improved lysosomal proteostasis, which could translate to better CTSD maturation in RPE even though that specific nexus is unproven for temsirolimus (Wang et al., 2022; Falcão et al., 2025).  
- Drug class knowledge base and formulation opportunities: extensive class experience informs safety risk assessment; emerging ocular delivery strategies (e.g., sHDL rapamycin nanoparticles) might overcome exposure constraints seen in prior sirolimus GA trials (Falcão et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022).

Weaknesses and Risks  
- Negative/unsafe clinical precedents in GA with class analog: multiple sirolimus trials in GA failed to slow lesion growth and revealed adverse ocular events, including accelerated retinal thinning and procedure-related sterile endophthalmitis, suggesting either mechanistic inadequacy at late stages, harmful network effects of chronic mTORC1 inhibition, or delivery-related issues; this materially lowers clinical plausibility for GA-stage disease (Wang et al., 2022).  
- Absence of temsirolimus-specific TFEB/lysosome data in RPE: while rapamycin demonstrates TFEB activation, lysosomal biogenesis, and AFG reduction, comparable mechanistic data for temsirolimus in RPE are lacking; the program would therefore carry translatability risk until head-to-head, mechanistic RPE assays confirm TFEB engagement and AFG reduction with temsirolimus (Falcão et al., 2024; Liegl et al., 2014).  
- Dependence on intact lysosomes and proteases: rapamycin’s benefit disappears with lipase inhibition or CTSD deficiency; AMD donor RPE show reduced cathepsin D/L, implying that many patient retinas may not have sufficient residual lysosomal capacity for mTORC1 inhibition alone to succeed without adjuncts that restore acidification/protease maturation (Falcão et al., 2024; Falcão et al., 2025).  
- Context and stage dependence of mTOR biology: autophagy upregulation that is protective in early disease can exacerbate neuronal apoptosis in late AMD; cell-type–specific and duration-dependent consequences of mTOR inhibition complicate chronic treatment in GA and may negate benefits or cause harm (Wang et al., 2022).  
- Systemic formulation constraints and ocular delivery safety: temsirolimus is an IV drug with known systemic toxicities; local ocular administration for a chronic indication must overcome prior safety/tolerability issues observed with sirolimus injections while achieving sustained, intraretinal/RPE-targeted exposure (Wang et al., 2022).

Go/No-Go Perspective and Development Recommendations  
- Indication selection: Given the negative GA clinical history with sirolimus, prioritize early/intermediate dry AMD with measurable lipofuscin/AFG burden and residual RPE lysosomal capacity, rather than established GA, where class effects have been neutral or detrimental (Falcão et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022).  
- Mechanistic bridging for temsirolimus: Run definitive RPE mechanistic studies comparing temsirolimus to rapamycin and an ATP-competitive mTOR inhibitor (e.g., INK-128) using POS-pulse/chase AFG assays, pS6/pTFEB readouts, TFEB nuclear localization, LAMP1, lysosomal pH, acid lipase, and CTSD maturation/activity, with rescue and block experiments to confirm TFEB dependence and lysosome-requirement (Falcão et al., 2024).  
- Combination strategies: Evaluate temsirolimus with lysosome-acidifying agents or TFEB-direct activators/gene transfer to overcome the protease dependency bottleneck evident in AMD RPE; mechanistic review suggests dual TFEB/mTOR and NRF2/KEAP1 modulation could be synergistic but requires careful control to avoid oncogenic risk (Falcão et al., 2025).  
- Delivery innovation: Avoid frequent intravitreal bolusing; explore sustained-release local delivery or targeted nanoparticles (e.g., sHDL-like) to deliver rapalogs selectively to RPE/retina at sub-toxic exposures, learning from prior sirolimus delivery failures (Falcão et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022).  
- Translational biomarkers: In early clinical exploration, incorporate pharmacodynamic biomarkers of TFEB activation (e.g., aqueous humor proteomics for CLEAR targets, retinal LAMP1 imaging if feasible), structural endpoints sensitive to RPE health (fundus autofluorescence for lipofuscin load), and stringent safety monitoring for retinal thinning and inflammation to detect class-typical adverse signals early (Falcão et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022).

Bottom line: The TFEB-lysosome rationale is robust at the cellular level in RPE, but the rapalog class has failed to translate benefit in GA; temsirolimus lacks direct RPE TFEB/lysosomal evidence and carries delivery/safety liabilities observed with sirolimus in GA. As a repurposing candidate, temsirolimus merits mechanistic validation in RPE with TFEB/lysosome endpoints and, if positive, a carefully designed, stage-appropriate pilot in early/intermediate dry AMD using optimized delivery; it is not a priority candidate for GA without compelling new mechanistic and translational data that overcome the class’s negative clinical precedent (Falcão et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022).
