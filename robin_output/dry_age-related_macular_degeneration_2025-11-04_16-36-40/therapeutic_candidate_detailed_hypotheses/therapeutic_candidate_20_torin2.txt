Proposal for Torin2 (Selleckchem S2813)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Torin2 is a synthetic, second-generation ATP-competitive inhibitor of the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) kinase designed to inhibit both mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1) and complex 2 (mTORC2) with high potency and favorable pharmacodynamic properties compared to first-generation allosteric mTOR inhibitors (rapamycin/rapalogs) and earlier catalytic mTOR kinase inhibitors (TORKi) such as PP242 and AZD8055 (Liu et al., 2013, pp. 7–10). Chemically optimized by structure-guided medicinal chemistry efforts, Torin2 exhibits rapid dephosphorylation of canonical mTORC1 substrates S6K1 (T389) and 4E-BP1 (T37/46) including rapamycin-insensitive sites, and of the mTORC2 substrate Akt (S473), reflecting dual-complex inhibition that is superior to rapamycin’s mTORC1-limited activity (with incomplete suppression of 4E-BP1) (Liu et al., 2013, pp. 7–10). In head-to-head comparisons with other ATP-competitive mTOR inhibitors, Torin2 maintains stronger and more sustained suppression of mTORC1/2 signaling after drug washout, attributable to its slow off-rate, with durable pathway inhibition persisting for approximately four hours in cellular assays, contrasting with rapid recovery observed with PP242 and shorter-duration suppression by AZD8055 (Liu et al., 2013, pp. 7–9). Torin2 is highly selective for mTOR over PI3K (>800-fold) and shows approximately 100-fold selectivity over the DNA damage response kinases ATM and ATR; these latter off-targets remain relevant at higher concentrations and inform safety margins and dose selection (Liu et al., 2013, pp. 9–10).

Mechanistically, Torin2 robustly induces autophagy across multiple cell lines (e.g., LC3-II accumulation, acridine-orange–positive acidic vesicle formation), consistent with its potent inhibition of mTORC1, a master negative regulator of autophagy initiation and flux (Liu et al., 2013, pp. 7–9). As a drug class, ATP-competitive TORKi have been widely investigated in oncology and proliferative diseases given their ability to fully suppress both mTOR complexes, thereby impacting protein synthesis, cell-cycle progression, metabolism, and survival signaling (Liu et al., 2013, pp. 7–10). Outside oncology, dual mTORC1/2 inhibition has demonstrated disease-modifying potential in organ systems where mTOR hyperactivation drives pathology; for example, Torin2 reduced S6 and 4E-BP1 phosphorylation and curtailed cyst burden in a mouse model of autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease, with efficacy comparable to sirolimus and a potentially more favorable side-effect profile (Holditch et al., 2019, pp. 6–7).

In the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), dysregulated mTOR signaling (particularly chronic mTORC1 activation with age) is strongly implicated in degradative/clearance failure, lysosomal dysfunction, metabolic reprogramming, and structural degeneration that recapitulate key features of dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD), including lipid-rich subretinal deposits and RPE dedifferentiation (Go et al., 2020, pp. 1–3, 11–13; Huang et al., 2019, pp. 1–2, 7–9; Zhao et al., 2011, pp. 10–11). RPE expresses and functionally engages both mTORC1 and mTORC2 complexes, providing a mechanistic rationale for evaluating dual-complex catalytic inhibitors like Torin2 as therapeutic candidates to correct core pathogenic processes in dry AMD (Intartaglia et al., 2022, pp. 15–17).

Therapeutic History  
The mTOR pathway has been studied extensively in retinal and RPE biology, with strong preclinical evidence linking mTOR hyperactivation to RPE degeneration and dry AMD-like pathology, and corresponding benefits from mTOR inhibition; however, direct ocular data with Torin2 itself are currently lacking and must be extrapolated from pharmacology in other tissues and from surrogates (Torin1, PP242, INK-128/MLN0128) and rapamycin (Liu et al., 2013, pp. 7–10; Holditch et al., 2019, pp. 6–7). Genetic and pharmacologic mTOR activation in RPE induces epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), suppresses autophagy, disrupts metabolism, and yields an AMD-like phenotype; in a knock-in model that overexpresses mLST8 (a core mTORC component) in RPE, both mTORC1 and mTORC2 are upregulated, and dual-complex inhibition with TORIN1 (a TORKi closely related to Torin2) normalized signaling and restored autophagy and RPE function in vitro, including rescue of autophagy regulators Atg9b and Atg7 and recovery of autophagy flux (Chowdhury et al., 2025, pp. 1–2, 8–13). These data directly support the therapeutic relevance of dual mTORC1/2 inhibition in RPE conditions that model early AMD pathogenesis (Chowdhury et al., 2025, pp. 1–2, 8–13).

In a complementary RPE disease model focused on amyloid-β (Aβ)-induced lysosomal/autophagy impairment—a pathology linked to dry AMD—Torin (a first-generation mTOR inhibitor in this study; not explicitly Torin2) restored lysosomal activity via LAMP2 activation, corrected p62/SQSTM1 accumulation, facilitated Aβ aggregate clearance, and ameliorated Aβ-driven tight junction disruption in vitro and in Aβ-challenged mouse RPE in vivo, directly addressing the barrier degradation and clearance deficits central to AMD (Jo et al., 2023, pp. 1–2). In multiple RPE cell systems modeling autofluorescent granule (AFG) accumulation and lipofuscinogenesis after photoreceptor outer segment (POS) challenge, mTOR inhibition (rapamycin; INK-128) decreased AFG load by approximately 30% and activated a TFEB-dependent lysosome biogenesis program; these effects required intact lysosomal proteases/lipases and were associated with LAMP1 upregulation and GADD34 induction, supporting the concept that mTOR→TFEB pathway modulation can restore lysosomal clearance in RPE (albeit these studies were preprints and should be interpreted accordingly) (Falcão et al., 2024, pp. 1–6, 10–18). Constitutively active TFEB dramatically reduced POS-dependent AFGs in preventive and resolving paradigms, further validating TFEB activation as a mechanistic goal for pharmacologic mTOR inhibition in early AMD models (Falcão et al., 2024, pp. 1–6, 13–18).

In genetic models where RPE mTORC1 is hyperactivated through Tsc1 deletion, chronic mTORC1 activity causes progressive RPE degeneration, lipid accumulation, and subretinal drusenoid deposits, and rapamycin treatment restored RPE markers (e.g., RPE65), improved ERG responses, and partially normalized metabolomic derangements, providing in vivo proof-of-concept that mTOR pathway suppression improves RPE structure and function in AMD-like degeneration (Huang et al., 2019, pp. 1–2, 7–9; Zhao et al., 2011, pp. 10–11). At a mechanistic level, age-driven increases in lysosome mass and mTORC1 docking in RPE create a vicious cycle of mTORC1 hyperactivation that impairs phagolysosomal function, promotes lipogenesis, suppresses lipolysis, and degrades autophagy, converging on classic dry AMD phenotypes; mTOR inhibition breaks this cycle and restores degradative capacity (Go et al., 2020, pp. 1–3, 11–13).

Beyond ocular systems, Torin2 has been used preclinically in oncology and in non-ocular degenerative models; its dual mTORC1/2 suppression, sustained pharmacodynamics, and autophagy induction underlie anti-proliferative activity and apoptosis in cancer models, while in an ADPKD model Torin2 demonstrated pathway inhibition with efficacy comparable to sirolimus, suggesting that torin-class agents may be tolerable with appropriate dose and schedule selection (Liu et al., 2013, pp. 7–9; Holditch et al., 2019, pp. 6–7). Notably, second-generation mTOR inhibitors can be cytotoxic in RPE due to potent mTORC2 suppression with downstream Akt inhibition, a survival pathway for metabolically stressed RPE; this risk emphasizes the importance of dose titration and local ocular delivery strategies for Torin2 in AMD (Huang et al., 2018, pp. 7–8).

To our knowledge, Torin2 has not been reported in clinical trials for dry AMD or directly tested in RPE/retinal models in vivo; however, the strength of the translational prior—mTOR hyperactivation as a convergent mechanism in RPE degeneration; rescue with catalytic mTOR inhibitors such as TORIN1; and reversible ALP defects with Torin-class inhibition—warrants rigorous preclinical testing of Torin2 in dry AMD-relevant models (Chowdhury et al., 2025, pp. 1–2, 8–11; Jo et al., 2023, pp. 1–2).
