Proposal for Nicotinamide riboside (NR; ChromaDex Niagen)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Nicotinamide riboside (NR), marketed as ChromaDex Niagen, belongs to the vitamin B3 family and is a naturally occurring precursor of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+). Originally discovered in trace amounts in milk and other food sources, NR has been isolated and subsequently synthesized to yield a high‐purity form for nutritional and pharmaceutical applications. As a member of a class of compounds designed to elevate intracellular NAD+ levels, NR has attracted significant attention in the fields of metabolism, aging, and neuroprotection. Its chemical structure—a combination of a nicotinamide moiety bound to ribose—enables it to enter salvage pathways in cells with minimal metabolic conversion losses. In this regard, NR is comparable to other NAD+ boosters such as nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN), though differences in bioavailability and cellular transporter expression have been noted in some studies. Preclinical research has documented retinal distribution of NR in rodent models, suggesting that it reaches ocular tissues in pharmacologically relevant concentrations, an essential prerequisite for any agent intended to treat dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) (Abokyi & Tse, 2025; Braidy et al., 2019).

Therapeutic History:  
NR has been extensively investigated for its potential to modulate cellular energy metabolism and mitigate the effects of aging in diverse tissues. Early studies revealed that supplementation with NR effectively elevates NAD+ levels in multiple cell types, including neurons, skeletal muscle, and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells, thereby enhancing mitochondrial function and cellular stress resistance (Dovijarski, 2019; Pîrvu et al., 2021). In the context of ocular research, while much of the direct experimental work has been performed using NMN, the close biochemical relationship between NMN and NR—as NAD+ precursors—has led investigators to propose that NR could produce similar outcomes in RPE cells. Models of retinal degeneration, including glaucoma and optic nerve injury, have demonstrated that NAD+ boosting via NR administration can ameliorate mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, indirectly supporting its potential repurposing for dry AMD (Kitaoka et al., 2020; Mehmel et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2024). Moreover, clinical studies investigating NR’s safety in humans have shown that it is well tolerated at doses that elevate systemic NAD+ levels; doses in the gram-range have been employed without significant adverse effects (Jadeja et al., 2020). Although direct clinical trials targeting dry AMD are still forthcoming, the overall therapeutic history of NR—encompassing robust preclinical ocular benefit across neurodegenerative models and a favorable safety profile in human subjects—strongly supports further investigation for eye diseases where NAD+ depletion is a central pathophysiological feature (Dovijarski, 2019; Braidy et al., 2019; Pîrvu et al., 2021).

Mechanism of Action:  
The proposed mechanism of NR in dry AMD centers on its capacity to elevate intracellular NAD+ levels, thereby fueling a cascade of beneficial biochemical events. After cell entry, NR is phosphorylated by nicotinamide riboside kinases (NRKs) to generate NMN, which is subsequently converted to NAD+ through the activity of NMN adenylyltransferase. The resultant increase in NAD+ acts as a vital cofactor for sirtuins—primarily SIRT1 and SIRT3—which are NAD+-dependent deacetylases involved in regulating numerous cellular processes. SIRT1, in particular, has been implicated in deacetylating key transcription factors such as peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator-1α (PGC-1α) and transcription factor EB (TFEB). TFEB is a master regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy; its deacetylation leads to its nuclear translocation and binding to CLEAR (Coordinated Lysosomal Expression and Regulation) elements in the promoter regions of autophagy and lysosomal genes. Such activation promotes lysosomal acidification and increases the activity of hydrolases like cathepsin D, which are critical for the degradation of damaged proteins and organelles. Concurrently, SIRT3, which localizes predominantly to mitochondria, enhances mitochondrial function by deacetylating enzymes crucial for mitochondrial bioenergetics and reactive oxygen species (ROS) detoxification. The overall effect of NR supplementation is therefore hypothesized to be a restoration of cellular homeostasis in RPE cells—improving both autophagic clearance and mitochondrial function, two processes known to be compromised in dry AMD (Kitaoka et al., 2020; Mehmel et al., 2020; Lin & Apte, 2018; Jadeja et al., 2020). Such a mechanism is consistent with a substantial body of literature demonstrating that the NAD+-sirtuin axis plays a critical role in regulating neuroprotective and anti-aging functions across various tissues (Dovijarski, 2019; Jadeja et al., 2018; Pîrvu et al., 2021).

At the molecular level, the cascade initiated by NR begins with its metabolic conversion to NAD+, which then serves as an essential substrate for SIRT1 activation. Activated SIRT1 deacetylates TFEB, facilitating its nuclear accumulation and binding to the CLEAR network promoters, thereby upregulating genes responsible for autophagy, lysosomal biogenesis, and lysosomal acidification. This process is expected to boost cathepsin D activity and overall lysosomal proteolytic function. Parallel activation of SIRT3 in mitochondria further strengthens cellular defenses by enhancing mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and reducing ROS generation through deacetylation of key metabolic enzymes. These reconstructed pathways directly address the deleterious effects of oxidative stress and metabolic dysfunction that drive RPE cell death in dry AMD (Kitaoka et al., 2020; Mehmel et al., 2020; Jadeja et al., 2018). While several studies using NMN have validated these pathways in RPE and retinal cells (Ren et al., 2022), the mechanistic rationale for NR’s effectiveness remains compelling given its similar mode of action and previously documented retinal distribution (Kitaoka et al., 2020; Jadeja et al., 2020).

Expected Effect:  
In the experimental setting, the administration of NR is expected to lead to a marked elevation of intracellular NAD+ levels within RPE cells, setting off a chain reaction of intracellular adjustments. The immediate consequence of increased NAD+ availability will be the enhanced activation of SIRT1 and SIRT3. Once activated, SIRT1 is anticipated to deacetylate TFEB, resulting in its translocation to the nucleus. In the nucleus, TFEB binds to target gene promoters, thereby upregulating a suite of genes involved in lysosomal biogenesis and the autophagic process. Critically, improvements in lysosomal acidification are expected to boost cathepsin D activity—a protease essential for the degradation of intracellular waste and damaged organelles. Simultaneously, SIRT3-mediated deacetylation events within the mitochondria are projected to improve mitochondrial respiratory capacity and enhance ATP production, while also reducing the production of ROS that ordinarily contribute to oxidative damage. The net effect of these cellular modifications should be an increase in RPE cell viability, particularly under conditions of induced oxidative stress (for example, H₂O₂ exposure), which mimics the pathological environment observed in dry AMD (Zhu et al., 2016; Tong et al., 2022).

Additionally, the NR-induced NAD+ boosting is hypothesized to initiate a beneficial feedback loop wherein improved mitochondrial function further supports cellular energy demands and sustains autophagic flux. This dual action—simultaneously targeting lysosomal and mitochondrial dysfunction—addresses two major hallmarks of dry AMD pathophysiology. RPE cells, which are crucial for the maintenance of photoreceptors, suffer from cumulative oxidative damage and impaired clearance of cellular debris as a result of aging and environmental stress. By restoring efficient autophagic processes and ensuring proper mitochondrial function, NR treatment is expected to reduce the accumulation of toxic protein aggregates and dysfunctional organelles, thereby staving off cellular senescence and death. This is especially salient given that recent studies have linked improved NAD+ metabolism to increased cellular resilience in models of age-related neurodegeneration (Jadeja et al., 2018; Jadeja et al., 2020). The expected outcomes in these assays include measurable increases in NAD+/NADH ratios, enhanced expression and nuclear localization of TFEB, elevated cathepsin D activity, and ultimately, improved cell survival following oxidative challenge. Such effects have been reported in various in vitro and in vivo studies employing NAD+ precursors, strengthening the biological plausibility of the NR hypothesis for treating dry AMD (Kitaoka et al., 2020; Mehmel et al., 2020; Dovijarski, 2019).

Overall Evaluation:  
An overall evaluation of NR as a candidate for treating dry AMD reveals both promising strengths and areas requiring further elucidation. Among the principal strengths is NR’s robust ability to elevate systemic and intracellular NAD+ levels, a key factor in numerous regulatory processes implicated in aging and cellular stress response. Its well-characterized pharmacokinetics, combined with documented safety in human supplementation studies, position NR as an attractive candidate for rapid clinical translation (Jadeja et al., 2020; Braidy et al., 2019). The underlying mechanism—that is, the activation of SIRT1 and SIRT3 leading to deacetylation and activation of TFEB—is supported by a breadth of biochemical and preclinical studies, albeit many of these have been performed with related compounds such as NMN. Nevertheless, the evidence suggests that the NAD+–sirtuin–TFEB axis is critical for autophagic and mitochondrial homeostasis in RPE cells—a process compromised in dry AMD (Kitaoka et al., 2020; Mehmel et al., 2020; Jadeja et al., 2018).

Further strengths include NR’s potential to confer dual-organellar benefit. Restoration of lysosomal function, as evidenced by improved acidification and protease activity (e.g., cathepsin D), is a novel therapeutic target that addresses the accumulation of cellular debris—a key factor in the progression of dry AMD. Concurrently, the enhancement of mitochondrial quality and bioenergetics through SIRT3 activation can mitigate oxidative stress and improve cell survival in metabolic stress conditions. Together, these actions provide a mechanistically coherent rationale for repurposing NR in a disease context that currently lacks effective treatments (Kitaoka et al., 2020; Mehmel et al., 2020).

However, several challenges and weaknesses must be acknowledged. First, while preclinical data offer promising insights, direct evidence of NR’s efficacy in RPE cells specifically challenged by dry AMD-like oxidative stress is still limited. Much of the supportive evidence comes from studies employing NMN or from models of other neurodegenerative conditions such as glaucoma and optic nerve degeneration (Lin et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2024). Given differences in uptake and metabolic processing between NR and NMN (Will-Orrego, 2020), it will be important to confirm that NR is adequately taken up by RPE and that its metabolic conversion is efficient in these cells.  

Furthermore, the full cascade—from NAD+ elevation through SIRT1/SIRT3 activation, subsequent TFEB deacetylation, and eventual lysosomal and mitochondrial improvements—remains to be validated within the specific context of dry AMD. Although the general biology of the NAD+–sirtuin axis is well established, its quantitative impact on TFEB activity and cathepsin D function in RPE under conditions of oxidative stress has not been directly demonstrated. This gap in the literature raises questions regarding the optimal dosing regimen, the timing of supplementation, and the potential for compensatory metabolic changes that might blunt the predicted benefits (Ren et al., 2022).

Another consideration is the heterogeneity inherent in dry AMD pathology. RPE cell dysfunction in AMD is multifactorial, involving not only oxidative stress and defective autophagy, but also inflammation, extracellular matrix alterations, and genetic predispositions. Whether NR’s NAD+ boosting effect alone can sufficiently address the multifaceted nature of dry AMD remains uncertain. There is also the possibility that chronic administration might lead to unforeseen metabolic imbalances or off-target effects, particularly if prolonged high-dose supplementation alters systemic NAD+ homeostasis or affects non-target tissues in unintended ways (Ghosh et al., 2023; Jadeja et al., 2018).

Despite these challenges, the candidate’s favorable safety profile and the mechanistic soundness of the NAD+–sirtuin–TFEB hypothesis underpin a strong case for further investigation. In vitro experiments using ARPE-19 cells and primary RPE cultures under oxidative stress conditions should be prioritized to determine whether NR supplementation can reproducibly induce TFEB nuclear translocation, enhance lysosomal acidification, and improve mitochondrial function. In addition, in vivo studies in animal models that recapitulate dry AMD pathology will be essential to assess the translational potential of NR. These studies might include detailed pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic analyses to ascertain the retinal bioavailability of NR, as well as dose-response studies to define the therapeutic window. Finally, controlled clinical trials in patients with early dry AMD would be necessary to confirm efficacy and to optimize treatment protocols (Martucci et al., 2025; Oshitari, 2024; Lin & Apte, 2018).

In summary, the current body of evidence suggests that NR is a promising repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD. Its ability to efficiently elevate intracellular NAD+ levels, combined with the downstream activation of SIRT1 and SIRT3, provides a strong mechanistic rationale for improving both autophagic flux and mitochondrial function in RPE cells. These cellular processes are crucial for maintaining retinal health and combating the oxidative and metabolic stress that drives dry AMD progression. Moreover, the extensive preclinical investigation of NAD+ precursors and the favorable safety record of NR in human studies further support its potential for fast-tracking into clinical evaluation for retinal disorders (Kitaoka et al., 2020; Mehmel et al., 2020; Dovijarski, 2019; Jadeja et al., 2020; Pîrvu et al., 2021).

Nonetheless, key uncertainties remain regarding the direct applicability of NR’s effects to the complex microenvironment of the retina in dry AMD. Issues such as differential cellular uptake, the efficiency of NR’s metabolic conversion to NAD+ in RPE cells, and the precise threshold of NAD+ elevation required to trigger beneficial deacetylation of TFEB must be addressed. Furthermore, given the multifactorial nature of AMD pathology, combinatorial therapeutic approaches may ultimately be necessary to achieve clinically significant improvements. Therefore, while NR emerges as a highly promising candidate with a compelling mechanistic framework, rigorous experimental validation tailored specifically to the ocular setting is imperative. Future studies should elucidate whether the activation of the NAD+–sirtuin–TFEB axis translates into meaningful improvements in lysosomal acidification, cathepsin D activity, and RPE cell survival in both in vitro models of oxidative stress and in vivo models of dry AMD (Kitaoka et al., 2020; Jadeja et al., 2018; Will-Orrego, 2020).

Looking forward, systematic exploration through well-designed preclinical trials is recommended to confirm the predicted molecular events. These experiments should include detailed time-course and dose-response analyses of NAD+ levels, sirtuin activities, TFEB deacetylation status, and lysosomal function in retinal cells exposed to oxidative insults. Such data will not only clarify the effectiveness of NR in restoring cellular homeostasis but also help refine dosing strategies and identify potential biomarkers of response. Ultimately, if subsequent research affirms the predicted benefits of NR in improving RPE function and survival, this therapeutic candidate could represent a major advancement in the pharmacological management of dry AMD—a condition for which current treatment options remain notably limited (Kitaoka et al., 2020; Mehmel et al., 2020; Dovijarski, 2019; Jadeja et al., 2020; Pîrvu et al., 2021).

Overall, the promising preclinical evidence supporting the NAD+–sirtuin–TFEB pathway, combined with the safety profile demonstrated in human trials, renders NR a highly attractive candidate for drug development in dry AMD. While further direct experimental evidence is required to confirm its efficacy in retinal cells, the mechanistic insights gleaned from various studies provide a sound scientific basis for proceeding with additional preclinical and clinical investigations. Thus, repurposing nicotinamide riboside for dry AMD holds significant potential and merits further exploration as part of an integrated strategy to address the unmet therapeutic need in this degenerative retinal disease (Abokyi & Tse, 2025; Cimaglia et al., 2020; Ebeling et al., 2020; Jadeja et al., 2020; Pîrvu et al., 2021; Lin & Apte, 2018).
