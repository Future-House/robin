Query: What are the current understandings of the molecular and cellular mechanisms driving RPE cell dysfunction in dry age-related macular degeneration, including oxidative stress pathways, mitochondrial impairment, lipid peroxidation, drusen component accumulation, and complement activation?

Current models of RPE dysfunction in dry AMD converge on a multi‐hit scenario in which chronic oxidative stress, impaired mitochondrial quality control, lipid peroxidation, sub‐RPE deposit (drusen) formation, and local complement overactivation form a vicious cycle that ultimately leads to RPE atrophy. Below is a summary of the key molecular and cellular players in each arm of this process.

1. Oxidative Stress Pathways  
  • High metabolic demand & constant light exposure in RPE → excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation (superoxide, H2O2).  
  • Age‐related decline in key antioxidant defenses:  
    – Nrf2/Keap1 axis becomes less active with age, reducing transcription of phase II detox enzymes (HO-1, NQO1, GCLM).  
    – Glutathione levels fall, catalase and superoxide dismutase (SOD1/2) activity is impaired.  
  • Consequences of unbuffered ROS: protein carbonylation, DNA strand breaks (including mitochondrial DNA), cross-linking of extracellular matrix—all of which sensitize RPE to further injury.  

2. Mitochondrial Impairment  
  • Structural alterations: mitochondrial swelling, loss of cristae integrity.  
  • Functional defects: reduced membrane potential (ΔΨm), impaired ATP synthesis, elevated mitochondrial ROS (mtROS).  
  • mtDNA damage: oxidative lesions and deletions accumulate, compromising respiratory chain subunits.  
  • Dysregulated dynamics & quality control:  
    – Imbalanced fission/fusion (Drp1 hyperactivation, reduced Mfn1/2) leads to fragmented organelles.  
    – Mitophagy deficits (PINK1/Parkin pathway downregulated) → accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria.  

3. Lipid Peroxidation & Bisretinoid (Lipofuscin) Accumulation  
  • Photoreceptor outer segment debris contains large amounts of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs); RPE phagocytosis of these leads to lipid‐rich waste within lysosomes.  
  • Iron‐ or light‐catalyzed peroxidation of PUFAs generates reactive aldehydes (4-HNE, MDA) that form adducts on proteins and DNA.  
  • Bisretinoids (A2E and related pyridinium bisretinoids) accumulate in RPE lysosomes as lipofuscin:  
    – Upon blue‐light exposure, A2E generates singlet oxygen and superoxide, further boosting oxidative stress.  
    – A2E impairs lysosomal acidification and autophagic flux.  

4. Drusen Component Accumulation  
  • Drusen are extracellular deposits between the RPE basal lamina and Bruch’s membrane composed of:  
    – Lipids: esterified and unesterified cholesterol, phosphatidylcholine, ceramides.  
    – Apolipoproteins: ApoE, clusterin.  
    – Complement factors: C3b, C5b-9 (sublytic membrane attack complex), vitronectin, CFH.  
    – Proteins associated with inflammation and ECM remodeling: fibronectin, TIMPs, amyloid‐β.  
  • Thickening and cross-linking of Bruch’s membrane reduces nutrient and oxygen flux, creating local hypoxia and metabolic stress.  

5. Complement Activation & Inflammation  
  • Genetic variants in alternative pathway regulators (CFH Y402H, C3 R102G, CFB, CFI) elevate baseline complement turnover.  
  • C3b deposition on RPE and drusen surfaces → amplification loop → generation of C5a (proinflammatory anaphylatoxin) and sublytic C5b-9 on RPE membranes.  
  • C5a/C3a engage RPE and resident microglia → NF-κB activation, secretion of IL-1β, IL-18, TNFα.  
  • NLRP3 inflammasome activation within RPE in response to drusen components and mitochondrial DAMPs (mtDNA, cardiolipin) → pyroptotic cell death.  

Integrated View  
Chronic ROS overload and mitochondrial failure sensitize RPE to lipid peroxidation and lysosomal dysfunction (lipofuscin build‐up). Lipid‐rich drusen both reflect and perpetuate local inflammation by trapping complement components. Complement overactivation and inflammasome signaling then drive a low‐grade but sustained inflammatory milieu that tips RPE from adaptation into atrophy. Once RPE cells die, photoreceptor support is lost, leading to the geographic atrophy that characterizes advanced dry AMD.
