Assay Overview  
The Synthetic Drusen Deposit Assay is designed to model one of the earliest and best-characterized hallmarks of dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD)—subretinal drusen accumulation and its impact on retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) function. In this Transwell-based system, human RPE cells (e.g. ARPE-19 or primary donor RPE) are cultured to confluence on permeable supports. On the basal side, researchers deposit liposome–complement–hydroxyapatite particles that mimic the lipid-protein mineral composition of native drusen. Over 48–72 hours, RPE monolayer barrier integrity is assessed by transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurements; inflammatory activation is quantified by sampling the basal and apical media for cytokines such as IL-6, IL-8, CCL2/MCP-1 and IL-1β using ELISA or multiplex bead arrays; and cell health is monitored by live/dead staining or mitochondrial assays (e.g. MTT, Seahorse flux). The assay can also include imaging of complement deposition (C3b, MAC) by immunofluorescence and quantification of RPE polarity markers (bestrophin, ZO-1) by confocal microscopy.

Biomedical Evidence  
1. Centrality of drusen to AMD pathogenesis: Clinical imaging (fundus autofluorescence, OCT) identifies drusen as predictive biomarkers of progression from early to advanced dry AMD (AREDS Report No. 17). Drusen are enriched in lipids, complement proteins (C3, C5b-9), and hydroxyapatite spherules (Crabb et al., Clin Chim Acta 2014; Curcio et al., PNAS 2005).  
2. Complement dysregulation: Genetic studies (CFH, C3, CFI polymorphisms) have firmly established complement overactivation as a driver of AMD (Fritsche et al., Nat Genet 2016). Deposition of complement on drusen correlates with local inflammation and RPE dysfunction in post-mortem eyes (Johnson et al., J Clin Invest 2007).  
3. RPE barrier breakdown and inflammation: RPE monolayer integrity (TEER) and cytokine release reflect key steps in disease progression—barrier compromise leads to photoreceptor stress and recruitment of microglia/macrophages (Datta et al., Prog Retin Eye Res 2017). The synthetic drusen assay recapitulates these mechanisms in vitro, linking particulate deposition to RPE inflammation and loss of barrier function.  

Previous Use  
• Umeda et al., Biomaterials 2017 first described the assay to screen small molecules and biologics for their ability to restore TEER and suppress IL-6, IL-8 release in drusen-challenged RPE. They identified complement inhibitors and LXR agonists as hits that reduced C3b deposition and normalized barrier integrity.  
• Follow-on studies have used similar drusen mimic systems to study NLRP3 inflammasome activation (Tarallo et al., Cell 2012) and to validate anti-complement peptides (Kita et al., Invest Ophthalmol Vis Sci 2019).  
• Early translational work leveraged the assay to prioritize candidates for subretinal gene therapy targeting complement factor I overexpression (Reich et al., Mol Ther 2020).  

Overall Evaluation  

Strengths  
• Disease relevance: Directly models drusen-induced RPE dysfunction, a proximate cause of photoreceptor loss in dry AMD.  
• Quantitative readouts: TEER and multiplex cytokine panels offer robust, high-throughput metrics for barrier function and inflammatory status.  
• Mechanistic flexibility: Can be adapted to probe complement activation, inflammasome involvement, lipid clearing, or antioxidant pathways.  
• Screening capacity: Amenable to 96-well Transwell formats for moderate throughput.  

Weaknesses  
• Simplified microenvironment: Lacks choroidal endothelial cells, immune cell infiltration, and a full Bruch’s membrane analog.  
• Drusen particle heterogeneity: Synthetic liposome/hydroxyapatite composition may not capture full complexity of in vivo drusen proteins (e.g. ApoE isoforms, vitronectin).  
• Cell source variability: ARPE-19 may not fully recapitulate primary RPE phenotype; donor RPE can introduce donor-to-donor variability.  
• Chronicity: Short-term exposure may not predict cumulative, age-related RPE changes or geographic atrophy development.  

Conclusion  
The Synthetic Drusen Deposit Assay provides a mechanistically grounded, disease-relevant platform to evaluate therapeutic candidates aimed at mitigating drusen-driven RPE compromise in dry AMD. Its strengths in modeling complement-mediated inflammation and barrier dysfunction make it a valuable early-stage screening tool, though follow-up in more complex co-culture or ex vivo systems will be required to confirm long-term efficacy and safety.
