Assay Overview  
• Biological model and disease aspect modeled  
  – Uses polarized human RPE monolayers (either primary RPE or iPSC-derived RPE) grown on porous Transwell® inserts to recreate the outer blood–retinal barrier.  
  – Applies pathologic stress (e.g. low‐dose H₂O₂, blue‐light–activated A2E or sublytic complement C5b-9) to mimic oxidative and complement‐mediated injury seen in early dry AMD.  
• Readouts and measurement  
  – Transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) is measured across the RPE monolayer in real time using an EVOM voltohmmeter or automated TEER reader.  
  – TEER values (Ω·cm²) quantify tight‐junction integrity: a drop in TEER indicates barrier breakdown, while maintenance or recovery of TEER signals protective drug effects.  
  – Optional multiplexed end points: immunostaining for ZO-1/occludin, paracellular flux of fluorescent dextrans, LDH release for cytotoxicity.  

Biomedical Evidence  
• Centrality of RPE barrier dysfunction in dry AMD  
  – Early AMD is characterized by sub-RPE drusen, complement deposition, low‐grade inflammation and eventual RPE atrophy (Eyeson et al., Prog. Retin. Eye Res. 2018).  
  – RPE tight‐junction breakdown precedes frank geographic atrophy; impaired barrier facilitates influx of complement proteins and inflammatory mediators (Bhutto & Lutty, Prog. Retin. Eye Res. 2012).  
  – Clinical OCT and autofluorescence studies correlate early junctional defects and RPE decompensation with drusen enlargement and progression to GA (Curcio et al., Ophthalmology 2013).  
• Relevance of oxidative and complement stress  
  – Oxidative load from chronic light exposure and lipofuscin (A2E) accumulation drives tight‐junction disassembly in vitro and in vivo (Kinnunen et al., Exp. Eye Res. 2012).  
  – Genetic and histopathologic data implicate complement overactivation (CFH, C3) in barrier disruption and RPE loss (Fritsche et al., Nat. Genet. 2016).  

Previous Use  
• Levy et al. (J. Cell Sci. 2015) demonstrated that sublethal complement attack lowers TEER in iPSC-RPE and that complement‐inhibiting peptides restore barrier function.  
• Dunn et al. (Exp. Eye Res. 1996) established TEER baselines (200–500 Ω·cm²) in primary human RPE and showed sensitivity to H₂O₂.  
• Peng et al. (Invest. Ophthalmol. Vis. Sci. 2017) used TEER screening to identify antioxidant small molecules that prevent blue-light–induced TEER loss in ARPE-19 monolayers.  
• Fernandez‐Godino et al. (Sci. Rep. 2015) correlated TEER with phagocytic capacity and polarized secretion (VEGF/PDGF), offering a multiparametric readout.  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
  • Physiologically relevant: models outer barrier function critical in AMD pathogenesis.  
  • Label‐free, noninvasive, real-time kinetic readout—enables rapid triage of candidate compounds.  
  • Scalable to medium throughput (24- to 96-well TEER systems).  
  • Human iPSC‐derived RPE can capture patient genetics (e.g. CFH risk alleles).  

Weaknesses  
  • Requires weeks‐long RPE maturation to reach stable high TEER; throughput limited by culture time.  
  • TEER variability across labs—needs rigorous standardization of seeding density, media, electrode placement.  
  • Simplified model: lacks Bruch’s membrane remodeling, choroidal endothelial interactions, microglial contributions.  
  • TEER readout alone may miss non–barrier‐related protective mechanisms (e.g. autophagy, mitochondrial rescue).  

Conclusion  
Barrier integrity assays measuring TEER in human RPE monolayers provide a direct, disease-relevant screen for compounds that preserve or restore the outer blood–retinal barrier under oxidative and complement challenge. While maturation time and physiologic complexity pose challenges, the assay’s label-free, real-time nature and strong linkage to early AMD pathogenesis make it a valuable tool in the preclinical evaluation of dry AMD therapeutics.
