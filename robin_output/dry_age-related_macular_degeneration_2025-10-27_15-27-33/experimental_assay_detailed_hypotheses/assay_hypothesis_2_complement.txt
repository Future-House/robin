Assay Overview  
• Goal: Model drusen‐associated complement attack on the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and measure resulting barrier breakdown.  
• Cells/materials: Human iPSC‐derived RPE differentiated into a polarized monolayer on Transwell inserts. This system forms high‐resistance tight junctions (ZO-1, occludin) and establishes apical/basal polarity.  
• Challenge: Apical application of human serum (source of complement proteins) or purified C5b-9 complex to mimic subretinal complement activation around drusen.  
• Readouts:  
  – Transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) measured continuously (hours to days) to quantify real-time barrier integrity.  
  – Automated immunofluorescence for ZO-1 (tight junction marker) and C5b-9 deposition to quantify junctional integrity and membrane attack complex (MAC) deposition at end points (e.g., 24–48 h).  

Biomedical Evidence  
1. Complement dysregulation is a major driver of dry AMD pathogenesis.  
   – Genetic association studies (CFH, C3, CFB, CFI) point to overactive alternative complement pathway in AMD.1,2  
   – Postmortem AMD eyes accumulate complement components (C3b, C5b-9) in drusen and at the RPE–Bruch’s membrane interface.3,4  
2. MAC‐mediated RPE injury leads to barrier breakdown and cell dysfunction.  
   – Sublytic MAC on RPE induces cytokine release, mitochondrial stress, and junctional remodeling.5,6  
   – In AMD donor tissue, disrupted ZO-1 staining and increased RPE permeability precede geographic atrophy.7  
3. Barrier dysfunction at the RPE–Bruch’s interface contributes to photoreceptor degeneration.  
   – Loss of RPE tight junctions allows influx of proinflammatory mediators and cellular debris, accelerating atrophy.8,9  
4. Clinical efforts targeting complement (anti-C5, anti-C3, factor D inhibitors) demonstrate proof of mechanism (reduced MAC deposition) though efficacy in slowing GA progression remains under active investigation.10–12  

Collectively, the literature places complement‐mediated RPE barrier disruption at a nexus of genetic, histopathological, and clinical evidence in dry AMD.  

Previous Use  
• Anderson et al., J Pathol (2010): Established that drusen extracts and serum induce MAC deposition on primary human RPE in Transwell culture, with associated ZO-1 loss.13  
• Subsequent screens (2012–2018) employed TEER in RPE Transwells to evaluate small-molecule or biologics complement inhibitors, reporting correlation between TEER protection and reduced C5b-9 staining.14,15  
• Key discoveries:  
  – Sublytic MAC triggers RPE secretion of VEGF and IL-6, providing insight into early AMD inflammation.5  
  – Differential efficacy of factor H–derived peptides vs full-length FH in preserving RPE barrier highlighted structure–function domains critical for complement regulation at cell surfaces.16  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
1. Physiological relevance: Human iPSC‐RPE recapitulates donor genetics and forms bona fide tight junctions, enabling disease-relevant readouts.  
2. Direct measurement of a central pathogenic mechanism: TEER drop and ZO-1 disruption are proximal markers of complement‐mediated RPE injury.  
3. Quantitative, real-time, medium‐throughput: TEER can be automated; immunofluorescence can be multiplexed.  
4. Flexibility: Serum from AMD patients vs controls, purified complement proteins, or recombinant regulators can be compared head-to-head.  

Weaknesses  
1. Reductionist: Lacks Bruch’s membrane, choroidal endothelial cells, microglia/macrophages, and chronic drusen context (lipids, hydroxyapatite).  
2. Acute vs chronic: High‐dose serum or C5b-9 over 48 h may not capture low‐grade, long‐term complement activation seen in early AMD.  
3. Complement source variability: Human serum batches vary in regulatory protein levels; purified C5b-9 may not reflect in vivo MAC heterogeneity.  
4. Barrier readout specificity: TEER drop can arise from generalized toxicity or off‐target junctional modulation, necessitating orthogonal assays (e.g., permeability to fluorescent tracer, live/dead staining).  

Conclusion  
The Complement-Mediated Barrier Integrity Assay provides a focused, disease-relevant platform for testing inhibitors of complement‐driven RPE injury in dry AMD. Its strength lies in modeling a genetically and histologically validated mechanism—MAC‐induced barrier breakdown—within human RPE. However, it should be used in concert with more complex co-culture or extracellular matrix–based models and chronic exposure paradigms to fully recapitulate drusen biology and the inflammatory milieu of the subretinal space.
