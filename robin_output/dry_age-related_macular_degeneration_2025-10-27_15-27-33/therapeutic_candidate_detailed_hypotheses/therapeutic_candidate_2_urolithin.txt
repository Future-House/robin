Proposal for Urolithin A (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. U1892)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Urolithin A is a naturally occurring gut-microbiome–derived metabolite of dietary ellagitannins (found in pomegranates, berries, and nuts). First characterized in human plasma by Mitchell et al. (2009), it belongs to the class of small‐molecule polyphenol metabolites. While originally isolated from microbial fermentation of ellagic acid in vitro, current commercial Urolithin A is produced by scalable chemical synthesis or by controlled fermentation with engineered microbial strains, yielding >98% pure 3,8-dihydroxy-6H-dibenzopyran-6-one. Urolithin A is the first in its class advanced as an oral mitophagy activator for age‐related dysfunction.

Therapeutic History  
• Preclinical studies (Ryu et al., 2016) showed that Urolithin A prolongs lifespan in C. elegans and improves muscle function in aged rodents via mitophagy induction.  
• A randomized, placebo-controlled Phase I trial in healthy elderly volunteers (Singh et al., 2019) demonstrated safety up to 1,000 mg/day, with a plasma Cmax ~4 µM at 500 mg dosing and no serious adverse events.  
• Urolithin A (commercially Mitopure®) has been tested in early Phase II for muscle endurance and metabolic health; no direct trials in ophthalmic diseases or dry AMD to date.  
• No published clinical or veterinary use in retinal degenerative conditions; prior applications have focused on skeletal muscle, systemic inflammation, and liver health.

Mechanism of Action  
1. Mitophagy Induction via PINK1–Parkin Pathway  
   – Urolithin A upregulates PINK1 stabilization on depolarized mitochondria and recruits Parkin, leading to ubiquitination of outer‐mitochondrial–membrane proteins (Ryu et al., 2016).  
   – Promotes LC3-II lipidation and autophagosome formation around damaged mitochondria; decreases p62/SQSTM1 levels, indicating enhanced autophagic flux (Ghosh et al., 2020).  

2. TFEB-Mediated Lysosomal Biogenesis  
   – Induces nuclear translocation of TFEB, the master regulator of lysosomal and autophagy genes (Settembre et al., 2011).  
   – Increases expression of CLEAR network genes, enhancing lysosomal acidification and enzyme content to process autophagic cargo.  

3. Downstream Effects on Redox and Inflammation  
   – By clearing dysfunctional mitochondria, Urolithin A lowers mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) production (Ghosh et al., 2018).  
   – Reduced mtROS attenuates activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome (Zhou et al., 2011), potentially limiting IL-1β/IL-18 release in RPE.  
   – May indirectly reduce complement activation and sub-RPE deposit formation by lowering oxidative post-translational modifications on local proteins.

Mechanistic expression in RPE: Human RPE cells (ARPE-19, hESC-derived) robustly express PINK1, Parkin, LC3, TFEB, and key inflammasome components (NLRP3, ASC), as demonstrated by RNA-seq (Strunnikova et al., 2010) and proteomics studies (Koeberle et al., 2011).

Expected Effect in Dry AMD Assay  
In sodium-iodate–injured ARPE-19 or primary human RPE cultures:  
• Enhanced mitophagic clearance of depolarized mitochondria (read out by Mitotracker loss, COX IV immunostaining).  
• Reduction of mtROS, measurable by MitoSOX imaging (~30–50% decrease vs. vehicle).  
• Increased LC3-II/I ratio and decreased p62 levels on Western blot.  
• Lower activation of caspase-1 and secretion of IL-1β (ELISA), indicating attenuated inflammasome signaling.  
• Improved RPE cell viability (MTT/XTT assays or real-time impedance) and barrier integrity (TEER measurements).  
• Over time, decreased autofluorescent lipofuscin accumulation (confocal autofluorescence) and reduced complement C3b deposition on cell surface.

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
• Mechanistic Rationale: Directly targets mitochondrial dysfunction—a core feature of RPE degeneration in dry AMD.  
• In Vitro and In Vivo Proof-of-Concept: Published ARPE-19 and Abca4−/− mouse data (Perchetti et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022) support mitophagy induction, lipofuscin reduction, and photoreceptor preservation.  
• Safety Profile: Human Phase I data demonstrate tolerability at doses that achieve micromolar plasma concentrations.  
• Oral Bioavailability: Amenable to chronic dosing without intravitreal delivery.  

Weaknesses and Risks  
• Ocular Pharmacokinetics: While preclinical distribution suggests retinal penetration, definitive quantification of RPE/upon repeated dosing in larger animals is lacking.  
• Model Limitations: ARPE-19 is a transformed line with incomplete polarization; primary or iPSC-derived RPE may respond differently.  
• Off-Target Effects: Long-term systemic mitophagy induction may have unforeseen effects in tissues with baseline high mitophagy (e.g., cardiac).  
• Translational Gap: Efficacy in muscle or neurodegeneration models does not guarantee analogous benefit in human AMD, which is multifactorial (complement dysregulation, lipid metabolism, choroidal blood flow).

Conclusion  
Urolithin A represents a novel, mechanism-based approach to restore mitochondrial homeostasis in RPE and mitigate key pathological processes in dry AMD. The existing preclinical and safety data are encouraging, but critical gaps remain in ocular PK, chronic toxicity, and validation in advanced AMD models. Advancing Urolithin A will require optimized formulations for retinal delivery, rigorous testing in polarized primary RPE and larger animal AMD models, and ultimately a carefully designed early-phase clinical trial assessing retinal function and structural biomarkers.
