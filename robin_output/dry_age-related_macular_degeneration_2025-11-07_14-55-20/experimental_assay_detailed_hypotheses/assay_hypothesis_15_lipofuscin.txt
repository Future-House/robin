Assay Overview:  
This assay quantifies the intrinsic autofluorescence originating from lipofuscin and its major bisretinoid, A2E, that accumulates in cultured retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells over prolonged culture. It recapitulates a key aspect of dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) pathogenesis—a gradual failure in lysosomal degradation of photoreceptor outer segments producing lipofuscin buildup. Measurements include fluorescence intensity (often at peaks near 530–585 nm), granule load, and spectral emission properties, which are acquired using confocal microscopy, FACS, or plate‐reader‐based assays. The cell models typically employed include primary human RPE cells and ARPE-19 lines subjected to repeated feeding with photoreceptor outer segments or direct A2E supplementation (boulton2014studyingmelaninand pages 3-4, lei2017inhibitionorstimulation pages 14-16).

Biomedical Evidence:  
Lipofuscin accumulation is a hallmark of aged, post-mitotic RPE cells and is implicated in the pathogenesis of dry AMD via its phototoxic properties, lysosomal dysfunction, and subsequent oxidative damage. Both biomedical and clinical studies support that increasing lipofuscin burden correlates with impaired RPE function, abnormal autophagy, and heightened susceptibility to blue-light induced damage (boulton2014studyingmelaninand pages 4-5, davies2001photocytotoxicityoflipofuscin pages 6-8).

Previous Use:  
This assay format has been previously utilized to monitor changes in lipofuscin levels following pharmacologic modulation of autophagy and lysosomal pathways. Studies employing this assay demonstrated that autophagy inducers (e.g., rapamycin) reduce lipofuscin-like autofluorescence, whereas autophagy inhibition increases it, establishing its utility in early drug discovery (lei2017inhibitionorstimulation pages 1-3, lei2017inhibitionorstimulation pages 8-10).

Overall Evaluation:  
The strengths of this assay lie in its direct measurement of a disease-relevant endpoint and in its adaptability for high-throughput screening applications. However, limitations include the challenge of replicating the complex in vivo microenvironment and potential differences between in vitro-generated and native lipofuscin granules (zhang2019highlydifferentiatedhuman pages 1-2, bergmann2004inhibitionofthe pages 1-3).

Lipofuscin autofluorescence assays present a robust tool for evaluating therapeutics targeting dry AMD. They offer mechanistic insights into lysosomal and autophagic dysfunction while being amenable to drug screening platforms.
