Proposal for Vorinostat (pan-HDAC inhibitor)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Vorinostat (suberoylanilide hydroxamic acid, SAHA) is a fully synthetic, small‐molecule inhibitor of Zn²⁺‐dependent histone deacetylases (HDACs). It was discovered in the late 1990s by Merck–Serono via a structure‐based approach to target the catalytic pocket of class I/II HDACs, and approved by the FDA in 2006 as the first HDAC inhibitor for cutaneous T‐cell lymphoma (CTCL). Chemically, vorinostat is an N-hydroxy-N′-phenyloctanediamide with a hydroxamic acid moiety that chelates the catalytic Zn²⁺ ion in HDAC active sites. HDAC inhibitors as a class have been widely studied in oncology for their ability to reverse aberrant histone deacetylation, restore acetylation marks on histones and nonhistone proteins, and thereby reactivate silenced genes involved in cell cycle arrest, differentiation, and apoptosis.

Therapeutic History  
1. Oncology: Vorinostat’s primary approved use is for relapsed/refractory CTCL. It has also been trialed in multiple myeloma, acute myeloid leukemia, and solid tumors (breast, lung) often in combination regimens (Prince et al., 2008; Piekarz et al., 2009).  
2. Neurology and Neurodegeneration: Preclinical studies have shown that pan‐HDAC inhibitors can protect neurons against oxidative stress and promote axonal growth (Camelo et al., 2005; Bowers et al., 2010).  
3. Ophthalmology (limited): A handful of in vitro and in vivo studies have examined HDAC inhibition in retinal disease models. For example, TSA and SAHA were shown to preserve retinal ganglion cells after optic nerve injury (Bolden et al., 2006) and to reduce vascular leakage in diabetic retinopathy models (Chen et al., 2019). To date, no clinical trial of vorinostat or any pan‐HDAC inhibitor has been conducted specifically for dry AMD.

Mechanism of Action  
Vorinostat noncompetitively binds the catalytic domain of class I (HDAC1, 2, 3) and class II (HDAC6, 8) enzymes via its hydroxamate zinc‐chelating group and a cap group that interacts with the rim of the active site (Finnin et al., 1999). Inhibition of HDAC activity leads to hyperacetylation of histones H3 and H4, relaxing chromatin structure and enhancing transcription of genes under epigenetic repression. Key downstream molecular effects include:  
• Upregulation of autophagy genes (e.g., LC3/ATG8, BECN1) via increased acetylation at their promoters and enhanced binding of transcription factors such as FOXO1/3 and TFEB (Li et al., 2013; Yang et al., 2017).  
• Modulation of nonhistone proteins: p53 acetylation increases its stability and activity (Sykes et al., 2006); HSP90 inhibition via hyperacetylation leads to degradation of client proteins (Kovacs et al., 2005).  
• Anti‐oxidant gene induction: Enhanced Nrf2 acetylation promotes nuclear retention and transcription of phase‐II detoxifying genes (Villeneuve et al., 2008).  

Many of these targets (LC3, BECN1, p62/SQSTM1, TFEB, Nrf2) are expressed in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells (Kaarniranta et al., 2013; Krohne et al., 2010). RPE cells also express class I and II HDACs at moderate to high levels (acc. to RNA-seq data from the Human Protein Atlas and qPCR in ARPE-19 cells, Jan 2015).

Expected Effect in RPE Autophagy Assay  
In a cell culture assay using human RPE (ARPE-19 or iPSC-derived RPE), vorinostat is expected to:  
1. Increase histone H3K9/14ac and H4K5/8/12ac at autophagy gene promoters (Wang et al., 2014), leading to upregulated mRNA and protein levels of LC3-II, Beclin-1, ATG5 and ATG7.  
2. Enhance formation of autophagosomes (LC3 puncta) and autolysosomes (co-localization with LAMP1), rescuing defective phagolysosomal clearance of photoreceptor outer segment (POS) debris in aged or lipofuscin-loaded RPE (Mitter et al., 2014).  
3. Reduce accumulation of A2E and other bisretinoid‐derived lipofuscin fluorophores in RPE cultures fed POS (supported by a pilot study showing TSA reduced lipofuscin‐like autofluorescence in RPE cells; Nguyen et al., 2015).  
4. Upregulate Nrf2 targets (HO-1, NQO1) and reduce ROS levels under oxidative challenge (H₂O₂), protecting RPE viability (Yu et al., 2016).  

Because HDAC1/2/3 transcripts are well‐documented in RPE (Krohne et al., 2010), the molecular machinery for vorinostat’s action is present. Standard end points—Western blot for acetyl‐histones, qPCR for ATG transcripts, immunofluorescence for LC3, LysoTracker assays, POS‐phagocytosis quantified by fluorescence—will capture on‐target activity.

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
• Mechanistic alignment: Broad epigenetic activation of autophagy and antioxidant pathways directly addresses two core pathologies in dry AMD—impaired clearance of POS and oxidative damage.  
• Precedent in RPE models: TSA and other HDAC inhibitors have shown proof‐of‐concept rescue of RPE autophagic flux and viability under stress.  
• Safety data: Systemic administration of vorinostat in oncology patients provides a safety framework; maximum tolerated doses and plasma/ocular PK can be modeled.  

Weaknesses and Risks  
• Pan‐HDAC inhibition carries risk of off‐target gene activation or repression, potentially altering RPE differentiation state or promoting unwanted proliferation.  
• Dose optimization: Concentrations effective in cancer cell lines (0.5–2 μM) may be cytotoxic to nontransformed RPE. A narrow therapeutic window in the eye is possible.  
• Delivery challenges: Systemic dosing may not achieve sustained vitreous or subretinal drug levels without systemic toxicity. Local intraocular delivery would require new formulations and safety testing.  
• Long‐term epigenetic remodeling: Chronic HDAC inhibition in post-mitotic RPE may have unpredictable effects on global chromatin and gene networks.  

Conclusion  
Vorinostat is a scientifically rational candidate for repurposing in dry AMD in vitro assays. Its well‐characterized chemistry, known HDAC targets in RPE, and demonstrated capacity to induce autophagy and antioxidant gene programs make it a strong tool compound for assay development. However, translational hurdles—selectivity, dosing, and safety of chronic broad HDAC inhibition in the retina—must be addressed. Isoform‐selective HDAC inhibitors (e.g., HDAC6‐specific compounds) or transient, localized delivery approaches may offer a better therapeutic index for eventual clinical application.
