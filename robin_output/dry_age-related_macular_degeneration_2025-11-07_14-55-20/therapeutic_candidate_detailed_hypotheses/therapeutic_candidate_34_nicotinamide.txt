Proposal for Nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
• Identity and origin  
  – Nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) is a naturally occurring nucleotide intermediate in the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD⁺) salvage pathway.  
  – Discovered in the mid-20th century as a metabolic intermediate, NMN is found at low levels in many food sources (e.g., edamame, broccoli, cabbage, avocado) and is produced endogenously in cells by nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT) from nicotinamide (NAM).  
  – Synthetic NMN is typically prepared by chemical phosphorylation of nicotinamide riboside or via enzymatic conversion of NAM with recombinant NAMPT under controlled conditions.  

• Therapeutic class  
  – NMN belongs to the class of NAD⁺ precursors, which also includes nicotinamide, nicotinic acid, and nicotinamide riboside.  
  – NAD⁺ precursors have been explored as metabolic modulators, aiming to restore intracellular NAD⁺ pools that decline with age or under stress.  

Therapeutic History  
• Preclinical studies (rodents, cell culture)  
  – Multiple studies have shown that oral or intraperitoneal NMN administration in aged mice restores tissue NAD⁺ levels, enhances mitochondrial function, improves insulin sensitivity (Yoshino et al., 2011; Mills et al., 2016), and ameliorates age-related physiological decline.  
  – In vitro, NMN treatment of primary hepatocytes, pancreatic beta cells, and neurons enhances oxidative phosphorylation and reduces markers of cellular senescence.  

• Human safety and early-phase trials  
  – Phase I trials in healthy older adults (Irie et al., 2020; ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.) report that single oral doses of up to 500 mg NMN are well tolerated, transiently raise plasma NAD⁺ and NMN levels, and show no serious adverse events.  
  – Ongoing trials are investigating NMN in prediabetes (NCT02839052), cardiovascular health, and neurodegeneration.  

• Use in retinal disease or dry AMD  
  – No published clinical trials or case reports to date have tested NMN specifically in dry age-related macular degeneration (dry AMD).  
  – A handful of preclinical studies have examined NAD⁺ augmentation in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) or photoreceptor degeneration models (e.g., SIRT1 overexpression in RPE, NAD⁺ depletion models), but direct NMN supplementation in AMD models remains unexplored.  

Mechanism of Action  
• NAD⁺ replenishment  
  – NMN is taken up into cells via specific transporters (Slc12a8 in mouse small intestine; putative transporters in other tissues) and converted by nicotinamide mononucleotide adenylyltransferases (NMNAT1–3) to NAD⁺.  
  – Intracellular NAD⁺ serves as an essential cofactor in redox reactions (glycolysis, TCA cycle, oxidative phosphorylation) and as a substrate for NAD⁺-consuming enzymes.  

• Activation of sirtuins  
  – SIRT1 (nuclear) and SIRT3 (mitochondrial) are NAD⁺-dependent deacetylases that regulate gene transcription, mitochondrial biogenesis, antioxidant defense, and autophagy.  
  – In RPE cells, SIRT1 deacetylates and activates PGC-1α, enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis and autophagic flux (Zhang et al., 2015). SIRT3 deacetylates MnSOD (SOD2), improving ROS detoxification and mitochondrial respiration (Someya et al., 2010).  

• Enhancement of autophagy and mitophagy  
  – NAD⁺-driven SIRT1 activation promotes deacetylation of autophagy proteins (LC3, ATG5), facilitating autophagosome formation.  
  – Improved autophagic flux supports phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) by RPE and clearance of damaged mitochondria.  

• Reduction of oxidative stress and inflammation  
  – NAD⁺ boosts PARP-mediated DNA repair and replenishes NAD⁺ pools consumed during genotoxic stress, preserving genomic integrity.  
  – Enhanced SIRT1 activity represses NF-κB signaling, potentially lowering chronic inflammation in the RPE–choroid complex.  

Expected Effect in RPE Cell Assay  
• NAD⁺ levels and bioenergetics  
  – NMN supplementation is expected to raise intracellular NAD⁺ in cultured human or animal RPE cells, leading to increased oxygen consumption rate (OCR), ATP production, and improved mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm).  

• Sirtuin activation  
  – Upregulated SIRT1 and SIRT3 activity in RPE should be measurable via decreased acetylation of PGC-1α and MnSOD, enhanced SOD2 activity, and elevated expression of mitochondrial biogenesis markers (TFAM, NRF1).  

• Autophagy-dependent phagocytosis of POS  
  – In co-culture or POS-fed assays, NMN-treated RPE cells are predicted to show faster internalization and degradation of fluorescently labeled POS, reflecting improved autophagic flux (LC3-II turnover, p62 clearance).  

• Oxidative stress resilience  
  – Under induced oxidative stress (H₂O₂ or blue light), NMN-pretreated RPE should display reduced ROS accumulation (DCFDA assay) and lower apoptotic markers (cleaved caspase-3).  

• Expression of key proteins in RPE  
  – Literature confirms that human RPE express NAMPT, NMNATs 1–3, SIRT1, SIRT3, PGC-1α, TFAM, LC3, and ATG5 (Bhattacharya et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2019), supporting the feasibility of the proposed mechanistic readouts.  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
  – Mechanistic rationale: Directly targets an age-associated metabolic deficit (declining NAD⁺) implicated in RPE dysfunction.  
  – Broad impact: By boosting sirtuin activity, NMN addresses mitochondrial health, autophagy, oxidative stress, and inflammation—hallmarks of dry AMD pathology.  
  – Safety profile: Early human trials indicate good tolerability, facilitating rapid translation to ocular indications.  
  – Precedent in aging models: Robust preclinical data in multiple tissues suggest efficacy in age-related functional decline.  

Weaknesses and Risks  
  – Ocular bioavailability: Oral NMN’s capacity to reach therapeutic levels in the RPE/choroid is unproven; may require targeted delivery (e.g., intravitreal, periocular, or nanoformulations).  
  – Metabolic fate: Rapid systemic metabolism by CD38 and gut microbiota could limit NMN half-life and tissue uptake.  
  – Off-target effects: Elevating NAD⁺ broadly may affect PARP activity and other NAD⁺-dependent enzymes in unintended ways, potentially impacting cell survival under stress.  
  – Lack of direct AMD data: No published studies have tested NMN in dry AMD animal models (e.g., Ccl2⁻/⁻/Cx3cr1⁻/⁻ mice), so disease-relevant efficacy remains speculative.  

Conclusion  
Given the established decline of NAD⁺ in aging RPE, the crucial role of SIRT1/SIRT3 in mitochondrial and autophagic homeostasis, and NMN’s favorable safety record, NMN is a compelling candidate for early-stage exploration in dry AMD. The next critical steps are (1) demonstration of RPE/choroidal penetration in vivo, (2) proof-of-concept efficacy in an animal model of dry AMD, and (3) development of an ocular formulation to optimize local delivery and minimize systemic metabolism. If these hurdles are addressed, NMN could become a first-in-class NAD⁺-boosting therapy for dry AMD.
