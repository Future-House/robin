Proposal for Recombinant human Complement Factor H (rhCFH)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Recombinant human Complement Factor H (rhCFH) is a full‐length, glycosylated ∼155 kDa plasma protein produced by recombinant DNA technology (most often in CHO or HEK293 expression systems). Native CFH (gene CFH on chr1q32) is a secreted regulator of the alternative complement pathway, composed of 20 complement control protein (CCP) domains. rhCFH therefore belongs to the class of biologics known as complement regulatory proteins. In its recombinant form, the protein recapitulates native glycosylation patterns sufficiently to bind C3b and host‐surface polyanions with high affinity. Biologics of this class (e.g., soluble Factor H–related proteins, engineered mini‐CFH constructs) have been explored in renal and inflammatory conditions to restore complement homeostasis.

Therapeutic History  
•  Atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS) and C3 glomerulopathy: CFH deficiency or dysfunction is a well‐described driver of renal microangiopathy. Preclinical models (CFH−/− mice, 129Sv CFH mutants) have shown that systemic administration of rhCFH or CFH‐mimetic peptides rescues glomerular C3 deposition and restores renal function (Pickering et al., 2002).  
•  Complement‐mediated inflammatory disorders: Engineered CFH fragments and mini‐CFH constructs entered Phase I/II for dense deposit disease and lupus nephritis; safety in humans has been reported with acceptable tolerability (Acacia Pharma, 2018).  
•  Eye‐related applications: To date there are no approved intravitreal or systemic CFH products for AMD. AAV2‐CFH gene therapy vectors have been tested preclinically in murine models of complement dysregulation and drusen‐like deposits (Johnson et al., 2017), but no late‐stage clinical trials of rhCFH for dry AMD have been reported.

Mechanism of Action  
CFH is the principal soluble inhibitor of the alternative complement pathway:  
1.  C3 Convertase Regulation – CFH binds C3b on host surfaces via CCP domains 1–4 and 19–20, accelerating the decay of the C3bBb convertase and serving as a cofactor for Factor I–mediated proteolytic inactivation of C3b (Unknown Reference).  
2.  Host‐Surface Targeting – Domain CCP7 contains a heparin/GAG‐binding site; the Tyr402His polymorphism impairs CFH binding to Bruch’s membrane and RPE extracellular matrix (ECM), leading to uncontrolled local activation of C3 and MAC (C5b‐9) (Clark et al., 2012).  
3.  Inflammasome and NF-κB Modulation – By preventing sublytic MAC formation, CFH indirectly limits NF-κB–driven transcription of pro‐inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-18) and NLRP3 inflammasome assembly in RPE (Tarallo et al., 2012). Restoration of CFH levels has been shown to preserve lysosomal pH and Cathepsin D maturation in RPE challenged with complement (Huang et al., 2016).

Expected Effect in Dry AMD Cell-Based Assays  
In RPE culture models (e.g., ARPE-19, primary human RPE):  
•  C5b-9 Deposition – Addition of rhCFH to complement-active serum reduces AP‐mediated C5b-9 on RPE basolateral membranes (Buschini et al., 2011). Assay readouts: immunofluorescent C5b-9 staining, ELISA for soluble SC5b-9.  
•  NF-κB Activation – CFH co-treatment decreases p65 nuclear translocation induced by sublytic MAC (WB or high-content imaging).  
•  Inflammasome Readouts – Lower IL-1β/IL-18 release (ELISA), reduced ASC speck formation (immunocytochemistry) versus controls.  
•  Lysosomal Function and POS Clearance – rhCFH rescues LysoTracker Red intensity, Cathepsin D activity, and phagocytosis of outer segment–labeled beads (Gelfman et al., 2019).  
RPE cells express CFH receptors (e.g., CR3, GAGs) and synthesize alternative‐pathway components (C3, Factor B) de novo (Unknown Reference), ensuring local complement activation that can be modulated by exogenous rhCFH.

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
•  Addresses a validated root cause of dry AMD: impaired AP control due to CFH dysregulation, especially in Y402H carriers (Majewski et al., 2013).  
•  Precedent for systemic safety in renal indications; full‐length protein likely better mimics physiologic regulation than small‐molecule inhibitors.  
•  Intravitreal delivery places rhCFH in direct contact with Bruch’s membrane and RPE, circumventing blood–retinal barrier.  
•  Multimodal benefit: reduces complement deposition, dampens inflammasome, preserves phagolysosomal clearance.

Weaknesses and Risks  
•  Half-life and penetration – CFH’s large size may limit diffusion through a thickened or lipid-rich Bruch’s membrane; frequent injections may be required.  
•  Polymorphic binding – The common Y402H variant reduces GAG affinity; supplemental CFH must overcome competitive binding from endogenous mutated CFH.  
•  Immunogenicity – Repeated intravitreal injections of a plasma‐derived recombinant protein may elicit intraocular inflammation or neutralizing antibodies.  
•  Off-target complement suppression – Local over-inhibition could raise microbial infection risk or alter normal synaptic pruning.  
•  Manufacturing and Cost – Large‐scale GMP production of full‐length CFH is complex and expensive compared with small proteins or antibody fragments.

Conclusion  
rhCFH is a mechanistically compelling biologic for dry AMD, directly targeting alternative‐pathway dysregulation at the RPE/Bruch’s interface. In vitro RPE assays (complement deposition, NF-κB, inflammasome, lysosomal function) are well‐validated and should effectively screen dose‐response and kinetics of action. Key challenges include optimizing intravitreal formulations for sustained release, mitigating immunogenicity, and confirming sufficient bioavailability in human Bruch’s membrane. Overall, rhCFH represents a high‐value candidate to advance into ex vivo models (human donor RPE–Bruch’s explants) and animal studies, with a clear path to first‐in-human ocular safety trials.
