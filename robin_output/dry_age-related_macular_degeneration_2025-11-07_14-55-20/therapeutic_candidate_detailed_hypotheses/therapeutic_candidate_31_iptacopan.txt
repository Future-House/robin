Proposal for Iptacopan (LNP023, Factor B inhibitor)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Iptacopan (also known as LNP023) is a small-molecule, orally bioavailable inhibitor of complement factor B (FB), discovered initially by high-throughput screening at Achillion Pharmaceuticals and subsequently optimized by medicinal chemistry (structure-based design around a 2,4-dihydrobenzo[1,4]oxazin scaffold). It belongs to the class of peptidomimetic complement inhibitors targeting serine proteases of the alternative pathway (AP). Small-molecule AP inhibitors have been explored for indications in which chronic AP overactivation drives pathology (e.g., paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, C3 glomerulopathy, IgA nephropathy).

Therapeutic History  
– Paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH): Iptacopan is in late-stage clinical trials (Phase III) for PNH, where oral FB inhibition reduces hemolysis and transfusion needs with a favorable safety profile (No significant infections or thromboses).  
– C3 glomerulopathy, IgA nephropathy: Early-phase studies demonstrated reduced glomerular C3 deposition and improved proteinuria.  
– Ophthalmology: To date, there are no published preclinical or clinical studies of iptacopan in dry AMD or other retinal diseases. By contrast, other AP inhibitors such as anti-factor D (lampalizumab) and anti-C3 (pegcetacoplan) have reached AMD trials—with mixed or negative outcomes—highlighting both the validity and challenges of complement targeting in dry AMD.

Mechanism of Action  
– Molecular target: Factor B is the serine protease zymogen that, upon binding to C3b, is cleaved by factor D into Ba (regulatory fragment) and Bb (catalytic subunit). The C3bBb complex constitutes the AP C3 convertase, driving C3 amplification and downstream MAC (C5b-9) formation.  
– Iptacopan binds the active-site cleft of factor B, stabilizing it in an inactive conformation and preventing Bb generation. Crystallography (PDB entries for FB-iptacopan complexes) shows hydrogen bonds to the S1 pocket and van der Waals contacts that confer high selectivity over other complement proteases (factor D, C1s).  
– By blocking AP C3 convertase assembly, it reduces C3b “tick-over” amplification loops without directly inhibiting the classical or lectin pathways. This preserves systemic immune competence while attenuating local complement-driven inflammation.

Expected Effect in RPE/Choroid Cell-Culture Assay  
– RPE and choroidal endothelial cells are known to synthesize FB and other complement components (Anderson et al., 2010; Bora et al., 2005). Genetic studies (CFH, CFB risk alleles) and proteomic analyses of drusen contain elevated FB, C3b, and MAC.  
– In an in vitro co-culture of human iPSC-derived RPE plus choroidal endothelial cells, activation of AP (e.g., via oxidative stress or sublytic C5b-9 exposure) leads to lysosomal dysfunction and impaired photoreceptor outer segment (POS) phagocytosis.  
– Addition of iptacopan is expected to (1) reduce deposition of C3b and sublytic MAC on RPE basal surfaces, (2) preserve lysosomal acidification and degradation capacity (tracked by LysoTracker and DQ-BSA assays), and (3) restore or maintain POS phagocytosis (measured by fluorescently labeled POS uptake assays).  
– Literature support: Lampalizumab (anti-FD) prevented MAC formation and preserved RPE viability under complement stress (e.g., Lyzenga et al., 2018). Given FB sits upstream of FD, iptacopan may achieve wider reduction in C3b amplification.

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
• Mechanistic alignment with AMD genetics: FB inhibition directly targets the overactive AP amplification loop implicated by CFH, CFB, and C3 risk variants.  
• Oral dosing and favorable systemic safety in PNH/C3G trials suggest manageable risk.  
• Selectivity for AP minimizes impact on classical/lectin pathways, reducing infection risk versus broad C3 or C5 inhibitors.  
• Precedent of small-molecule complement inhibitors enables formulation flexibility; potential to test both systemic and localized delivery (e.g., periocular).

Weaknesses & Risks  
• Ocular bioavailability: Human retinal penetration after oral dosing is not yet published; drug concentrations in RPE/choroid must reach IC₅₀ (~5–20 nM) for FB.  
• Alternative pathway redundancy: Local complement regulators (e.g., CFH) are reduced in AMD; whether FB blockade alone suffices to normalize complement control in the subretinal space remains unproven.  
• Previous complement trials in AMD have failed (e.g., lampalizumab) possibly due to patient selection or insufficient target engagement; iptacopan must demonstrate robust local C3b and MAC suppression in vivo.  
• Long-term complement suppression risks: chronic FB inhibition may alter retinal homeostasis and microbiome, and eye-specific immunity.

Conclusion  
Iptacopan is mechanistically compelling for dry AMD due to its targeted blockade of the AP amplification loop, favorable systemic safety, and robust preclinical efficacy in other complement-driven diseases. Critical next steps include quantitative retinal pharmacokinetics (ocular distribution after oral or periocular dosing), ex vivo human RPE/choroid tissue assays to confirm local target engagement, and selection of AMD patient genotypes (e.g., CFB risk carriers) for future proof-of-concept studies.
