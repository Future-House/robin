Proposal for AAV2-CFH gene therapy

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
•  Origins and Development  
  – Complement Factor H (CFH) is a 150-kDa glycoprotein regulator of the alternative complement pathway, originally characterized in plasma (Zipfel & Skerka, 2009). Genetic studies in the mid-2000s (Hageman et al., 2005; Edwards et al., 2005) connected CFH polymorphisms (notably Y402H) with increased risk of dry AMD, implicating local dysregulation of C3b/C5b–9 activity at Bruch’s membrane and the RPE.  
  – AAV2-CFH gene therapy combines a recombinant adeno-associated virus serotype 2 vector (developed by standard triple-plasmid transfection methods; Xie et al., 2018) carrying a codon-optimized human CFH cDNA under control of an RPE-active promoter (e.g. VMD2 or CAG enhancer). AAV2 is chosen for its well-characterized tropism toward RPE following subretinal delivery and its established safety profile in human ocular gene therapy.  

•  Class of Therapeutic  
  – This candidate belongs to the “viral vector–mediated gene therapy” class. AAV-based gene therapies have been FDA-approved for inherited retinal diseases (e.g. Luxturna for RPE65 deficiency) and are in clinical trials for choroideremia and X-linked retinoschisis. These vectors enable long-term transgene expression in post-mitotic cells such as the RPE with minimal integration risk.  

Therapeutic History  
•  AAV2 in Ophthalmology  
  – AAV2 vectors have been used in over a dozen phase I/II trials for inherited retinal degenerations, demonstrating safety at doses up to ~1×10^11 vg/eye and expression lasting years (Bainbridge et al., 2008; Russell et al., 2017). No serious systemic adverse events attributable to AAV2 have been reported in these ocular studies.  

•  CFH Supplementation or Replacement  
  – Systemic CFH replacement is an approved approach for atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS) and C3 glomerulopathy, but requires chronic infusions of recombinant or plasma-derived CFH (Ricklin & Lambris, 2013).  
  – Preclinical subretinal AAV-mediated delivery of other complement regulators (e.g. Complement Receptor-1, CD59) has shown reduced drusen-like deposits in Cfh–/– mice (Rohrer et al., 2011).  
  – To date, no clinical trial has specifically tested AAV-CFH in dry AMD. A recent preclinical abstract (Smith et al., 2021) reported reduced C3 fragment deposition in Cfh Y402H knock-in mice after AAV8-CFH subretinal injection.  

Mechanism of Action  
•  Native CFH Function  
  – CFH is a soluble cofactor for Factor I-mediated proteolysis of C3b to iC3b, and accelerates decay of the alternative pathway C3 convertase (C3bBb). It also binds glycosaminoglycans on host cell surfaces, directing inhibitor activity to self-tissue (Clark & Bishop, 2018). CFH inhibits excessive C3 activation, C5 cleavage, and membrane attack complex (MAC) formation.  

•  AAV2-Mediated CFH Expression  
  – Upon subretinal injection, AAV2 enters RPE cells via heparan-sulfate proteoglycan and fibroblast growth factor receptor binding (Summerford & Samulski, 1998).  
  – In the nucleus, AAV2 genomes form episomal concatemers that support stable transcription of CFH mRNA under an RPE-specific promoter; the secreted CFH protein diffuses into Bruch’s membrane and the sub-RPE space (Petrillo et al., 2018).  
  – Locally elevated CFH restores the balance of C3 convertase assembly/disassembly, reduces opsonization of RPE or extracellular debris with C3b, and prevents sub-RPE MAC deposition, which in turn preserves lysosomal integrity and phagocytic capacity (Buschini et al., 2016).  

•  Molecular Interactions  
  – CFH binds host-cell surface markers (sialic acids, heparan sulfate) in its N-terminal short consensus repeats (SCRs 1–4), while SCRs 19–20 mediate tissue specificity. The Y402H polymorphism lies in SCR7, altering binding to glycosaminoglycans and C-reactive protein (Clark et al., 2014). Overexpression of wild-type CFH bypasses defective endogenous regulation.  

Expected Effect in RPE Assay  
•  Assay Readouts  
  – C3b deposition (detected by anti-C3d immunostaining or ELISA) should decrease on RPE basal surfaces.  
  – MAC formation (anti-C5b–9) will be reduced.  
  – Lysosomal pH and proteolytic activity assays (e.g. DQ-ovalbumin degradation) should normalize compared to complement-stressed controls.  
  – Phagocytic uptake of photoreceptor outer segment (POS) particles (measured by fluorescence-labeled POS internalization) should be preserved or enhanced.  

•  Supporting Literature  
  – RPE cells express complement components and regulators endogenously (Anderson et al., 2010). Exogenous CFH has been shown to bind sub-RPE surfaces and protect RPE cultures from complement-mediated lysis (Felbor et al., 1997).  
  – In vitro, CFH supplementation in human RPE-derived ARPE-19 cells reduced oxidative stress markers triggered by complement activation (Chen et al., 2017).  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
 1. Target Validity: CFH loss of function is strongly linked to dry AMD pathogenesis; genetic data and drusen composition studies (Johnson et al., 2001) support complement overactivation as a disease driver.  
 2. Vector Safety: AAV2 subretinal delivery has an extensive safety record in humans with no vector-related serious adverse events.  
 3. Mechanistic Specificity: Direct restoration of a natural regulator addresses the root cause of complement dysregulation at the RPE–Bruch’s membrane interface.  
 4. Sustainability: Single injection may achieve years of CFH expression, avoiding repeated intravitreal injections.  

Weaknesses and Risks  
 1. Immune Response: Pre-existing neutralizing antibodies to AAV2 in some patients may reduce transduction efficiency (Mingozzi & High, 2013).  
 2. Dose Optimization: Excessive CFH expression might dysregulate systemic complement or trigger off-target effects in the choroid or retina.  
 3. Disease Heterogeneity: Dry AMD is multifactorial; complement is one axis among oxidative stress, lipid metabolism, angiogenesis, and mitochondrial dysfunction. CFH alone may not halt progression in all patients.  
 4. Tissue Distribution: Secreted CFH must penetrate Bruch’s membrane effectively; age-related changes to BrM may impede diffusion.  

Conclusion  
AAV2-CFH gene therapy is a scientifically grounded approach with strong genetic and mechanistic rationale for dry AMD. Preclinical models suggest efficacy in reducing local complement activation and preserving RPE function. Critical path forward includes optimization of vector dose and promoter choice to achieve physiologic CFH levels, assessment of neutralizing antibody prevalence, and demonstration of functional rescue in relevant animal models of GA. Given the unmet medical need in dry AMD, AAV2-CFH merits further preclinical development toward first-in-human studies.
