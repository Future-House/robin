Proposal for Beta-hydroxybutyrate (BHB)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
• Identity and origins  
  – Beta-hydroxybutyrate (BHB) is one of the three endogenous ketone bodies (along with acetoacetate and acetone) produced in liver mitochondria during periods of low glucose availability (fasting, prolonged exercise, ketogenic diet).  In vivo it exists primarily as the R(‐) enantiomer (D-BHB).  Because of its water solubility, D-BHB can be prepared by bacterial fermentation of carbohydrate feedstocks or by chemical synthesis from acetoacetate precursors.  
  – BHB falls into the class of small-molecule metabolites/epigenetic regulators.  As a ketone body, its canonical role is as an alternate energy substrate: it is taken up by extra-hepatic tissues (via monocarboxylate transporters MCT1/2), converted to acetoacetate by β-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase (BDH1), then to acetoacetyl-CoA and ultimately acetyl-CoA for entry into the TCA cycle.  Separately, BHB has emerged as an endogenous histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor at millimolar concentrations and as a ligand for the G-protein–coupled receptor HCAR2 (GPR109A).  

Therapeutic History  
• Metabolic and neurological applications  
  – Ketogenic diets and exogenous ketone supplements (BHB salts/esters) have long been used for refractory epilepsy, where elevated plasma BHB correlates with seizure control.  
  – More recently, clinical trials have explored exogenous BHB in heart failure (improving myocardial energetics), type II diabetes (insulin sensitivity), and neurodegenerative diseases (Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s) on the basis of both energy-substrate rescue and epigenetic/anti-inflammatory effects.  
• Use in ocular disease  
  – Direct use of BHB in retinal or RPE disorders has been limited to a handful of preclinical studies: ketogenic diet models have shown slowed photoreceptor loss in some retinitis pigmentosa mouse models, and BHB infusion protected against ischemia-reperfusion injury in rat retina.  However, there is no clinical precedent for BHB use in dry age-related macular degeneration (dry AMD).  

Mechanism of Action  
1. Energy substrate for RPE and retina  
   – RPE cells are highly phagocytic and energetically demanding, especially as they clear photoreceptor outer segments (POS).  BHB uptake via MCT1/MCT3 (both expressed in human RPE) provides acetyl-CoA for ATP generation under conditions of mitochondrial dysfunction or glucose limitation (Zhao et al., 2011).  
2. HDAC inhibition and epigenetic modulation  
   – Shimazu et al. (2013) first demonstrated that millimolar D-BHB reversibly inhibits class I and class IIa HDACs (IC₅₀ ~2–5 mM for HDAC1/3), leading to increased histone H3K9/K14 acetylation and upregulation of oxidative stress response genes (FOXO3a, MT2, catalase).  In RPE, FOXO3a is a master regulator of autophagy-related genes (ATG5, LC3B, Beclin-1); enhanced transcription could restore autophagic clearance of damaged proteins and phagocytosed POS.  
3. Receptor-mediated anti-inflammatory signaling  
   – BHB is a high-affinity agonist for HCAR2 (GPR109A) at low millimolar concentrations (EC₅₀ ~0.7 mM).  In macrophages and microglia, HCAR2 activation increases intracellular cAMP and suppresses NLRP3 inflammasome assembly (Youm et al., 2015; Rahman et al., 2014).  RPE and resident choroidal macrophages express HCAR2 (Voigt et al., 2016), suggesting BHB could dampen chronic inflammation that drives geographic atrophy in dry AMD.  

Expected Effect in Dry AMD–Relevant Assay  
• Assay context: cultured human RPE cells (e.g., ARPE-19 or primary hRPE) exposed to oxidative stress or POS challenge.  
1. Enhanced phagocytosis and POS turnover  
   – By supplementing BHB (1–5 mM), expect increased ATP production (measured by Seahorse OCR) and restored phagocytic index (quantified by fluorescently labeled POS uptake assays).  Zhao et al. (2011) showed BHB rescued phagocytosis in RPE under nutrient deprivation.  
2. Upregulation of autophagy markers  
   – Western blot or immunofluorescence for LC3-II/I ratio, ATG5, p62 clearance; qPCR for FOXO3a, Beclin-1.  Shimazu et al. (2013) observed ~2-fold induction of autophagy genes in hepatocytes at 4 mM BHB.  Presence of FOXO3a mRNA and protein in RPE has been confirmed (RPE transcriptome atlas).  
3. Anti-inflammatory readouts  
   – In cytokine-primed RPE (e.g., IL-1β or TNFα), measure IL-18/IL-1β secretion, NLRP3 expression by ELISA or RT-qPCR.  Expect BHB-dependent suppression of inflammasome activation (Youm et al., 2015).  
4. Epigenetic state  
   – ChIP-qPCR for histone H3 acetylation at promoters of autophagy and antioxidant genes.  Validate reduction in HDAC1/3 deacetylase activity (commercial HDAC activity assays).  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
 • Dual-mode action: combines metabolic support with epigenetic upregulation of protective gene networks and anti-inflammatory signaling.  
 • Endogenous molecule with excellent safety profile; BHB salts/esters already in human trials for other indications suggest ocular tolerability.  
 • Mechanistic rationale directly addresses two key pathologies in dry AMD RPE: impaired autophagy/phagocytosis and chronic inflammation.  
 • Receptor- and enzyme-based modes of action provide multiple measurable biomarkers in vitro (ATP, OCR, histone acetylation, cytokine readouts).  

Weaknesses and Risks  
 • Effective HDAC inhibition requires high extracellular BHB (≥2–4 mM).  Achieving and maintaining these levels in the subretinal space via systemic dosing may be challenging.  Local delivery (intravitreal or periocular) could mitigate this, but formulation and pH (BHB is acidic) require optimization.  
 • Chronic daily dosing over years may shift systemic metabolism (e.g., altering glucose/insulin homeostasis) or lead to acid–base disturbances in susceptible patients.  
 • Off-target effects: HCAR2 is also on adipocytes and immune cells; systemic activation could have unwanted effects (e.g., flushing, altered lipolysis).  
 • Endogenous ketone pathways may be downregulated in aged RPE (e.g., reduced MCT expression), limiting uptake.  Expression of BDH1 and SCOT (succinyl-CoA:3-oxoacid CoA transferase) declines with age in some tissues; RPE data are sparse.  
 • Long-term impact on retinal function and photoreceptor health remains untested in dry AMD models; data extrapolated from acute injury or genetic rodent models may not fully translate.  

Conclusion  
Exogenous BHB is a compelling repurposing candidate for dry AMD.  Its combination of epigenetic, metabolic, and anti-inflammatory mechanisms directly targets core RPE pathologies.  Key next steps should include:  
 1. Pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic studies in animal models of geographic atrophy to determine achievable ocular concentrations and duration.  
 2. In vitro dose–response assays in primary human RPE under AMD-mimetic stressors to define minimal efficacious BHB concentration for HDAC inhibition and autophagy rescue.  
 3. Formulation development for sustained ocular delivery, ensuring pH neutrality and targeting to RPE.  
 4. Exploration of synergistic combinations (e.g., low-dose BHB plus small-molecule autophagy inducers or anti-oxidants).  

If these studies confirm robust RPE rescue at clinically tolerable doses, BHB stands out as a rapid-to-clinic candidate for dry AMD.
