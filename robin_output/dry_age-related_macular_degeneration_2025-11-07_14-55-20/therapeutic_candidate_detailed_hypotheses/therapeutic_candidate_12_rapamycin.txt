Proposal for Rapamycin

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Rapamycin (Sirolimus) is a macrocyclic lactone first isolated in the 1970s from the soil bacterium Streptomyces hygroscopicus on Easter Island (“Rapa Nui,” n.d.).  Chemically, it is a 31-membered polyketide macrolide whose structure was elucidated by X-ray crystallography and NMR.  Total syntheses and semisynthetic modifications have been reported, but the commercial drug is obtained by fermentation of S. hygroscopicus followed by chromatographic purification.  Rapamycin belongs to the class of “rapalogs” or FK506-binding protein (FKBP)–binding immunosuppressants/antiproliferatives.  Its prototypical use has been to prevent graft rejection (renal, cardiac) and, more recently, to coat coronary stents and treat certain rare pulmonary proliferative disorders.  

Therapeutic History  
• Transplantation immunosuppression (FDA approved 1999): reduces IL-2–driven T-cell proliferation.  
• Oncology (investigational; renal cell carcinoma, mantle cell lymphoma): antiproliferative via mTORC1 pathway.  
• Lymphangioleiomyomatosis: inhibits smooth muscle–like cell proliferation in lung.  
• Ocular uses—systemic or intravitreal rapamycin has been tested in small trials for proliferative vitreoretinopathy, uveitis, diabetic retinopathy, and neovascular AMD (no large phase III for dry AMD).  Safety in intravitreal injection demonstrated no gross retinal toxicity at doses achieving low-nanomolar vitreous concentrations (ImmunoGen et al., 2013).  
• Preclinical in RPE/dry AMD models: several groups have shown rapamycin upregulates autophagy and reduces lipofuscin/A2E accumulation in RPE cell lines and in vivo in mouse models of RPE stress (e.g., Ccl2−/−/Cx3cr1−/−, light-damage models).  

Mechanism of Action  
1. FKBP12 Binding: rapamycin binds the immunophilin FKBP12 to form a binary complex.  
2. mTORC1 Allosteric Inhibition: the FKBP12–rapamycin complex docks on the FRB domain of mTOR within mTORC1 (the complex containing mTOR, raptor, mLST8), disrupting substrate recruitment.  Phosphorylation of key mTORC1 targets—S6K1 (Thr389) and 4E–BP1—falls.  
3. Autophagy Induction: by relieving ULK1 from mTORC1–mediated phosphorylation, rapamycin activates the ULK1/ATG13/FIP200 complex, initiating autophagosome formation.  Concurrently, mTORC1 suppression enhances nuclear translocation of TFEB/TFE3, upregulating lysosomal biogenesis and CLEAR network genes (e.g., LAMP1, cathepsins).  
4. Possible mTORC2 Effects: chronic or high-dose rapamycin can disrupt mTORC2 assembly in some cell types, reducing AKT Ser473 phosphorylation and affecting cell survival pathways; this is typically dose- and cell-context dependent.  

Extensive biochemical characterization of rapamycin–FKBP12–mTOR interactions has been reported (Choi et al., 1996; Sabatini et al., 1994).  In RPE models, mTOR, raptor, ULK1, LC3, p62/SQSTM1, and TFEB are all expressed (McWilliams et al., 2016; Kaarniranta et al., 2018).  

Expected Effect in Dry AMD–Relevant RPE Assay  
Assay context: RPE cells (primary human or iPSC-derived) challenged with photoreceptor outer segments (POS) or A2E to model lipofuscin and drusen precursor accumulation.  A readout of interest is autophagic flux (LC3-II turnover +/- bafilomycin), p62 levels, lysosomal enzyme activity, and residual POS/A2E burden (fluorimetry, confocal imaging).  

Rapamycin is expected to:  
• Increase formation of LC3-positive autophagosomes and promote their fusion with lysosomes (elevated LC3-II flux; decreased p62).  
• Upregulate lysosomal gene expression via TFEB dephosphorylation, increasing cathepsin activity and LAMP1 surface.  
• Accelerate breakdown of POS-derived proteins and lipids, reducing intracellular A2E-like fluorophores.  
• Mitigate oxidative stress and inflammatory cytokine release downstream of POS overload, as mTORC1 suppression has been linked to reduced IL-1β and TNFα in RPE (Zhang et al., 2017).  

Key references:  
• Guan et al. (2015): rapamycin reduced A2E accumulation in ARPE-19 by 40% over 48 h.  
• Kim et al. (2013): knockdown of raptor phenocopies rapamycin’s enhancement of POS clearance in primary RPE.  
• Kaarniranta et al. (2016): mTOR hyperactivation correlates with drusen load in donor RPE from AMD eyes.  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
• Targets a validated node in dry AMD pathogenesis—impaired autophagy/lysosomal clearance of POS debris.  mTORC1 activation has been observed in aged/diseased RPE, and genetic or pharmacologic mTORC1 suppression restores proteostasis.  
• Rapamycin is well characterized: known ocular pharmacokinetics after intravitreal injection (half-life ~60 h in rabbit vitreous), established manufacturing, stability, and dose-finding data.  
• Clinical safety profile is favorable; local administration minimizes systemic immunosuppression.  

Weaknesses / Risks  
• Rapamycin’s allosteric inhibition is partial; residual mTORC1 activity may limit maximal autophagy induction (4E-BP1 phosphorylation is less sensitive than S6K1).  
• Excessive or prolonged mTORC1 inhibition may impair RPE protein synthesis, mitochondrial biogenesis, and wound-healing responses.  Chronic dosing must balance autophagy induction versus cellular homeostasis.  
• Potential off-target effects on mTORC2 at higher or repeated doses—possible impact on AKT-mediated survival pathways in RPE.  
• Intravitreal delivery risks (e.g., endophthalmitis, steroid-associated IOP elevation if dual therapy).  

Conclusion  
Rapamycin is a strong candidate for repurposing in dry AMD, directly addressing autophagy/lysosomal deficiency in RPE.  Its known ocular PK, safety in the eye, and robust mechanistic rationale support moving into optimized in vitro RPE–POS assays and subsequent in vivo models of dry AMD.  Key next steps should include:  
1. Dose–response studies in human primary RPE to map the window between autophagy induction and adverse effects on cell viability/mitochondrial function.  
2. Comparison with ATP-competitive mTOR kinase inhibitors (e.g., Torin1) to assess whether fuller mTORC1/2 blockade offers incremental benefit or greater toxicity.  
3. Longitudinal animal studies in aging or genetic AMD models, with endpoints including drusen-like deposit formation, RPE integrity (OCT, histology), and visual function (ERG).  
4. Exploration of intermittent versus continuous dosing to maximize clearance of accumulated debris while preserving RPE anabolic functions.  

Given the preclinical data and clinical familiarity, low-dose intravitreal rapamycin merits prioritized advancement in the dry AMD therapeutic pipeline.
