Proposal for Divanillin (Phagocytosis enhancer; Biomol Cat. No. ENZ-CH3)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Divanillin is a naturally occurring oxidative dimer of vanillin that belongs to the class of phenolic dimers. Its synthesis is based on the oxidative coupling of monomeric vanillin—a well‐known flavor and fragrance molecule—using either chemical oxidants or, more efficiently, enzymatic methods. For instance, studies have demonstrated that fungal laccases, in particular, those derived from white-rot fungi such as Funalia trogii, can oxidize vanillin to form divanillin with near-quantitative yields under controlled reaction conditions (Esparan, 2015; Tai et al., 2011). Historically, divanillin was first noted for its presence in cured vanilla beans and has subsequently been developed for applications in the flavor and fragrance industries owing to its potent taste-enhancing properties, including its well-documented bitterness masking effects and its very low sensory threshold (Esparan, 2015; Krings et al., 2015; Torres-Zamudio et al., 2025). Its chemical profile, with lower water solubility compared to vanillin, also sets it apart as a candidate in formulations where controlled bioavailability is desired. As a low-molecular-weight compound, divanillin is amenable to formulation for ocular applications, and its repurposing as a modulator of intracellular signaling pathways, specifically in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), is a novel extension of its traditional roles.  

Therapeutic History:  
Divanillin’s historical usage has been largely in the realm of food and cosmetic applications. It has been known since the late 19th century and has been subject to extensive chemical characterization and synthesis studies through the early to mid-20th century (Esparan, 2015; Krings et al., 2015). In these fields, it is typically employed as a taste enhancer and bitterness masking agent, with its generation via enzymatic oxidation contributing to its characterization as a stable, bioactive phenolic dimer (Esparan, 2015; Torres-Zamudio et al., 2025). Over the years, the antioxidant properties of vanillin and its derivatives have been a major focus, with research demonstrating that multi-vanillin moieties can yield compounds with dramatically enhanced antioxidant activity compared to vanillin alone (Scipioni et al., 2018). Although these studies have primarily concentrated on neuroprotection, DNA damage protection, and cellular antioxidant capacities—such as those observed in neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cells—the literature does not currently report any direct clinical or preclinical application of divanillin for retinal diseases, including dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) (Scipioni et al., 2018; Tai et al., 2011). In parallel, a number of small-molecule SIRT1 activators, such as resveratrol and other natural polyphenols, have been explored in various ocular models and have shown promise in protecting RPE cells from oxidative damage and in modulating inflammatory pathways (Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Ishida et al., 2017; Taurone et al., 2022). Thus, while divanillin itself has not been previously used for treating dry AMD or similar retinal conditions, its structural relationship to vanillin combined with emerging evidence regarding the benefits of vanillin derivatives suggests that repurposing divanillin for ocular use is a feasible and promising strategy.

Mechanism of Action:  
The central hypothesis proposed for divanillin is that it can function as a small molecule activator of SIRT1 in retinal pigment epithelial cells by achieving an effective concentration (EC₅₀) of approximately 1 µM. SIRT1 is a nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+)-dependent deacetylase that has critical roles in cellular metabolism, stress responses, and longevity. One of its well-established functions is the deacetylation of key cytoskeletal regulatory proteins, including cortactin and cofilin, which govern actin assembly and remodeling (Shentu et al., 2016). Cortactin, for instance, is a scaffold protein that binds to filamentous actin (F-actin) and the Arp2/3 complex and is involved in the formation of cell protrusions and phagocytic cups, whereas cofilin facilitates actin filament severing and turnover. In endothelial cells, it has been shown that AMPK phosphorylates cortactin at a critical threonine residue, priming it for SIRT1-mediated deacetylation; this sequential modification is essential for cortactin’s proper localization at the cell periphery and for dynamic actin remodeling (Shentu et al., 2016). Although this mechanism has been elucidated mainly in endothelial and other cell types, it is highly plausible that a similar pathway operates in RPE cells, where robust cytoskeletal dynamics are needed to support the daily phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) (Tarau et al., 2019; Cabrera, 2018).  

In RPE cells, phagocytosis is not a mere housekeeping task but a highly regulated process that demands rapid and targeted rearrangement of the actin cytoskeleton to form phagocytic cups that engulf the shed POS. Emerging studies have demonstrated that oxidative stress—a feature central to dry AMD pathology—can cause significant disruptions in the actin cytoskeleton, which contribute to impaired POS uptake and cellular degeneration (Tarau et al., 2019). SIRT1 activation, by deacetylating substrates such as cortactin and possibly cofilin, may restore or enhance the dynamic assembly of F-actin, thereby promoting more efficient phagocytic cup formation and POS internalization. In parallel, activation of the SIRT1/PGC-1α axis in RPE cells has been linked with improved mitochondrial biogenesis and antioxidant responses, offering a dual mode of cytoprotection—a mechanism that is particularly attractive in the context of chronic oxidative stress typical of dry AMD (Ishida et al., 2017; Buonfiglio et al., 2024). Furthermore, other small-molecule SIRT1 activators have already demonstrated the capacity to modulate oxidative stress and confer cellular protection in ocular models (Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Taurone et al., 2022), lending additional credence to the hypothesis that divanillin may exert similar beneficial effects. In summary, the proposed mechanism of action for divanillin involves its SIRT1 activation properties leading to enhanced deacetylation of cytoskeletal regulatory proteins, which in turn promotes actin reorganization necessary for efficient phagocytosis by RPE cells under conditions of oxidative stress.

Expected Effect:  
Under the experimental conditions outlined in the proposal, treatment of RPE cells with divanillin at concentrations around its EC₅₀ of 1 µM is expected to result in the activation of SIRT1, a response that could be measured using biochemical assays indicative of its deacetylase activity. This SIRT1 activation is anticipated to lead to the deacetylation of its substrates, notably cortactin and cofilin, which are critical to the regulation of F-actin polymerization and reorganization (Shentu et al., 2016). At the molecular level, enhanced deacetylation of cortactin should facilitate its translocation to the cell periphery, where it can interact more effectively with the actin cytoskeleton, thereby promoting the formation of phagocytic cups, structures that are essential for the ingestion of POS (Tarau et al., 2019; Cabrera, 2018). The resulting improvement in cytoskeletal dynamics is projected to augment the ability of RPE cells to internalize shed photoreceptor outer segments—a process that is compromised in dry AMD due to cumulative oxidative damage and cytoskeletal disruption (Scipioni et al., 2018; Tarau et al., 2019).

Furthermore, activation of SIRT1 is expected to simultaneously confer additional cytoprotective benefits, including the enhancement of mitochondrial biogenesis and function through the SIRT1/PGC-1α axis. This effect is highly relevant in RPE cells because their high metabolic demand and constant exposure to oxidative stress make them particularly vulnerable to mitochondrial dysfunction (Ishida et al., 2017; Buonfiglio et al., 2024). The improved mitochondrial function coupled with enhanced phagocytic efficiency would likely reduce the accumulation of oxidative damage and cellular debris, thereby mitigating key pathological features of dry AMD. Based on analogous studies employing other SIRT1 activators—such as resveratrol, which has been shown to protect RPE cells from oxidative stress and improve their phagocytic capability—the anticipated outcomes with divanillin include a measurable increase in POS uptake and improved cellular resilience under stress conditions (Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Taurone et al., 2022). Consequently, these translational effects would be expected to translate into overall improved cellular homeostasis and a potential retardation of the degenerative processes observed in dry AMD.

Overall Evaluation:  
In assessing divanillin as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD, several compelling strengths emerge. First and foremost, divanillin is a small-molecule agent that can be synthesized via well-established enzymatic processes from vanillin, a compound with a long history of safe use in food and cosmetic applications (Esparan, 2015). This favorable tolerability profile, combined with its robust antioxidant properties, provides a strong rationale for its repurposing in the ocular setting. The preclinical literature on vanillin derivatives indicates that incorporating multiple vanillin moieties into a single molecule can lead to a dramatic increase in antioxidant and cytoprotective activities (Scipioni et al., 2018), suggesting that divanillin may confer similar benefits in the context of retinal degeneration.  

Moreover, the innovative hypothesis that divanillin can activate SIRT1 in RPE cells is supported by a broader body of work demonstrating that SIRT1 plays a critical role in modulating oxidative stress, mitochondrial biogenesis, and cytoskeletal dynamics. There is substantial literature illustrating that SIRT1 activation leads to the deacetylation of key regulatory proteins such as cortactin, thereby promoting actin cytoskeleton remodeling—a process essential for phagocytic cup formation and efficient photoreceptor outer segment internalization (Shentu et al., 2016; Tarau et al., 2019). In this regard, targeting the SIRT1 pathway is a sound strategy, given that other natural SIRT1 activators, including resveratrol, have exhibited beneficial effects in RPE models under oxidative stress (Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Taurone et al., 2022). This dual action—not only providing antioxidant protection but also enhancing the structural functionality of RPE cells—addresses two critical components of dry AMD pathogenesis: oxidative damage and impaired phagocytosis.

Despite these promising attributes, several weaknesses and uncertainties remain. Foremost among these is the lack of direct experimental evidence linking divanillin with SIRT1 activation in RPE cells. The proposed effective concentration (EC₅₀ ~ 1 µM) is based on extrapolations from studies with other vanillin derivatives and SIRT1 activators; however, no published data currently validate this specific potency for divanillin in ocular cells. In addition, while the mechanism by which SIRT1 deacetylates cortactin and potentially cofilin is well established in endothelial cells and other models (Shentu et al., 2016), it remains an assumption that RPE cells will respond identically, given their unique metabolic demands and specialized phagocytic functions (Tarau et al., 2019; Cabrera, 2018).  

There is also a significant gap concerning the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties of divanillin in the context of ocular delivery. Although its small-molecule status and prior applications in non-ocular formulations suggest that it may have acceptable ocular tolerability and penetration, dedicated studies in retinal models or in vivo eye preparations are necessary to confirm its bioavailability and efficacy at the site of action. Furthermore, the multifactorial nature of dry AMD—with contributions from chronic inflammation, complement system dysregulation, and extracellular deposit formation—means that targeting SIRT1 and cytoskeletal dynamics may represent only one aspect of a comprehensive therapeutic approach (Crooke et al., 2017; Taurone et al., 2022). It will be important to consider whether divanillin’s purported mechanism can effectively address these other pathogenic mechanisms either alone or in combination with other treatments.

Another limitation is that, while divanillin’s antioxidant and cytoprotective properties have been indirectly demonstrated in studies examining vanillin and its derivatives (Scipioni et al., 2018; Tai et al., 2011), the direct link between these properties and improved phagocytic performance in RPE cells is yet to be established. Given that RPE phagocytosis is a highly specialized function that relies on precise spatial and temporal coordination of cytoskeletal activity (Tarau et al., 2019; Cabrera, 2018), even modest discrepancies in the modulation of actin dynamics could have substantial downstream effects on cellular homeostasis.  

Taken together, the overall evaluation of divanillin as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD is cautiously optimistic. On the strength side, divanillin’s natural origin, ease of synthesis, and demonstrated antioxidant activity support its potential repurposing as a SIRT1 activator in the RPE. Its ability to modulate SIRT1—if validated experimentally—could translate into improved deacetylation of cytoskeletal regulatory proteins, enhanced F-actin remodeling, and more efficient phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments. These effects, in turn, could mitigate key aspects of dry AMD pathology, including the accumulation of oxidative damage and the gradual loss of RPE function. Such a dual mechanism of action—combining antioxidant protection with improved cellular architecture—is particularly attractive given the multifactorial etiology of dry AMD (Scipioni et al., 2018; Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Shentu et al., 2016).  

However, the proposal is not without its challenges. The absence of direct evidence for divanillin’s SIRT1 activation in RPE cells, an unvalidated EC₅₀, and potential differences in the response of RPE cells compared to other cell types mean that significant preclinical work remains to be done. Rigorous in vitro studies using primary RPE cultures or established RPE cell lines will be necessary to confirm that divanillin can indeed enhance SIRT1 activity, deacetylate key substrates like cortactin and cofilin, and ultimately improve phagocytic cup formation and POS uptake. Additionally, in vivo studies using animal models of dry AMD will be critical to evaluate the compound’s pharmacokinetics, ocular bioavailability, and long-term safety and efficacy.  

In conclusion, divanillin represents a promising novel candidate for the treatment of dry age-related macular degeneration. Its potential to activate SIRT1 and thereby enhance actin cytoskeleton remodeling and phagocytosis addresses two critical pathological aspects of dry AMD—oxidative stress and impaired clearance of photoreceptor outer segments. Nonetheless, the current body of literature provides only indirect support for this mechanism, and several key areas require further investigation. If subsequent experimental studies confirm the proposed SIRT1 activation and cytoskeletal effects in RPE cells, divanillin could emerge as a valuable addition to the therapeutic arsenal against dry AMD, either as a monotherapy or as part of a combinatory treatment regimen. This candidate, therefore, warrants immediate prioritization in preclinical screening assays with a focus on verifying its molecular mechanism, efficacy in enhancing phagocytosis, and its overall impact on RPE cell function under oxidative stress conditions (Scipioni et al., 2018; Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Shentu et al., 2016; Cabrera, 2018; Ishida et al., 2017).
