Proposal for CD59-Fc fusion protein (Complement MAC inhibitor; Genentech research reagent)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
The therapeutic candidate under evaluation is a CD59-Fc fusion protein, a recombinant biologic that combines the extracellular functional domain of CD59—a naturally occurring complement regulatory protein—with the Fc portion of an immunoglobulin (typically IgG1) to extend its ocular half‐life and improve tissue penetration. This fusion protein is synthesized using recombinant DNA technology in mammalian cell expression systems to ensure proper post‐translational modifications and folding, which are critical for retaining high binding affinity (with a reported dissociation constant, Kd, of <1 nM) toward its targets, the complement components C8 and C9, on retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) surfaces (Qin et al., 2021; Tan, 2018). The compound belongs to the class of complement inhibitors—a group of therapeutic agents that have been developed to modulate the immune complement cascade, thereby mitigating complement-mediated tissue damage; historically, this class has been primarily investigated for inflammatory and degenerative conditions, including paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, and more recently age-related macular degeneration (AMD) (Cerniauskas et al., 2020). The rationale behind harnessing a CD59-based approach is based on the well-established physiological role of native CD59 in inhibiting the terminal pathway of complement activation by blocking the incorporation of C9 into the C5b-8 complex to form the membrane attack complex (MAC), thus preventing cell lysis and downstream inflammatory cascades (Tan et al., 2016).

Therapeutic History:  
CD59, as a natural complement regulatory protein, has been a focus of research for several years due to its intrinsic ability to safeguard host tissues from complement-mediated injury; multiple studies have demonstrated that impaired CD59 recycling or decreased CD59 surface expression is associated with increased MAC deposition on RPE cells in AMD models, ultimately leading to lysosomal dysfunction and cellular injury (Cerniauskas et al., 2020). Although the CD59-Fc fusion protein itself has not been extensively tested in clinical settings, analogous therapeutic approaches—including gene therapy strategies such as AAVCAGsCD59, which deliver soluble forms of CD59 to the retina—have been evaluated preclinically and in early-phase clinical trials, with evidence suggesting that enhancement of CD59 function can reduce complement-mediated damage in the eye (Garcia-Garcia et al., 2022; Qin et al., 2021). In addition, complementary studies using other classes of complement inhibitors (for example, compstatin analogs like Cp40 that act upstream at the C3 convertase level) have demonstrated reversal of pathological phenotypes in AMD models by protecting lysosomal integrity and restoring RPE cellular function (Cerniauskas et al., 2020). While most of the clinical research in dry AMD has focused on C3- and C5-targeting agents (such as pegcetacoplan and avacincaptad pegol, respectively) with mixed outcomes, these data collectively support the strategy of terminal pathway inhibition as a viable approach; however, the CD59-Fc candidate distinguishes itself by directly recapitulating the naturally evolved mechanism of MAC inhibition (Cerniauskas et al., 2020; Garcia-Garcia et al., 2022). Notably, while the clinical history of CD59-based therapies in AMD is still in its early stages, preclinical models strongly implicate the loss of CD59 function in the pathogenesis of dry AMD, thereby making CD59 enhancement a rational repurposing strategy in this context (Tan et al., 2016).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, the CD59-Fc fusion protein acts by binding specifically to the complement components C8 and/or C9, which is critical during the final assembly phase of the MAC; normally, native CD59 prevents the recruitment of C9 to the C5b-8 complex, thereby effectively halting pore formation on the target cell membrane, which would otherwise lead to uncontrolled ion fluxes, cell lysis, or sub-lytic MAC deposition that can trigger pro-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic signaling cascades (Tan et al., 2016). The high affinity binding (Kd < 1 nM) is expected to allow the fusion protein to effectively compete with endogenous CD59, thereby providing robust inhibition even in the milieu of heightened complement activation as observed in AMD (Cerniauskas et al., 2020). In addition to its direct inhibition of MAC assembly, the Fc fragment confers several pharmacokinetic advantages: it enhances the protein’s stability, increases its half-life through neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn)-mediated recycling, and may facilitate localized retention within the ocular environment upon intravitreal injection, ensuring sustained therapeutic levels over prolonged treatment intervals (Garcia-Garcia et al., 2022; Qin et al., 2021). Moreover, by preventing MAC deposition on RPE cells, the candidate is anticipated to safeguard the integrity of lysosomal membranes—a process crucial for proper autophagic function and the degradation of photoreceptor outer segments (POS); lysosomal damage through MAC-induced membrane permeabilization has been implicated in RPE dysfunction and the subsequent progression of dry AMD (Cerniauskas et al., 2020). On a cellular level, experimental models have shown that complement attack leads to lysosomal swelling, membrane fragility, and eventual mitochondrial dysfunction due to leakage of lysosomal enzymes (such as Cathepsin D) into the cytosol or drusen-like deposits; by mitigating MAC formation, CD59-Fc is expected to interrupt this deleterious cascade and restore cellular homeostasis (Cerniauskas et al., 2020).

Expected Effect:  
The expected pharmacodynamic effect of the CD59-Fc fusion protein centers on its capacity to prevent MAC deposition on the cell surface of RPE cells; this is hypothesized to occur through the high-affinity binding of the fusion protein to complement components C8 and C9, thereby precluding the insertion of the complete MAC pore into the plasma or lysosomal membranes of these cells (Cerniauskas et al., 2020). By averting MAC formation, the candidate helps maintain lysosomal membrane integrity, which in turn supports normal autophagic degradation and proper phagocytic processing of POS—a process that is critically impaired in AMD and is associated with lipofuscin accumulation and further complement activation (Cerniauskas et al., 2020). In functional assays, treatment with CD59-Fc is expected to result in a significant reduction of intracellular MAC deposition, as evidenced by decreased staining of markers associated with complement activation, and to preserve lysosomal structure, as reflected by normalized lysosome size and reduced markers of lysosomal membrane permeability (Cerniauskas et al., 2020). This preservation of lysosomal function is central to ensuring that the RPE retains its ability to efficiently phagocytose and degrade POS, thereby sustaining the retinal microenvironment and potentially slowing or reversing the progression of geographic atrophy—the late stage of dry AMD (Cerniauskas et al., 2020). Moreover, by protecting mitochondrial integrity indirectly through the maintenance of lysosomal function, CD59-Fc may also reduce oxidative stress and apoptotic signaling within RPE cells, contributing further to cell survival and retinal health (Tan et al., 2016). The candidate’s mechanism is supported by data from ex vivo experiments in complement-challenged explants, where application of complement inhibitors similar to CD59-based agents reduced MAC deposition and preserved lysosomal morphology (Garcia-Garcia et al., 2022; Qin et al., 2021). Additionally, studies where cholesterol accumulation or the presence of oxidized lipoproteins disrupts CD59 recycling correlate with enhanced MAC deposition and lysosomal dysfunction, reinforcing the notion that restoring CD59 activity through a fusion protein could have broad protective effects on the RPE (Cerniauskas et al., 2020; Garcia-Garcia et al., 2022).

Overall Evaluation:  
In assessing the potential of the CD59-Fc fusion protein as a therapeutic candidate for dry age-related macular degeneration, several strengths and weaknesses become apparent. On the positive side, the candidate leverages the body’s natural defense mechanism against complement-mediated injury by directly targeting a key step in the formation of the membrane attack complex, a process that has been strongly implicated in the pathogenesis of AMD. Numerous preclinical studies have established that excessive MAC deposition on RPE cells leads to lysosomal dysfunction, impaired autophagy, and subsequent RPE cell death, all of which are critical factors in the progression of geographic atrophy (Cerniauskas et al., 2020). The CD59-Fc fusion protein is therefore expected to interrupt this pathological cascade by preserving lysosomal integrity and promoting the maintenance of cellular homeostasis, with potential downstream benefits including the restoration of normal POS phagocytosis and improved metabolic health of RPE cells (Cerniauskas et al., 2020).

Another strength is the inclusion of the Fc moiety, which not only extends the protein’s half-life, ensuring sustained therapeutic exposure in the eye, but may also aid in achieving effective localized concentrations following intravitreal administration (Garcia-Garcia et al., 2022; Qin et al., 2021). This pharmacokinetic advantage is particularly significant in ocular therapies, where frequent dosing and short half-life of drugs have historically challenged the long-term management of chronic conditions such as dry AMD. Moreover, the concept of using a recombinant fusion protein to mimic or enhance endogenous regulatory mechanisms has precedence in other therapeutic areas, providing a solid scientific foundation for this approach (Garcia-Garcia et al., 2022; Tan et al., 2016).

However, there are also notable challenges and potential weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. One primary concern is that, while preclinical models and ex vivo explant studies provide promising evidence for the efficacy of complement inhibitors in halting or reversing complement-mediated damage, the translation of these findings into clinical success in dry AMD has been fraught with mixed results. Several complement inhibitors, particularly those targeting upstream components like C3 and C5 (e.g., pegcetacoplan, avacincaptad pegol), have shown significant anatomical benefits such as reduced geographic atrophy lesion growth but have failed to reliably improve visual acuity, and in some instances have been associated with adverse events such as conversion to neovascular AMD (Tzoumas et al., 2023). Although CD59-Fc acts at a different point in the complement cascade, the complexity of the complement system in the ocular microenvironment and the possibility of compensatory mechanisms remain a potential hurdle that could limit therapeutic efficacy (Tzoumas et al., 2023).

Another consideration is that the CD59-Fc fusion protein has yet to be tested extensively in in vivo models of AMD, and its pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetics in the human eye are largely unknown. Challenges such as achieving sufficient tissue penetration, avoiding systemic immune activation, and mitigating any unforeseen off-target effects will need to be addressed in rigorous preclinical studies before any clinical trial can be contemplated (Cerniauskas et al., 2020; Garcia-Garcia et al., 2022). Additionally, there is always the risk associated with biologics that their large molecular size or Fc-mediated effector functions might lead to immunogenicity or unexpected interactions with other components of the immune system, potentially triggering inflammatory responses—this is particularly critical in the immune-privileged ocular environment where preserving immune homeostasis is essential (Cerniauskas et al., 2020).

Finally, while the hypothesis postulates that by protecting lysosomal membranes CD59-Fc will indirectly improve POS phagocytic and degradative function, the relationship between complement suppression and restoration of complex RPE cell functions remains to be conclusively established in AMD pathology. Although there is compelling evidence that complement-mediated MAC deposition is deleterious to lysosomal function and that complement inhibition can restore cellular homeostasis, the multifactorial nature of AMD pathogenesis—which includes oxidative stress, lipid dysregulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and genetic predispositions—means that CD59-Fc may need to be used in combination with other therapeutic strategies to achieve clinically meaningful outcomes (Cerniauskas et al., 2020; Tan et al., 2016).

In summary, the CD59-Fc fusion protein represents a promising repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD based on its profound mechanistic rationale, which is anchored in the prevention of MAC formation and the subsequent protection of lysosomal membranes in RPE cells. Its design to mimic and potentiate a natural protective pathway, combined with the pharmacokinetic benefits conferred by the Fc fusion, positions it favorably among complement-based therapeutic strategies. However, the challenges of translating preclinical successes into reliable clinical outcomes in the heterogeneous and multifactorial landscape of AMD cannot be understated. A focused program of further preclinical studies—including detailed pharmacodynamic assessments, tissue distribution, efficacy in robust in vivo models of dry AMD, and safety evaluations—will be essential to validate the therapeutic potential of CD59-Fc prior to embarking on human clinical trials. Overall, while the strengths of the candidate are compelling, its ultimate success will depend on resolving the inherent complexities of complement regulation in the retinal microenvironment and ensuring that the beneficial effects on lysosomal preservation translate into meaningful improvements in visual function and disease progression in patients with dry AMD (Cerniauskas et al., 2020; Qin et al., 2021; Tan et al., 2016; Garcia-Garcia et al., 2022).
