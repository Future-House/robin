Proposal for Trichostatin A (HDAC inhibitor; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. T1952)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Trichostatin A (TSA) is a naturally occurring compound originally isolated from fermentation cultures of Streptomyces species (Grayson et al., 2010). Belonging to the hydroxamate class of histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors, TSA possesses a distinct molecular architecture characterized by three critical structural motifs: a hydroxamic acid moiety, a hydrophobic linker, and a bulky polar cap. These elements cooperate to enable TSA’s high‐affinity binding to the zinc ion within the catalytic site of HDAC enzymes, thereby blocking deacetylation reactions (Grayson et al., 2010). Unlike many newer synthetic compounds, TSA’s discovery dates back several decades, where it was initially identified for its antifungal properties yet quickly found utility as a potent epigenetic modulator in various research fields, including oncology and neurobiology (Grayson et al., 2010; Hamid et al., 2021). Within this class, HDAC inhibitors are well recognized for their ability to alter chromatin configuration; by preventing the removal of acetyl groups from histone tails, these compounds induce chromatin relaxation and, as a consequence, facilitate robust transcriptional activation of genes that are otherwise epigenetically repressed (Grayson et al., 2010; Hamid et al., 2021). In the context of ocular research, TSA has been exploited primarily as a tool compound to dissect the role of epigenetic modifications in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) function and has been applied in studies examining angiogenesis, apoptosis, and inflammatory responses in RPE cells (Hamid et al., 2021). Its availability from commercial suppliers such as Sigma-Aldrich (Cat. No. T1952) underscores its utility as a research-grade standard for HDAC inhibition studies, making it an attractive candidate for drug repurposing efforts aimed at diseases such as dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) where impaired transcription of clearance and autophagy genes plays a pivotal role (Grayson et al., 2010; Hamid et al., 2021).

Therapeutic History:  
TSA has garnered extensive attention over the years in the biomedical literature due to its potent HDAC inhibitory function. Originally explored for its antifungal properties, subsequent studies rapidly shifted focus toward its ability to cause histone hyperacetylation, thereby rekindling interest in epigenetic regulation as a therapeutic strategy for various pathologies (Grayson et al., 2010; Hamid et al., 2021). In oncology, for instance, TSA and its synthetic derivatives have been used to induce cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in cancer cells, ultimately leading to the development of newer, more selective HDAC inhibitors that have reached clinical trials and even regulatory approval for certain hematological malignancies (Grayson et al., 2010). In ocular research, TSA has been employed in experimental models involving retinal pigment epithelial cell lines, such as ARPE-19, where its application has led to notable changes in the transcription of genes involved in inflammation, angiogenesis, and cellular stress responses (Hamid et al., 2021). Although TSA itself has not been approved for clinical use in any ocular disease, its ability to modify gene expression through HDAC inhibition has provided valuable insights into potential interventions for retinal degenerative conditions, including both wet and dry forms of AMD (Hamid et al., 2021; Falcão et al., 2025). Moreover, preliminary safety studies—such as those performed using topical formulations in animal models for corneal wound healing—have demonstrated that TSA can be applied at low nanomolar concentrations with minimal cytotoxicity, suggesting that careful dose titration could allow its repurposing for RPE-targeted therapies without incurring the severe systemic toxicities observed in some cancer studies (Sharma et al., 2009). However, it is important to note that despite the promising preclinical data in ARPE-19 cells and related ocular models, there remains a significant gap in clinical evidence directly linking TSA treatment with efficacy in dry AMD, meaning that its repurposing for this indication remains experimental and largely hypothesis-driven at this stage (Kaarniranta et al., 2023; Hamid et al., 2021).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, TSA is known to inhibit histone deacetylases by binding to the zinc ion in the enzyme’s catalytic pocket, thereby preventing the removal of acetyl groups from lysine residues on histone tails (Grayson et al., 2010; Hamid et al., 2021). In normal physiological conditions, HDACs contribute to the formation of tightly packed chromatin by deacetylating histones, which leads to decreased transcriptional activity at specific gene promoters. TSA’s ability to inhibit this process results in a “relaxed” chromatin structure that is more accessible to transcription factors, leading to enhanced gene expression (Grayson et al., 2010; Hamid et al., 2021). In the context of the RPE, several genes critical for autophagic flux and phagocytic function—such as lysosomal‐associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP1) and the receptor tyrosine kinase MERTK—are thought to be epigenetically regulated and may be transcriptionally upregulated upon HDAC inhibition (Boya et al., 2023; Falcão et al., 2025). Importantly, the transcription factor EB (TFEB) is recognized as a master regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy, controlling the expression of a suite of genes organized within the coordinated lysosomal expression and regulation (CLEAR) network (Falcão et al., 2025). In RPE cells, where efficient degradation of phagocytosed photoreceptor outer segments (POS) is dependent on both lysosomal function and autophagy, the upregulation of TFEB through enhanced chromatin accessibility could theoretically result in improved expression of LAMP1 and, by extension, better phagocytic capacity via receptors such as MERTK (Boya et al., 2023; Falcão et al., 2025). Moreover, TSA’s inhibition of HDACs also affects non-histone substrates; for example, it increases the acetylation of α-tubulin—a modification that can influence microtubule dynamics and vesicular transport processes (Grayson et al., 2010; Hamid et al., 2021). However, this action comes with a potential caveat: excessive tubulin acetylation has been reported to impair autophagosome trafficking, which is a critical step in the autophagic clearance pathway (Toops et al., 2015; Tan, 2018). Therefore, while TSA’s global HDAC inhibition can promote the transcription of genes essential for lysosomal biogenesis and POS degradation, careful titration of its concentration is necessary to ensure that the beneficial effects on gene transcription do not get overshadowed by disruptions in cytoskeletal dynamics and autophagosome motility (Grayson et al., 2010; Hamid et al., 2021). At the same time, TSA has been shown in some studies to downregulate pro-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic mediators such as HIF-1α and IL-1β while upregulating factors like IL-18, which may contribute additional neuroprotective benefits to the RPE (Hamid et al., 2021). Together, these multifaceted actions underline TSA’s potential to reprogram the epigenetic landscape of RPE cells, theoretically reinstating the expression of key genes involved in autophagic and phagocytic function (Boya et al., 2023; Hamid et al., 2021).

Expected Effect:  
Based on the working hypothesis, the expected effect of TSA in RPE cells is twofold. First, by inhibiting HDAC activity, TSA is anticipated to elevate overall chromatin accessibility, thereby stimulating the transcription of genes that are vital for proper lysosomal biogenesis and phagocytosis. In particular, the upregulation of LAMP1, a well-established marker of lysosomal membranes, should lead to an increase in the number and activity of lysosomes. Simultaneously, enhanced expression of MERTK, which plays a central role in mediating the binding and internalization of POS, should result in an improved phagocytic rate of these essential outer segment components (Boya et al., 2023). In RPE cells, where the daily burden of POS phagocytosis is critical for maintaining retinal integrity, such enhancements could potentially mitigate the accumulation of undigested cellular debris—a phenomenon that is closely linked with the progression of dry AMD (Falcão et al., 2025; Tan, 2018). Second, TSA is expected to promote the activation and nuclear translocation of TFEB. This transcription factor is normally sequestered in the cytoplasm when phosphorylated by mTORC1, but under conditions of stress or when mTORC1 activity is reduced, TFEB enters the nucleus and drives the expression of genes involved in lysosomal function and autophagy (Falcão et al., 2025). By facilitating an HDAC inhibition–mediated de-repression of the genes governing TFEB activity, TSA may help drive a feed-forward loop that enhances the cellular capacity for lysosomal degradation and autophagy. Given that RPE cells exhibit compromised autophagy and deteriorating lysosomal function in the pathogenesis of dry AMD, the net effect of TSA treatment should be an improvement in POS uptake and degradation, leading to better clearance of lipofuscin and other deleterious cellular debris (Garcia-Garcia et al., 2022; Falcão et al., 2025). Furthermore, some studies using ARPE-19 cells have reported TSA-induced changes in the expression of other oxidative stress and inflammatory markers—for example, a decrease in HIF-1α expression and a corresponding increase in antioxidant enzymes such as SOD2 and GPX3 (Hamid et al., 2021)—which may contribute to an overall protective environment that diminishes the progressive degeneration of RPE cells. The expected cascade of molecular events—from HDAC inhibition through increased histone acetylation, enhanced transcription of TFEB, LAMP1, and MERTK, to improved lysosomal biogenesis and phagocytic function—forms the basis of the therapeutic rationale for repurposing TSA in the context of dry AMD (Boya et al., 2023; Falcão et al., 2025).

Overall Evaluation:  
Evaluating TSA as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD requires an in-depth analysis of both its promising attributes and its inherent limitations. One of the primary strengths of TSA lies in its well-characterized mechanism of action. As a proven, potent pan-HDAC inhibitor, TSA can recast the epigenetic landscape of RPE cells by enforcing hyperacetylation of histone proteins and upregulating transcription of ordinarily silenced genes (Grayson et al., 2010; Hamid et al., 2021). Considering that dry AMD is marked by severe dysfunction in protein clearance, lysosomal degradation, and autophagic flux, TSA’s capacity to activate TFEB—the master regulator of lysosomal biogenesis—and to potentially enhance the expression of key phagocytic receptors such as MERTK and lysosomal markers like LAMP1 offers a compelling pharmacological strategy to restore cellular homeostasis (Boya et al., 2023; Falcão et al., 2025). The cascading effect of promoting both lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy could improve the clearance of photoreceptor outer segments (POS), thereby mitigating the accumulation of toxic by-products like lipofuscin that contribute significantly to the pathogenesis of dry AMD (Falcão et al., 2025; Tan, 2018).

Furthermore, TSA’s history as a research compound in various cell culture models, including ARPE-19, provides a solid preclinical foundation for its repurposing. Studies have demonstrated that TSA can modulate the expression of inflammatory and apoptotic markers, reduce oxidative stress, and, importantly, alter the expression of genes that control cellular clearance pathways (Hamid et al., 2021). These data support the hypothesis that TSA might improve RPE function, a critical factor given that RPE degeneration is a driving force behind AMD progression. Preliminary ocular safety assessments, particularly in corneal models, indicate that TSA can be administered topically at carefully controlled low doses without significant toxicity (Sharma et al., 2009). Such findings are encouraging in light of the longstanding challenges associated with delivering effective therapeutics to the posterior segment of the eye; however, it must be emphasized that safety profiles established in corneal applications may not fully translate to the intricate microenvironment of the retina and RPE (Kaarniranta et al., 2023).

On the other hand, several weaknesses temper the enthusiasm for TSA as a repurposed treatment for dry AMD. Foremost among these is its broad-spectrum activity; as a pan-HDAC inhibitor, TSA does not differentiate between enzyme isoforms. This lack of selectivity raises the possibility of off-target effects that could disrupt not only histone modifications but also the acetylation status of numerous non-histone proteins, including α-tubulin (Grayson et al., 2010; Hamid et al., 2021). In RPE cells, proper microtubule dynamics are essential for the trafficking of autophagosomes to lysosomes. Excessive tubulin acetylation—an effect that is purportedly enhanced by agents such as TSA, and observed in conditions mimicked by lipofuscin bisretinoids—can impede autophagosome mobility, potentially undermining the very autophagic flux that TSA is intended to improve (Toops et al., 2015; Tan, 2018; Toops et al., 2015). This paradoxical effect suggests that while increasing transcription of lysosomal genes is beneficial, alterations in cytoskeletal regulation might counteract these benefits if not precisely calibrated.

Another concern arises from the conflicting roles of HDAC activity in the context of AMD. Some studies have observed that specific HDAC activities, such as those mediated by HDAC3, are already reduced in the RPE of AMD patients (Chatterjee et al., 2024; Hamid et al., 2021). This raises the possibility that further inhibition of these enzymes might exacerbate a preexisting imbalance rather than re-establish homeostasis. In other words, if the pathological state of AMD is associated with diminished HDAC activity, then TSA’s further suppression could potentially lead to unintended and deleterious transcriptional changes that might compromise cell survival (Chatterjee et al., 2024; Hamid et al., 2021).

Moreover, despite encouraging in vitro data, there remains a paucity of information regarding TSA’s effects on the specific gene targets central to the proposed mechanism—namely, the upregulation of phagocytic receptors like MERTK and lysosomal membrane proteins such as LAMP1. While the rationale is built upon sound principles of epigenetic regulation and the critical role these proteins play in ensuring efficient POS uptake and degradation (Boya et al., 2023), direct evidence linking TSA treatment to enhanced expression of these markers in RPE cells is yet to be conclusively demonstrated. This gap in the literature makes it difficult to fully endorse TSA as having the predicted beneficial impact on RPE phagocytic function and lysosomal biogenesis without further, targeted investigations (Tan, 2018; Garcia-Garcia et al., 2022).

In addressing the translational potential of TSA, one must also consider its pharmacokinetic and toxicity profiles. Although ocular studies—particularly those focusing on corneal wound healing—have suggested that TSA is safe at low nanomolar concentrations (Sharma et al., 2009), its use in chronic conditions such as dry AMD would likely require long-term administration. The potential cumulative toxicity of TSA, given its broad activity and historical association with off-target effects in other tissues, remains a serious concern. The challenge will be to reconcile the need for sufficient HDAC inhibition to achieve therapeutic upregulation of autophagic and phagocytic genes with the imperative to minimize adverse side effects that could compromise either the safety or the efficacy of the treatment (Grayson et al., 2010; Hamid et al., 2021).

Overall, TSA is a compelling candidate for repurposing in dry AMD due to its robust epigenetic modulatory effects, which align well with the therapeutic goal of enhancing lysosomal biogenesis and phagocytic clearance in RPE cells. Its ability to interfere with HDAC activity has been demonstrated to affect not only histone acetylation but also the transcription of genes involved in cellular stress responses and degradation pathways. In theory, such modifications could reverse or mitigate several of the pathological features of dry AMD, including the accumulation of undigested POS and subsequent lipofuscin buildup that drive RPE degeneration (Boya et al., 2023; Falcão et al., 2025; Falcão et al., 2025). The elevation of TFEB, a master regulator of lysosomal gene expression, through TSA-mediated chromatin relaxation offers a particularly attractive avenue for restoring the autophagy-lysosome machinery, thereby enhancing both cellular clearance and the overall health of RPE cells (Falcão et al., 2025; Falcão et al., 2025).

Nonetheless, the candidate’s broad-spectrum nature and potential to disrupt processes such as microtubule dynamics present significant challenges. The risk of impaired autophagosome trafficking due to aberrant tubulin acetylation (Toops et al., 2015; Tan, 2018) must be weighed against the expected benefits of improved lysosomal function. Moreover, the heterogeneity of HDAC activity in AMD pathology—with some reports indicating already compromised HDAC function in diseased RPE—complicates the prospect of using a pan-HDAC inhibitor without a carefully defined therapeutic window (Chatterjee et al., 2024; Hamid et al., 2021). The absence of definitive data linking TSA treatment to increased expression of MERTK and LAMP1 in RPE cells further underscores the need for additional research to validate the proposed mechanism of action (Tan, 2018; Garcia-Garcia et al., 2022).

In light of these considerations, our evaluation supports the continued exploratory investigation of TSA as a repurposed intervention for dry AMD, with the understanding that several critical questions remain unanswered. Future studies should aim to:  
1. Directly measure the impact of TSA on the expression levels of key phagocytic and lysosomal genes, such as MERTK and LAMP1, in RPE cells using both transcriptional and protein-level assays.  
2. Delineate the dose-response relationship in RPE cells to identify concentrations that achieve optimal activation of the CLEAR network and TFEB-mediated transcription without inducing detrimental levels of tubulin acetylation that could impede autophagosome mobility.  
3. Assess the long-term safety and pharmacokinetics of TSA in ocular tissues, particularly in animal models of dry AMD, to determine whether its beneficial effects on autophagy and phagocytosis can be sustained without incurring off-target toxicity or systemic side effects.  
4. Explore the possibility of developing more selective HDAC inhibitors or targeted delivery systems that concentrate TSA’s effects specifically within the RPE, thereby minimizing the risks associated with its broad-spectrum activity.

Ultimately, while TSA’s established role as an epigenetic modulator makes it an attractive candidate for addressing the underlying cellular dysfunction in dry AMD, careful balancing of its beneficial transcriptional effects against its potential to disrupt critical cellular processes is essential. The data reviewed herein provide a strong mechanistic rationale for TSA’s utility, yet underscore the necessity for rigorously designed preclinical studies to confirm its efficacy and safety in the context of retinal degeneration (Grayson et al., 2010; Hamid et al., 2021; Boya et al., 2023; Falcão et al., 2025).
