Proposal for Nicotinamide Riboside (NR; ChromaDex TruNiagen)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Nicotinamide riboside (NR) is a naturally occurring derivative of vitamin B3 that was originally isolated from milk and later recognized for its role as an NAD⁺ precursor. NR belongs to the class of NAD⁺‐boosting compounds and is structurally a pyridine riboside with a glycosidic bond linking nicotinamide to ribose; this bond has proven to be chemically labile, which has driven innovations in its synthesis and stabilization (Zhang et al., 2020; Braidy et al., 2019). Recent advances in synthetic methods have enabled the production of purer, more thermally and chemically stable forms, such as borate-stabilized NR, although commercial formulations like NR chloride (ChromaDex TruNiagen) are widely used owing to their proven oral bioavailability (Biţă et al., 2023). As a member of the NAD⁺ precursor family that also includes nicotinamide and nicotinic acid, NR has been repurposed for its potential to restore intracellular NAD⁺ levels, a vital coenzyme in redox reactions and as a substrate for enzymes such as sirtuins and PARPs; these properties have been harnessed in research aimed at improving mitochondrial function and addressing age-related degeneration (Zhang et al., 2020; Cimaglia et al., 2020).

Therapeutic History:  
NR has a robust preclinical and early clinical history as a nutritional supplement with demonstrated efficacy in boosting NAD⁺ levels systemically. Prior biochemical studies have documented that oral NR supplementation raises circulating NAD⁺ and associated metabolites in both murine and human subjects, with repeated dosing regimens showing increases in NAD⁺ of up to 100% above baseline without causing adverse events such as flushing or liver toxicity—side effects that have been problematic with other niacin derivatives (Airhart et al., 2017; Conze et al., 2019). Furthermore, NR has been shown to ameliorate metabolic deficits in various disease models, including mouse models of diet-induced obesity, neurodegeneration, peripheral neuropathy, and even light-induced retinal degeneration, where systemic treatment with NR improved retinal outcomes in mice (Zhang et al., 2020; Biţă et al., 2023). Although NR has not been directly tested in clinical trials for dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) or in dedicated experiments on retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) phagocytosis, the accumulated evidence from related ocular studies and from the broader literature on NAD⁺ restoration suggests that its application in retinal degenerative conditions is plausible (Jadeja et al., 2020; Cimaglia et al., 2020).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, NR is rapidly taken up by cells and phosphorylated by nicotinamide riboside kinases (NRK1 and NRK2) to produce nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN), which is subsequently converted to NAD⁺ by NMN adenylyltransferases (NMNATs) (Zhang et al., 2020; Jadeja et al., 2020). NAD⁺ is a key metabolic hub that is required for essential cellular processes, including fueling mitochondrial ATP production via glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation, while also serving as a critical substrate for sirtuin deacetylases (notably SIRT1 and SIRT3) and poly(ADP-ribose) polymerases (PARPs) that mediate DNA repair and stress responses (Braidy et al., 2019; Biţă et al., 2023). In the context of the RPE, diminished NAD⁺ availability is linked to compromised mitochondrial bioenergetics, impaired autophagy/mitophagy, increased oxidative stress, and defective phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments (POS), all of which contribute to the etiology of dry AMD (Jadeja et al., 2020). NR supplementation is proposed to elevate intracellular NAD⁺ levels above the physiological threshold, thereby stimulating sirtuin activity (particularly SIRT3 in mitochondria), stabilizing mitochondrial membrane potential, promoting efficient mitophagy, and reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production (Cimaglia et al., 2020; Biţă et al., 2023). Additionally, by restoring the NAD⁺/NADH balance, NR may directly support energy-dependent processes such as phagocytosis in RPE cells, where the clearance of aged POS is critically energy demanding (Zhang et al., 2020).

Expected Effect:  
In the proposed hypothesis for dry AMD, exogenous NR supplementation is expected to boost NAD⁺ levels in RPE cells, an effect that should be observable at an effective concentration (EC₅₀) in the range of approximately 100 µM in vitro (Zhang et al., 2020). This increase in NAD⁺ is anticipated to enhance mitochondrial respiration and activate sirtuin-dependent pathways that promote mitochondrial biogenesis and repair. In turn, improved mitochondrial function provides the necessary ATP for energy-dependent cellular functions, particularly the phagocytic clearance of photoreceptor outer segments (POS), which is impaired in dry AMD. Preclinical studies have indicated that NAD⁺ precursors, including nicotinamide and NMN, can correct defects in mitochondrial respiration and restore normal phagocytic activity in RPE models; NR, being an orally bioavailable NAD⁺ precursor with favorable safety profiles, is predicted to act similarly by reducing oxidative stress–induced damage to the phagocytic machinery and enhancing mitophagy to clear damaged mitochondria (Cimaglia et al., 2020; Jadeja et al., 2020; Hawkins et al., 2024). In animal models of retinal degeneration, NR supplementation has been linked to improved functional outcomes, supporting the notion that similar mechanisms may be relevant in the context of dry AMD, where RPE dysfunction underlies photoreceptor degeneration (Zhang et al., 2020). Thus, the expected effect is a dual benefit in RPE cells: increased energy production to drive POS phagocytosis and enhanced resistance to oxidative damage through improved mitochondrial quality control, ultimately helping to preserve RPE function and slow dry AMD progression (Conze et al., 2019).

Overall Evaluation:  
NR is an attractive candidate for repurposing in dry AMD due to its unique combination of advantages. Among its strengths is its origin as a naturally occurring, orally bioavailable NAD⁺ precursor that has a long record of safe use in human nutritional studies; clinical trials have repeatedly demonstrated its ability to significantly elevate systemic NAD⁺ levels without dose-limiting adverse effects such as flushing or hepatotoxicity, which are common to other niacin derivatives (Airhart et al., 2017; Conze et al., 2019). Its mechanism of action is well understood at the biochemical level: by feeding into the NAD⁺ salvage pathway, NR supports key cellular functions including mitochondrial respiration, DNA repair, and stress signaling via sirtuins and PARPs—pathways that are critically compromised in the aging RPE and in dry AMD (Braidy et al., 2019; Biţă et al., 2023). Additionally, preclinical data have shown that NAD⁺ restoration via NR or related precursors can ameliorate mitochondrial dysfunction and improve phagocytic activity in retinal tissues, providing a plausible molecular rationale for its use in maintaining RPE health (Zhang et al., 2020).

On the other hand, there are significant challenges that must be acknowledged. Despite promising preclinical evidence in various models of retinal degeneration, direct application of NR in the context of dry AMD has not yet been rigorously evaluated in dedicated clinical trials, leaving a gap between the compelling mechanistic rationale and the demonstration of clinical efficacy (Jadeja et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2024). The concentration required (EC₅₀ ≈ 100 µM) and the extent of NR’s penetration into the RPE in the human eye remain to be fully elucidated; tissue-specific pharmacokinetics may differ from those observed in systemic circulation, and there remains the possibility that presystemic metabolism (conversion to nicotinamide, for example) could limit the amount of NR available for direct uptake by the RPE (Damgaard & Treebak, 2023; Mehmel et al., 2020). Furthermore, while the drug’s safety profile is established in the context of metabolic and neurodegenerative indications, the unique microenvironment of the retina—with its high oxygen consumption and exposure to light-induced oxidative stress—may present unforeseen responses that require careful validation in animal models and early-phase clinical studies (Gemae et al., 2024; Jadeja et al., 2020).

In summary, NR (ChromaDex TruNiagen) holds considerable promise as a repurposed therapeutic for dry age-related macular degeneration. Its ability to safely boost NAD⁺ levels, improve mitochondrial function, and reduce oxidative stress aligns well with the pathophysiological needs of the RPE in dry AMD. Strengths of the candidate include its natural origin, established safety and bioavailability profiles in humans, and robust preclinical evidence supporting NAD⁺ repletion as a strategy for enhancing mitochondrial quality and phagocytic function. However, the absence of direct clinical trials in dry AMD and the need for targeted studies to confirm effective RPE delivery and functional restoration of POS clearance remain important considerations. Overall, NR merits further investigation in preclinical RPE models and carefully designed clinical trials to assess its efficacy in mitigating the progression of dry AMD (Zhang et al., 2020; Cimaglia et al., 2020; Airhart et al., 2017; Conze et al., 2019; Jadeja et al., 2020).
