Proposal for Nicotinamide (Vitamin B3; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. N4126)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Nicotinamide, a water‐soluble form of vitamin B3, is a naturally occurring compound that plays a critical role in cellular metabolism by serving as a precursor for nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD⁺) synthesis. It is available both through dietary sources and chemical synthesis and has been produced at high purity for research purposes (Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. N4126). Nicotinamide belongs to the vitamin B3 class of compounds, which have been extensively used as nutritional supplements and metabolic regulators; these compounds are well recognized for their role in the salvage pathway that replenishes NAD⁺ levels in cells. The therapeutic candidate under consideration is repurposed with the hypothesis that at a concentration of 10 mM, nicotinamide will boost intracellular NAD⁺ pools and enhance the activity of NAD⁺‐dependent enzymes, such as sirtuins, leading to improved mitochondrial function and increased deacetylation of autophagy regulators. Such effects are expected to restore phagocytic function, specifically the photoreceptor outer segment (POS) degradation, in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cultures pertinent to dry age‐related macular degeneration (Gemae et al., 2024; Jadeja et al., 2018).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, nicotinamide has been investigated in various preclinical and clinical settings for its potential neuroprotective effects and its capacity to modulate cellular metabolism. In the context of ocular health, several studies have demonstrated that nicotinamide supplementation can enhance NAD⁺ levels and improve mitochondrial function in retinal cells. Preclinical studies have reported that this vitamin not only supports retinal ganglion cell health in glaucoma models but also shows promise in improving photoreceptor and RPE function in models of age-related macular degeneration (Gemae et al., 2024). Although there remains limited direct clinical experience with nicotinamide in dry AMD, the compound has been a subject of interest in related ocular conditions due to its favorable safety profile and widespread use in other age-related and mitochondrial dysfunction settings (Gemae et al., 2024). More recent investigations have focused on its ability to rescue mitochondrial deficits and restore autophagic flux in RPE cells, with data indicating that in models of CFH Y402H iPSC-RPE, nicotinamide can ameliorate lysosomal processing and POS degradation problems (Gemae et al., 2024; Jadeja et al., 2018). This body of evidence, compounded with its extensive historical use as a nutritional supplement, supports the rationale for repurposing nicotinamide as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD (Gemae et al., 2024).

Mechanism of Action:  
At a molecular level, nicotinamide exerts its effects primarily through its role as a key precursor in the NAD⁺ salvage pathway. In this pathway, nicotinamide is converted to nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) via the enzyme nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT), and subsequently to NAD⁺ by NMN adenylyltransferase. NAD⁺, in turn, is an essential co-factor for numerous enzymes involved in energy metabolism and cellular homeostasis, most notably the sirtuin family of NAD⁺-dependent deacetylases (Gemae et al., 2024; Jadeja et al., 2018). Sirtuins, such as SIRT1 and SIRT3, play a pivotal role in mitochondrial biogenesis, oxidative phosphorylation, and regulation of autophagy by deacetylating key transcription factors and enzymes that control metabolic stress responses (Gemae et al., 2024). In RPE cells, increased NAD⁺ levels facilitate the activation of these sirtuins, which then deacetylate a range of targets including histones and autophagy regulators, leading to improved mitochondrial structure and function, enhanced energy production, and the promotion of efficient autophagic and lysosomal degradation pathways (Gemae et al., 2024). Furthermore, the improved mitochondrial function translates into a better cellular energy state, providing the necessary ATP required for high-energy processes such as POS phagocytosis. Experimentally, administration of nicotinamide has been shown to yield a two-fold increase in NAD⁺ levels in ARPE-19 cells, thereby confirming its ability to augment the cellular bioenergetic capacity (Hazim et al., 2022). This mechanism also intersects with the regulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, as enhanced mitochondrial function and sirtuin activity contribute to lower oxidative stress, a known contributor to cellular senescence and dysfunction in the aging retina (Gemae et al., 2024).

Expected Effect:  
The central hypothesis driving the current proposal is that nicotinamide, administered at a concentration of 10 mM in vitro, will significantly increase NAD⁺ levels in RPE cells, thereby enhancing sirtuin activity and downstream mitochondrial function. This boost in NAD⁺ is expected to promote deacetylation of autophagy regulators, which will, in turn, normalize the autophagic flux and facilitate the effective degradation of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) by the RPE. In models of dry AMD, where phagocytosis deficits and impaired proteostasis are key pathological features, the restoration of autophagy and mitochondrial activity is anticipated to ameliorate cellular senescence and maintain retinal homeostasis (Gemae et al., 2024; Jadeja et al., 2018). Data from previous studies suggest that in aging RPE, reduced NAMPT expression is linked to decreased NAD⁺ levels and compromised SIRT1 function, leading to cellular senescence and defective mitochondrial dynamics (Jadeja et al., 2018). By contrast, nicotinamide supplementation helps to reverse these effects, as evidenced by restored NAD⁺ content and reactivation of sirtuins that enable degradation of accumulated defective cellular components, thereby improving lysosomal enzyme processing and phagocytosis (Jadeja et al., 2018). Moreover, enhanced mitochondrial metabolism has been correlated with improved RPE differentiation and the maintenance of a polarized epithelial phenotype, critical factors in ensuring effective POS phagocytosis and overall retinal health (Hazim et al., 2022). Thus, the expected outcome is that nicotinamide treatment will correct metabolic impairments, reduce oxidative stress, and restore phagocytic efficiency in dry AMD cultures, which are all essential for halting or even reversing disease progression (Gemae et al., 2024; Jadeja et al., 2018).

Overall Evaluation:  
Nicotinamide emerges as a highly promising therapeutic candidate for the treatment of dry AMD based on its multi-faceted mechanism of action and extensive preclinical evidence supporting its role in maintaining retinal health. One of the primary strengths of nicotinamide is its excellent safety profile and widespread historical use as a nutritional supplement, which significantly lowers the risk associated with its repurposing (Gemae et al., 2024). Its ability to augment NAD⁺ levels and activate sirtuins directly addresses several key pathological processes in dry AMD, including mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and impaired autophagy. Experimental findings have consistently shown that increased NAD⁺ levels, mediated by nicotinamide or its analogues such as NMN, correlate with reduced cellular senescence and improved lysosomal and mitochondrial function in RPE cells (Jadeja et al., 2018).

Moreover, the convergence of multiple lines of evidence—from studies detailing NAMPT decline in aging RPE, to those demonstrating the restoration of NAD⁺ and sirtuin activity by nicotinamide—provides a robust biochemical rationale for its use in combating dry AMD (Gemae et al., 2024). The ability of nicotinamide to enhance mitochondrial respiration in ARPE-19 cells and primary human fetal RPE cells further underscores its potential in promoting a healthy, polarized RPE phenotype capable of efficient POS phagocytosis (Hazim et al., 2022). These studies also suggest that improved mitochondrial metabolism is closely linked to enhanced proteostasis and autophagic clearance of damaged cellular components—an outcome that is particularly relevant given the known deficits in autophagy and lysosomal processing observed in dry AMD (Gemae et al., 2024).

However, while the preclinical data are encouraging, several challenges must be acknowledged. One potential weakness lies in the translation of in vitro findings to in vivo therapeutic efficacy. The concentration of 10 mM nicotinamide used in cell culture experiments may not directly correspond to achievable or sustainable therapeutic levels in the human retina. Moreover, while nicotinamide is known to boost NAD⁺ synthesis, there is a nuanced balance in its effects on sirtuin activity; in certain contexts, excessive nicotinamide has been reported to inhibit sirtuins, suggesting that dosing and timing will be critical parameters that require careful optimization (Will-Orrego, 2020). There is also the inherent challenge of the multifactorial nature of dry AMD, where mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired autophagy are only parts of a complex network of pathological events that include inflammatory signaling, extracellular matrix alterations, and genetic predispositions. Thus, while nicotinamide addresses several of these pathways, it may need to be combined with other therapeutic agents to achieve a comprehensive treatment effect (Gemae et al., 2024; Will-Orrego, 2020).

Another important consideration is the need for more extensive clinical studies. Although nicotinamide’s safety profile is well established in the context of dietary supplementation and its use in other neurological and ocular conditions, robust clinical trials specifically targeting dry AMD are lacking (Gemae et al., 2024; Lin, 2020). Future research will need to focus not only on the pharmacokinetics and optimal dosing strategies in human subjects but also on precise biomarkers indicating the restoration of NAD⁺ metabolism, mitochondrial function, and POS phagocytosis. Furthermore, mechanistic studies that dissect the interactions between nicotinamide-driven NAD⁺ elevation and the downstream modulation of autophagy regulators will be essential to confirm the proposed mode of action and to identify any potential off-target effects.

Overall, the repurposing of nicotinamide for dry AMD presents several compelling advantages. Its established safety, low cost, and known biochemical mechanisms strongly support its potential as a therapeutic candidate. Preclinical evidence indicates that nicotinamide not only restores NAD⁺ levels but also reactivates sirtuin pathways, thereby mitigating mitochondrial dysfunction and defective autophagy that are central to the pathogenesis of dry AMD. These effects could translate into improved cellular proteostasis and enhanced POS phagocytosis, ultimately preserving retinal integrity and visual function (Gemae et al., 2024; Jadeja et al., 2018). Nevertheless, the path forward will require careful dose optimization and rigorous clinical validation to ensure that the in vitro benefits observed translate into meaningful clinical outcomes. In summary, despite the challenges associated with translating cell-based results into human therapy, nicotinamide remains an attractive candidate whose mechanism, historical use, and preclinical successes justify further investigation as a potential treatment for dry AMD (Jadeja et al., 2018; Lin, 2020).
