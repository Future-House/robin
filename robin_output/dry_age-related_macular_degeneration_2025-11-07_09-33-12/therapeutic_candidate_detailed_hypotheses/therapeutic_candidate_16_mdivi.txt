Proposal for Mdivi-1 (DRP1 inhibitor; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. M0199)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Mdivi-1 is a small molecule pharmacological agent originally discovered through high-throughput chemical screening in yeast models and subsequently characterized in mammalian systems as an inhibitor of mitochondrial fission. It is a quinazolinone derivative that belongs to a class of compounds targeting mitochondrial dynamics, with the specific intent of modulating the activity of dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1) (Cassidy-Stone et al., 2008; Smith & Gallo, 2017). DRP1 is a large GTPase that mediates mitochondrial fission by undergoing self-assembly at constriction sites on the outer mitochondrial membrane and catalyzing membrane scission; under physiological conditions, this process is balanced by mitochondrial fusion activities to maintain mitochondrial morphology, bioenergetics, and quality control. Mdivi-1 was synthesized and later commercialized (e.g., Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. M0199) because of its ability to selectively inhibit DRP1 activity, thereby shifting the balance away from excessive mitochondrial division and promoting a more interconnected, tubular mitochondrial network (Rosdah et al., 2016; Cassidy-Stone et al., 2008). This compound’s origins as a research tool in basic mitochondrial biology have led to its investigation in various disease models ranging from neurodegenerative conditions and cardiovascular ischemia to ocular disorders, where mitochondrial dysfunction is known to play a critical role. Its repurposing for dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is of particular interest because of the emerging evidence linking chronic oxidative stress, mitochondrial fragmentation, and RPE (retinal pigment epithelium) dysfunction to AMD pathology (Yako et al., 2021; Somasundaran et al., 2020).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, Mdivi-1 has been extensively used in preclinical studies to interrogate the role of excessive mitochondrial fission in diverse pathological conditions. Initial seminal work demonstrated that inhibition of DRP1 activity by Mdivi-1 could prevent mitochondrial fragmentation, reduce cytochrome c release, and attenuate apoptosis in yeast and mammalian cell models (Cassidy-Stone et al., 2008; Rosdah et al., 2016). Following these foundational observations, several studies explored its utility in neuronal and cardiovascular systems. For example, preclinical investigations in models of ischemia-reperfusion injury, myocardial infarction, and neurodegeneration revealed that Mdivi-1 treatment increased cell survival and improved functional outcomes by preserving mitochondrial structure (Chen et al., 2023; Park et al., 2011).  

In the ocular domain, Mdivi-1 has been tested in models of retinal ischemia, glaucoma, and retinal detachment. Studies have shown that Mdivi-1 can increase the survival of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) in settings of acute ischemic injury, and its administration in retinal detachment models improved photoreceptor survival by mitigating DRP1-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction (She et al., 2018; Park et al., 2011). Moreover, within RPE-specific research, there is evidence from ARPE-19 cell studies that Mdivi-1 can counteract H₂O₂-induced oxidative damage by preventing mitochondrial fragmentation and preserving mitochondrial membrane potential, thereby protecting cells from apoptosis (Yako et al., 2021). Although the majority of these studies have focused on acute oxidative stress paradigms, the use of Mdivi-1 in these diverse retinal models provides a strong rationale to explore its potential in treating dry AMD, a condition characterized by chronic RPE stress, impaired photoreceptor outer segment (POS) phagocytosis, and progressive mitochondrial dysfunction (Yako et al., 2021).

Mechanism of Action:  
The molecular mechanism of Mdivi-1 centers on its inhibitory effect on DRP1, a critical regulator of mitochondrial fission. In a normally functioning cell, DRP1 resides in the cytosol and, upon activation—often triggered by cellular stress or proliferative signals—it translocates to the outer mitochondrial membrane. There, DRP1 self-assembles into oligomeric complexes that hydrolyze GTP to execute the fission process (Cassidy-Stone et al., 2008; Rosdah et al., 2016). Mdivi-1 acts by binding to an allosteric site on DRP1, thereby inhibiting its GTPase activity and preventing the oligomerization necessary for mitochondrial membrane constriction (Rosdah et al., 2016; Smith & Gallo, 2017). This inhibition not only prevents the excessive fragmentation of mitochondria under conditions of oxidative stress but also stabilizes mitochondrial membrane potential and curbs the release of pro-apoptotic factors such as cytochrome c (Yako et al., 2021).

At the biochemical level, studies using ARPE-19 cells have shown that Mdivi-1 treatment correlates with a significant reduction in markers of mitochondrial dysfunction—such as decreased cytochrome c leakage and improved maintenance of mitochondrial membrane potential—even when the cells are challenged with oxidative insults like H₂O₂ (Yako et al., 2021). These protective effects are central to the proposed hypothesis for dry AMD, where preservation of mitochondrial function in RPE is paramount for sustaining ATP production and supporting energy-demanding processes like POS phagocytosis.

It is important to note, however, that emerging evidence suggests that Mdivi-1’s actions may not be limited solely to DRP1 inhibition. Several studies have reported that Mdivi-1 also acts as a reversible inhibitor of mitochondrial Complex I of the electron transport chain (Marx et al., 2024; Avilan, 2023). This off-target effect can modulate reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and potentially influence cellular bioenergetics in a manner that might either contribute beneficially by reducing oxidative stress or detrimentally by impairing ATP production if the inhibition of Complex I is too pronounced. Such dual activity necessitates careful titration of Mdivi-1 dosing to ensure that the beneficial effects of DRP1 inhibition are not overshadowed by unwanted metabolic interference. Additionally, post-translational modifications of DRP1, such as phosphorylation at serine residues 616 and 637, modulate its activity and may influence the sensitivity of DRP1 to inhibition by Mdivi-1 in different cellular contexts (Yako et al., 2021). This complexity underscores the need for a thorough understanding of Mdivi-1’s molecular interactions in RPE cells and how these might translate to functional benefits in conditions like dry AMD.

Expected Effect:  
The central hypothesis guiding the proposed therapeutic application of Mdivi-1 in dry AMD is that by inhibiting excessive DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission, the drug will preserve the integrity of the mitochondrial network in RPE cells. This preservation is critical because RPE cells rely heavily on mitochondria to generate ATP, which in turn is essential for energy-dependent processes such as the phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) (Yako et al., 2021). Under oxidative stress conditions—conditions that mimic the chronic environmental challenges faced by the RPE in dry AMD—excessive mitochondrial fragmentation leads to diminished ATP production and impaired phagocytic function. In vitro studies using ARPE-19 cells have provided evidence that treatment with Mdivi-1 can normalize mitochondrial morphology, preserve mitochondrial membrane potential, and reduce the release of cytochrome c, all of which contribute to maintaining cellular ATP levels (Yako et al., 2021).

In the context of the proposed assay, Mdivi-1 is expected to act at an effective concentration—hypothetically around an IC₅₀ of ~1 µM—to inhibit excessive mitochondrial fission in stressed RPE cells. By stabilizing mitochondrial dynamics, RPE cells should be able to sustain adequate ATP production even under conditions of oxidative challenge, thereby directly supporting the energy-demanding process of POS phagocytosis. This is critical because efficient phagocytosis is a cornerstone of RPE function; failure to clear shed photoreceptor debris leads to the formation of drusen and the accumulation of lipofuscin, both hallmark features of dry AMD pathology (Yako et al., 2021).

Moreover, improved mitochondrial function should also contribute to the normalization of lysosomal pH—a factor that is important for the proper degradation of phagocytosed material. Together, these effects are anticipated to not only preserve cellular viability in the face of chronic oxidative stress but also enhance the functional capacity of the RPE to process photoreceptor debris, which in turn could slow or halt the progression of dry AMD. However, it is crucial to achieve a delicate balance in dosing; while low concentrations might yield the desired protective effects by selectively inhibiting DRP1, higher concentrations run the risk of triggering off-target Complex I inhibition, with a subsequent decrease in ATP production that would be counterproductive in an energy-deficient cell (Marx et al., 2024; Avilan, 2023). Thus, while the expected effect is highly promising, it is contingent upon precise dose optimization and careful validation in both acute and chronic RPE models.

Overall Evaluation:  
Taken together, the literature supports the potential of Mdivi-1 as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD, predicated on its capacity to inhibit DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission and preserve mitochondrial network integrity in RPE cells. The compound’s strength lies in its well-documented ability to prevent mitochondrial fragmentation, maintain mitochondrial membrane potential, and reduce apoptotic signaling under conditions of oxidative stress, which are all pivotal factors in the pathogenesis of dry AMD (Yako et al., 2021). Preclinical studies in various retinal and neurodegenerative models further bolster the case for its broader applicability in ocular diseases; evidence from glaucoma and retinal detachment models, as well as in ARPE-19 cell studies, suggests that modulating mitochondrial dynamics can offer significant neuroprotective benefits (Cueva-Vargas et al., 2025; Park et al., 2011; She et al., 2018).

Nonetheless, several weaknesses temper the enthusiasm for Mdivi-1’s clinical translation in dry AMD. One of the primary concerns is the duality in its mechanism of action. Although Mdivi-1 is primarily characterized as a DRP1 inhibitor, multiple studies have raised valid concerns regarding its off-target effects, notably its inhibition of mitochondrial Complex I at higher doses (Marx et al., 2024; Avilan, 2023). This is particularly significant in the context of RPE cells, which are highly dependent on robust mitochondrial function and efficient ATP production to sustain their phagocytic and metabolic activities. Inadvertent inhibition of Complex I could lead to decreased ATP synthesis and an accumulation of ROS, thereby exacerbating rather than alleviating cellular dysfunction—an outcome that would be highly undesirable in a disease like dry AMD.

Another key issue is the apparent variability in effective dosing. While some studies report cytoprotective effects at concentrations around 1–10 µM, others have employed doses up to 50 µM to observe meaningful modulatory effects on mitochondrial dynamics (Yako et al., 2021). Such variability suggests that the effective therapeutic window might be narrow, and that even minor deviations in dosing could result in suboptimal efficacy or unintended toxicity. Notably, some in vitro studies using ARPE-19 cells have demonstrated that higher concentrations of Mdivi-1 can lead to cytotoxicity, raising concerns about its safety profile for long-term use in the retina (Shulman et al., 2010).

Additionally, while the hypothesis underlying the current proposal—that inhibition of excessive mitochondrial fission can restore ATP production and thereby improve POS phagocytosis—is mechanistically sound, direct evidence linking Mdivi-1 treatment to enhanced phagocytic function in RPE cells remains somewhat preliminary. Most of the existing literature has focused on acute protective effects in response to oxidative stress rather than on the chronic functional deficits observed in dry AMD (Yako et al., 2021). This gap in the literature signals a need for more targeted studies that evaluate not only the biochemical and morphological endpoints but also the functional outcomes, such as phagocytotic efficiency, in models that closely mimic the chronic nature of AMD.

Furthermore, the complex intracellular milieu of the RPE—characterized by high metabolic demand, ongoing phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments, and a finely balanced process of autophagy and lysosomal degradation—means that interventions must be precisely calibrated. Chronic inhibition of mitochondrial fission, if not tightly controlled, could disrupt normal mitophagy (the selective removal of damaged mitochondria) and impair cellular turnover processes, potentially leading to the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria over time (Lin et al., 2024; Rosdah et al., 2016). Therefore, while the short-term benefits of Mdivi-1 are encouraging, its long-term impact on overall mitochondrial quality control and cellular homeostasis in RPE cells remains an open question.

From a translational standpoint, Mdivi-1’s established use in several preclinical ocular models is a significant strength, as it facilitates the leveraging of existing data and methodologies in further studies designed to address dry AMD specifically (Cueva-Vargas et al., 2025; Park et al., 2011; She et al., 2018). Moreover, the absence of current effective treatments for dry AMD underscores the urgency of pursuing novel therapeutic approaches that target its underlying pathophysiology. In this regard, the idea of preserving mitochondrial integrity to support energy-dependent processes like photoreceptor outer segment phagocytosis is particularly compelling (Yako et al., 2021).

In conclusion, while Mdivi-1 exhibits several attractive features as a candidate for dry AMD therapy—including the ability to modulate mitochondrial dynamics, preserve ATP production, and reduce oxidative stress—the existing body of evidence also highlights critical challenges that must be overcome. These include resolving the controversy surrounding its off-target effects on Complex I, establishing a reliable and safe dosing regimen, and generating direct evidence linking its use to functional improvements in RPE phagocytosis in models that accurately reflect the chronic nature of dry AMD (Yako et al., 2021; Marx et al., 2024; Avilan, 2023). Provided that these challenges are addressed through rigorous preclinical studies and careful dose optimization, Mdivi-1 remains a promising candidate for repurposing in the treatment of dry AMD.

Overall, our evaluation indicates that Mdivi-1 holds considerable potential as a therapeutic agent for dry age-related macular degeneration by targeting the mitochondrial dysfunction that is central to RPE pathology. Its ability to inhibit DRP1 and thereby reduce oxidative stress-induced mitochondrial fragmentation could preserve critical cellular functions such as ATP production and photoreceptor outer segment phagocytosis, offering a novel mechanism of action to address this currently untreatable condition. However, the possibility of off-target Complex I inhibition and dose-dependent cytotoxicity underscore the need for further detailed mechanistic studies and dose-ranging experiments in both in vitro and in vivo models that better simulate the chronic progression of dry AMD. With these challenges appropriately managed, Mdivi-1 could potentially become an innovative mitochondrial-based therapeutic for a disease desperately in need of effective intervention (Yako et al., 2021; Marx et al., 2024; Avilan, 2023).
