Proposal for Mini-FH (Recombinant FH CCP1-4/19-20; Tel-Aviv University research reagent)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Mini‐FH is a rationally engineered, recombinant protein therapeutic derived from human complement factor H (FH) by selectively fusing its N-terminal complement control protein (CCP) modules 1–4 with its C-terminal CCP modules 19–20 (Kamala et al., 2021; de Boer et al., 2020). This minimal construct is synthesized through recombinant DNA methodologies that allow for expression in suitable heterologous systems such as yeast, insect cells, or mammalian cell lines; its design takes full advantage of recent advances in protein engineering guided by high‐resolution structural data (Dobo et al., 2018; Makou et al., 2013). Belonging to the class of engineered biologics—or “modular complement regulators”—Mini‐FH represents a more manufacturable, compact alternative to full‐length FH (≈155 kDa), reducing the molecular weight to roughly 40–45 kDa, which is expected to facilitate improved tissue and cellular penetration (Kamala et al., 2021; de Boer et al., 2020). This therapeutic candidate is designed to recapitulate the dual functionality of native FH: the CCP1–4 region provides regulatory activity through decay‐accelerating and cofactor functions against the alternative pathway (AP) C3 convertase, while the CCP19–20 region confers selective binding to surface‐associated ligands, including C3b/C3d and glycosaminoglycans, thereby anchoring the inhibitor to host membranes (Merle et al., 2015; Biggs et al., 2022). In essence, Mini‐FH is synthesized as a fusion protein that retains critical complement regulatory activities in a smaller, more agile molecule; this design underscores a broader trend in drug development toward developing “miniaturized,” high‐potency biologics that can overcome limitations of full‐length proteins in terms of tissue distribution and immunogenicity (Dobo et al., 2018; Makou et al., 2013).

Therapeutic History:  
The use of complement regulators as therapeutic agents has a long history in clinical research, particularly for diseases driven by dysregulation of the AP of complement. Engineered FH fragments like Mini‐FH have been the subject of extensive biochemical and preclinical evaluation, with studies demonstrating that constructs consisting of CCP1–4/19–20 exhibit enhanced binding to C3 fragments and improved decay‐accelerating activity compared to native FH on a per‐molecule basis (Kamala et al., 2021; de Boer et al., 2020). Preclinical in vitro assays, such as surface plasmon resonance (SPR) and hemolytic assays, have confirmed that Mini‐FH binds C3b with high affinity and competitively inhibits AP convertase formation by both displacing factor B and accelerating convertase decay (Dobo et al., 2018; Biggs et al., 2022). Moreover, animal studies, including those involving FH‐deficient mice, have provided compelling evidence for the in vivo activity of such engineered constructs, wherein administration of Mini‐FH or homodimeric variants (e.g., HDM-FH) reduced complement deposition and ameliorated tissue pathology in models of complement‐mediated kidney disease (de Boer et al., 2020; Kamala et al., 2021). Although direct clinical trials of Mini‐FH specifically for dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) have not yet been reported, related compounds such as full‐length recombinant FH (GEM103) have entered phase-2 clinical testing for AMD, thereby validating the concept of using FH‐based molecules to restore complement homeostasis in ocular diseases (Kamala et al., 2021; Dobo et al., 2018). Additionally, engineered versions of FH, including dimerized variants designed to enhance serum half-life and tissue binding, have shown promising preclinical efficacy in non-ocular models such as paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH) and C3 glomerulopathy, further suggesting that the underlying mechanism is broadly applicable to complement‐mediated disorders (de Boer et al., 2020; Biggs et al., 2022).

Mechanism of Action:  
The mechanism of action of Mini‐FH relies on its ability to simultaneously exploit the dual functionalities of FH through its modular design. The N-terminal CCP1–4 domain comprises the core regulatory region that mediates AP control by binding directly to C3b. This interaction competitively prevents the binding of factor B (FB), thereby inhibiting the formation of the pro-convertase C3bB complex and effectively blocking the assembly of the C3 convertase (C3bBb) (Merle et al., 2015; Kamala et al., 2021). In addition, CCP1–4 exerts decay-accelerating activity by accelerating the dissociation of Bb from the C3bBb complex; it also provides a binding platform for factor I (FI), thereby functioning as a cofactor that promotes the proteolytic cleavage of C3b to inactive iC3b (Kamala et al., 2021; de Boer et al., 2020). Complementing this, the C-terminal CCP19–20 domains of Mini‐FH are involved in high-affinity ligand recognition. These domains bind to the thioester-containing domain (TED) of C3b as well as to C3d fragments, which are present at sites of previous complement activation, and they interact with glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) and sialic acid residues on host cell surfaces (Merle et al., 2015; Biggs et al., 2022). This binding not only increases the overall avidity of the interaction by engaging multiple sites on a single C3b molecule but also serves to target the regulatory activity specifically to host surfaces that are decorated with complement activation products—a critical factor in distinguishing self from non-self (Dobo et al., 2018; Makou et al., 2013). Furthermore, by engaging in a dual binding mode, Mini‐FH effectively “bridges” the regulatory and recognition functions that in full-length FH are partially masked by the intervening CCP modules, thus resulting in a higher potency per molecule (Kamala et al., 2021; Dobo et al., 2018). Structural studies, including crystallographic analysis of complexes such as C3b:Mini-FH:FI, substantiate that Mini-FH engages two spatially distinct sites on C3b and even recruits FI through additional contacts involving CCP2–3, which further enhances its complement inhibitory function (Biggs et al., 2022). The overall consequence of these molecular interactions is the efficient disruption of the AP amplification loop, thereby reducing the cascade of complement activation products and ultimately limiting tissue damage driven by uncontrolled complement activity (Kamala et al., 2021; Dobo et al., 2018).

Expected Effect:  
In the context of dry AMD, the hypothesis posits that Mini-FH will competitively bind to C3b deposited on the surfaces of retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells and within Bruch’s membrane, specifically restoring local complement regulation by the alternative pathway (Kamala et al., 2021; de Boer et al., 2020). The expected mechanism is that, upon intravitreal administration, Mini-FH will localize to the subretinal space where excessive complement activation is known to occur as a result of FH deficiency or dysfunction associated with the Y402H polymorphism. Once there, the CCP1–4 segment of Mini-FH is anticipated to exert decay-accelerating and cofactor activities that promote the inactivation of C3b—inhibiting the formation and persistence of the C3 convertase (C3bBb) and thereby interrupting the amplification of the cascade (de Boer et al., 2020; Dobo et al., 2018). This biochemical activity is expected to result in a reduction of downstream complement components such as C5b–9 (the membrane attack complex) and inflammatory mediators like C3a and C5a, which are implicated in the chronic inflammation observed in dry AMD (Kamala et al., 2021; Dobo et al., 2018).

From a cellular perspective, by limiting complement activation on RPE surfaces, Mini-FH is expected to reduce the formation and accumulation of drusen-like deposits—extracellular aggregates that have been associated with inappropriate complement activity and are a hallmark of AMD pathology (Dobo et al., 2018; Makou et al., 2013). In addition, the engagement of CCP19–20 with surface-bound C3b/C3d and GAGs should enhance the targeted accumulation of Mini-FH on the RPE and in Bruch’s membrane, areas that are known hotspots for complement dysregulation in AMD (Merle et al., 2015; Biggs et al., 2022). This targeted binding not only localizes the regulatory function where it is most needed but may also facilitate secondary beneficial effects; for example, by mitigating complement-mediated cytotoxicity, Mini-FH may indirectly improve the phagocytic function of the RPE. Efficient post-phagocytosis is critical for the daily clearance of photoreceptor outer segments (POS), and disruption of this process is a contributing factor in the progression of AMD (Kamala et al., 2021; Dobo et al., 2018). Preclinical experiments in RPE explant models or murine AMD models that have been exposed to Mini-FH are thus expected to demonstrate a decrease in C3 deposition and complement activation markers, accompanied by restoration of normal RPE phagocytic capacity (de Boer et al., 2020; Dobo et al., 2018). Moreover, due to its relatively small size when compared to full-length FH, Mini-FH is predicted to have superior tissue penetration, which is essential for reaching the deeper layers of Bruch’s membrane and the subretinal space, where the pathological complement activation predominantly occurs (Kamala et al., 2021; Dobo et al., 2018).

Overall Evaluation:  
In evaluating the potential of Mini-FH (Recombinant FH CCP1–4/19–20) as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD, several key strengths and challenges emerge. One of the primary strengths of Mini-FH is its rational design based on a deep understanding of FH architecture and function. By incorporating the most critical regulatory and recognition domains into a single, compact molecule, Mini-FH offers enhanced potency on a per-molecule basis and efficient competitive binding to C3b. The enhanced decay-accelerating activity and ability to serve as a cofactor for FI-mediated C3b inactivation have been well documented in preclinical assays, including SPR and hemolysis studies (Kamala et al., 2021; de Boer et al., 2020). These features are especially promising given that dysregulated complement activation is a central element of AMD pathogenesis. Furthermore, Mini-FH’s smaller size relative to full-length FH suggests improved penetration into ocular tissues such as Bruch’s membrane and the subretinal space, where therapeutic intervention is most needed (Dobo et al., 2018; de Boer et al., 2020).

Another significant strength is the translational rationale supported by biochemical and preclinical animal studies. Engineered FH fragments, including Mini-FH variants, have demonstrated efficacy in reducing complement activity in murine models of complement dysregulation, such as C3 glomerulopathy and PNH, which supports the concept that restoring control over the AP can ameliorate tissue damage (Kamala et al., 2021; Biggs et al., 2022). The fact that full-length recombinant FH (GEM103) is already being evaluated clinically for AMD further bolsters the therapeutic validity of targeting the complement cascade in ocular diseases (Kamala et al., 2021; Dobo et al., 2018). Moreover, the diverse strategies employed in the development of similar biologics (e.g., dimerized forms of Mini-FH or fusion constructs with additional targeting domains) point to an active area of research and innovation that is likely to yield clinically effective solutions (Makou et al., 2013; Dobo et al., 2018).

Nevertheless, several weaknesses and challenges warrant careful consideration. Although Mini-FH has demonstrated robust in vitro complement regulatory activity, its reduced size relative to the full-length molecule may also come with a trade-off, such as a potential reduction in FI cofactor activity under certain conditions (de Boer et al., 2020; Kamala et al., 2021). This diminished cofactor activity might necessitate higher dosing to achieve complete complement inhibition, which in turn could impact the safety profile and overall therapeutic index in the delicate ocular environment. Immunogenicity remains another concern; engineered proteins can sometimes elicit anti-drug antibodies (ADAs), and although intravitreal delivery benefits from the eye’s relative immune privilege, long-term administration in a chronic disease like AMD may eventually overcome this barrier (Kamala et al., 2021; Biggs et al., 2022). In addition, while animal model data in non-ocular systems are promising, the unique anatomy and microenvironment of the retina—especially the dynamics of RPE function, Bruch’s membrane characteristics, and POS phagocytosis—present additional layers of complexity that have not yet been fully addressed by preclinical studies specific to this application (Kamala et al., 2021; Dobo et al., 2018).

There is also the challenge of ensuring consistent manufacturing and high-yield recombinant production of a complex, multi-domain protein like Mini-FH. Although advances in recombinant expression systems have made it feasible to produce such proteins at scale, the retention of full activity, appropriate post-translational modifications, and proper folding is critical for therapeutic efficacy and must be established within a robust quality control framework (Makou et al., 2013). Furthermore, the pharmacokinetic profile of Mini-FH, including its half-life on ocular tissues and systemic clearance if any, requires detailed investigation; alterations in inter-domain linker length or structural flexibility could impact tissue distribution and drug retention at the target site (Kamala et al., 2021; Dobo et al., 2018).

From a mechanistic standpoint, while Mini-FH is designed to restore regulation by competitively binding to C3b and facilitating its inactivation, the downstream consequences of complement repression in the retina are multifaceted. In dry AMD, chronic complement dysregulation not only drives inflammation but also contributes to impaired phagocytosis of POS by the RPE. Although preclinical evidence suggests that restoring complementary control can indirectly improve RPE function (Kamala et al., 2021), the exact molecular pathways linking complement inhibition to improved POS clearance have not been fully elucidated, necessitating further mechanistic studies in retinal model systems (Dobo et al., 2018).

In summary, Mini‐FH represents a highly promising therapeutic candidate for dry AMD due to its engineered dual functionality—providing both potent AP convertase regulatory activity and targeted binding to complement‐deposited surfaces—as well as its potential for superior tissue penetration given its compact molecular size (Kamala et al., 2021; de Boer et al., 2020). Its mechanism of action is well supported by structural, biochemical, and preclinical data, and the conceptual framework is further validated by ongoing clinical evaluations of full‐length recombinant FH in AMD. However, while the biochemical rationale and preclinical models lend strong support to the therapeutic strategy, challenges remain in translating these findings to the complex ocular environment; these include ensuring sufficient FI cofactor activity, managing potential immunogenicity, optimizing intravitreal delivery parameters, and confirming that the beneficial effects observed in kidney or systemic models are recapitulated in retinal tissues (de Boer et al., 2020; Dobo et al., 2018).

However, several critical issues must be addressed before advancing Mini‐FH as a clinical candidate for dry AMD. First, direct evidence from retinal model systems is required to confirm that the biochemical potency of Mini‐FH translates into meaningful therapeutic outcomes in the context of RPE integrity, Bruch’s membrane function, and POS phagocytosis (de Boer et al., 2020; Dobo et al., 2018). Second, while the immune privileged status of the eye may mitigate ADA risk, long-term intravitreal delivery studies need to be conducted to establish both safety and sustained efficacy over the chronic course of AMD (Kamala et al., 2021; Biggs et al., 2022). Third, the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles in the ocular milieu must be rigorously characterized to determine the optimal dosing regimen, ensuring that Mini‐FH reaches and remains active at the target site without eliciting off-target effects (Kamala et al., 2021; Dobo et al., 2018). Finally, manufacturing consistency and scalability remain practical challenges for any complex recombinant protein therapeutic, and these aspects must be optimized early in the development process to facilitate clinical translation (Makou et al., 2013).

In conclusion, Mini‐FH (Recombinant FH CCP1–4/19–20) is a state-of-the-art, mechanistically driven therapeutic candidate that harnesses the targeted complement inhibitory potential of engineered FH fragments for the treatment of dry AMD. Its design leverages the strengths of both the N-terminal regulatory and C-terminal recognition domains, offering a potent means to counteract the dysregulated complement activation underlying AMD pathology. While the preclinical data from non-ocular models are very promising, targeted studies in retinal systems are necessary to confirm its efficacy and safety in the context of dry AMD. Based on the comprehensive literature review, Mini‐FH represents a compelling candidate with strong scientific rationale and significant translational potential, but further preclinical work specifically focused on retinal endpoints is essential before progressing to clinical trials. The balance between enhanced C3b binding and potential trade-offs in FI cofactor activity, along with considerations of immunogenicity and optimal delivery strategies, will be critical determinants of its ultimate clinical success in alleviating the burden of dry AMD (Kamala et al., 2021; Dobo et al., 2018; de Boer et al., 2020; Biggs et al., 2022).
