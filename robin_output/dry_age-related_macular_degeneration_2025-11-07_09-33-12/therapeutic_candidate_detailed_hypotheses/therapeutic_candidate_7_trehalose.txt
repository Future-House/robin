Proposal for Trehalose (TFEB activator; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. T9531)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Trehalose is a naturally occurring, nonreducing disaccharide composed of two D‐glucose units (MW = 342.3 Da) that is not synthesized endogenously in mammals but is abundant in other organisms such as fungi, plants, and invertebrates (Cox et al., 2025). It was originally discovered as a cellular protectant and osmolyte that stabilizes proteins and membranes under conditions of stress, and its unique chemical stability and protein‐protective properties have led to its use in cryopreservation and organ transplant protocols (Lotfi et al., 2018). As a small-molecule therapeutic candidate, trehalose belongs to a class of compounds known as osmolytes and molecular chaperones; this category has been extensively explored for its capacity to stabilize proteins, prevent aggregation, and enhance cellular resilience in the face of diverse environmental and metabolic stresses (Abokyi et al., 2020; Cox et al., 2025). In drug development contexts, its repurposing for dry age-related macular degeneration (dAMD) is driven by its capacity to activate TFEB—a master transcription factor regulating the coordinated lysosomal expression and regulation (CLEAR) network—and thereby boost autophagy and lysosomal biogenesis in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells (Ghosh et al., 2023). Trehalose has been formulated for ocular use, most notably as an ingredient in tear substitutes for dry eye, and its safety profile in such formulations provides ancillary support for its potential repurposing to target RPE dysfunction in dAMD (Cox et al., 2025).

Therapeutic History:  
Trehalose has a well-documented history in biochemical and preclinical research due to its potent protein-stabilizing and osmoprotectant actions. It has been widely used in non-ophthalmic applications, most notably in cryopreservation protocols, where its ability to preserve protein structure and cellular integrity under desiccating and freezing conditions is exploited (Cox et al., 2025; Lotfi et al., 2018). In the ocular field, trehalose has been evaluated primarily for anterior segment indications such as dry eye disease; several randomized clinical trials have demonstrated its tolerability and efficacy as a tear substitute when combined with hyaluronic acid, with improvements in tear film break-up time (TBUT) and ocular surface symptoms (Chiambaretta et al., 2017).  
More importantly for dAMD, multiple in vitro studies using human RPE cell models—including ARPE-19 and iPSC-derived RPE from patients with genetic risk factors like CFH mutations—have shown that trehalose rescues autophagy deficits and lysosomal dysfunction, especially under oxidative-stress conditions induced by agents such as hydroquinone (Abokyi et al., 2020). These studies report that trehalose treatment upregulates key autophagy regulators (e.g., ATG5, ATG7) and lysosomal markers (e.g., LAMP2, cathepsin D) while enhancing autophagic flux, thereby improving the degradative capacity of RPE cells—a fundamental defect implicated in the accumulation of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) and lipofuscin in dAMD (Abokyi et al., 2020; Abokyi & Tse, 2025; Falcão et al., 2025). Additionally, in murine models of retinal degeneration—whether induced by light exposure or by genetic interventions—trehalose has been shown to protect against retinal cell loss and preserve outer nuclear layer (ONL) thickness, offering further preclinical evidence of its neuroprotective potential (Cox et al., 2025). Thus, while trehalose has an established record in non-ocular uses and in the treatment of anterior ocular surface disorders, emerging data now support its repurposing for dAMD by virtue of its ability to modulate autophagy and lysosomal degradation in RPE cells (Abokyi & Tse, 2025; Ghosh et al., 2023).

Mechanism of Action:  
The central mechanism by which trehalose is hypothesized to benefit dry AMD involves its capacity to activate the transcription factor EB (TFEB), a master regulator of the CLEAR network. TFEB controls the transcription of a whole battery of genes required for autophagy, lysosomal biogenesis, and metabolic adaptation (Abokyi et al., 2020). Typically, TFEB resides in the cytoplasm in an inactive, phosphorylated state under nutrient-rich or stress-depleted conditions; its translocation to the nucleus is tightly regulated by mTORC1-dependent and mTORC1-independent signaling pathways (Ghosh et al., 2023; Karim et al., 2020). Trehalose has been shown to induce TFEB nuclear translocation independently of mTOR inhibition, potentially through mechanisms involving Akt signaling modulation and AMPK activation (Ghosh et al., 2023; Ghosh et al., 2024). Upon nuclear entry, TFEB binds to CLEAR elements in the promoter regions of target genes, thereby upregulating autophagy mediators such as ATG5 and ATG7, enhancing the expression of lysosomal proteins like LAMP2, and increasing the levels of protein markers such as LC3B and p62 that are involved in autophagosome formation and maturation (Abokyi et al., 2020).  
Several biochemical studies in human RPE cells have demonstrated that trehalose treatment leads to increased TFEB mRNA and protein levels with concomitant upregulation of CLEAR network gene expression. For instance, in ARPE-19 cells exposed to hydroquinone—a model for oxidative stress relevant to dAMD—it was observed that trehalose not only increased LC3-II levels and GFP-LC3 puncta formation but also enhanced the degradation of autophagic substrates, indicating that trehalose facilitates effective autophagic flux rather than merely blocking lysosomal degradation (Abokyi et al., 2020).  
Mechanistically, trehalose is thought to penetrate mammalian cells via fluid-phase endocytosis and possibly through the SLC2A8 (GLUT8) transporter, which facilitates its intracellular accumulation at millimolar concentrations (Cox et al., 2025). Once inside the cell, trehalose appears to modulate several key pathways: by inhibiting Akt signaling, it reduces the phosphorylation of TFEB at specific residues (such as S467) that normally prevent its nuclear import; concurrently, trehalose may activate AMPK, which further supports autophagy induction (Ghosh et al., 2024; Karim et al., 2020). Moreover, although trehalose transiently upregulates NRF2 under basal conditions—thereby enhancing antioxidant defenses—its primary protective effect in oxidative stress models of the RPE appears to be mediated by restored autophagy, as demonstrated by loss of cytoprotection when autophagy is genetically disrupted via ATG5 knockdown (Abokyi et al., 2020).  
Beyond its role in autophagy, trehalose has been implicated in promoting chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) through stimulation of LAMP2 expression, which is essential for CMA substrate recognition and lysosomal import (Abokyi et al., 2020). In this way, trehalose may offer a dual benefit: boosting canonical macroautophagy while also enhancing CMA, collectively leading to improved clearance of damaged organelles, aggregated proteins, and undigested POS in the RPE—a critical function given the strong association between defective POS clearance and the pathogenesis of dAMD (Abokyi et al., 2020). Furthermore, in CFH-mutant iPSC-derived RPE cells, trehalose has been shown to rescue the diminished activity of lysosomal hydrolases such as cathepsin D and to restore other proteostasis markers, establishing a direct molecular link between trehalose-induced TFEB activation and functional improvements in the degradative capacity of RPE cells (Ghosh et al., 2023; Ghosh et al., 2024).  
Collectively, the mechanistic data suggest that trehalose exerts its beneficial effects through a complex interplay of intracellular signaling pathways that converge on the activation of TFEB, thereby re-establishing efficient autophagic clearance processes that are impaired in the aging and oxidative-stressed RPE (Abokyi et al., 2020; Laihia & Kaarniranta, 2020).

Expected Effect:  
Based on the mechanistic insights outlined above, the hypothesis posits that treatment with trehalose at a concentration of 5 mM in an in vitro assay using iPSC-derived RPE cells from dry AMD patients will lead to robust TFEB nuclear translocation. This nuclear translocation is expected to result in the transcriptional upregulation of CLEAR network genes, thereby increasing the expression of autophagy regulators such as ATG5 and ATG7 and promoting the biogenesis of lysosomes, as evidenced by increased levels of lysosomal markers like LAMP2 and cathepsin D (Abokyi et al., 2020). In an environment of oxidative stress—for example, when the iPSC-RPE cells are challenged with hydroquinone to mimic cigarette smoke-induced damage or other oxidative insults commonly associated with AMD—the activation of TFEB by trehalose is anticipated to restore impaired autophagic flux. This restoration would be reflected in the accumulation of LC3-II upon inhibition of lysosomal function (e.g., in the presence of chloroquine) and decreased levels of autophagy substrate p62 (Abokyi et al., 2020).  
Crucially, by enhancing autophagic and lysosomal function in the RPE, trehalose is expected to improve POS degradation. Efficient degradation of POS is vital for photoreceptor health, and its impairment has been implicated in the pathogenesis of dry AMD through the accumulation of toxic byproducts and lipofuscin (Abokyi et al., 2020; Falcão et al., 2025). Thus, the expected phenotype in the in vitro assay would include not only molecular signatures of enhanced autophagy (e.g., increased LC3B puncta, upregulated ATG gene expression) but also functional improvements such as reduced oxidative stress (lower reactive oxygen species levels) and improved cell viability under stress conditions (Abokyi et al., 2020).  
Moreover, previous studies have shown that trehalose’s cytoprotective effects are autophagy-dependent because genetic or pharmacologic inhibition of autophagy (e.g., via ATG5 knockdown) abolishes the otherwise protective effects of trehalose, thereby reinforcing the notion that the expected improvement in POS degradation and overall cellular homeostasis is tightly linked to the restoration of autophagy and lysosomal competence (Abokyi et al., 2020). In essence, the application of 5 mM trehalose to oxidative-stressed iPSC-RPE cells is anticipated to reverse the autophagic deficits that are characteristic of dry AMD, ultimately leading to enhanced clearance of POS, reduced accumulation of cytotoxic aggregates, and improved cellular survival and function in an in vitro model that recapitulates key aspects of the disease (Abokyi et al., 2020; Abokyi & Tse, 2025).

Overall Evaluation:  
Trehalose represents a promising repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD based on its multifaceted capacity to modulate intracellular degradation pathways that are critically impaired in the disease state. One of the primary strengths of trehalose is its robust preclinical evidence base. Multiple studies have demonstrated that trehalose can induce TFEB nuclear translocation in RPE models, resulting in the transcriptional activation of an array of autophagy and lysosomal genes (Abokyi et al., 2020; Ghosh et al., 2023; Laihia & Kaarniranta, 2020). This mechanistic property directly addresses a key pathogenic mechanism in dry AMD, where the accumulation of undegraded photoreceptor outer segments and lipofuscin due to defective autophagy plays a contributory role in RPE dysfunction and subsequent retinal degeneration (Abokyi et al., 2020; Falcão et al., 2025). The ability of trehalose to enhance both macroautophagy and chaperone-mediated autophagy augments its therapeutic appeal, as it suggests an overall improvement in cellular proteostasis, which is imperative in aged or stressed RPE cells (Abokyi et al., 2020; Cox et al., 2025).  
Another notable strength is the established safety profile of trehalose. It is a naturally occurring compound with a long history of use in various medical and industrial applications, including as an ingredient in ocular surface formulations for dry eye disease (Chiambaretta et al., 2017; Cox et al., 2025). Furthermore, the preclinical data showing that trehalose can protect against oxidative stress and preserve retinal function in animal models lend credibility to the hypothesis that it may exert similar protective effects in human RPE cells (Cox et al., 2025).  
However, several weaknesses and gaps must be acknowledged as well. First, the majority of the compelling evidence for trehalose’s efficacy in modulating TFEB and enhancing autophagy comes from in vitro studies using immortalized RPE cell lines (e.g., ARPE-19) or rodent models, with only limited data available from human iPSC-derived RPE models specifically derived from AMD patients (Abokyi et al., 2020). Second, there are ongoing debates in the literature regarding the precise molecular mechanisms by which trehalose exerts its autophagy-enhancing effects, with some reports disputing its ability to induce autophagy under certain conditions (Abokyi et al., 2020; Abokyi & Tse, 2025).  
Another potential challenge lies in the delivery of trehalose to the RPE in a clinically meaningful manner. Although trehalose is orally bioavailable and has been successfully formulated for topical ocular use in dry eye applications, the posterior segment of the eye presents unique drug delivery hurdles. Penetration through the cornea and subsequent distribution to the RPE and retina often require specialized formulations, and while there are preclinical reports of trehalose reaching posterior ocular tissues (Cox et al., 2025), comprehensive pharmacokinetic and ocular bioavailability data for trehalose in the context of dAMD remain sparse.  
Furthermore, while the repurposing of trehalose is attractive due to its established safety and low cost, intellectual property issues and regulatory challenges associated with drug repurposing may complicate clinical development. Despite these concerns, the overall body of evidence supports further investigation into trehalose as a potential modulator of TFEB and autophagy in the RPE (Abokyi et al., 2020; Abokyi & Tse, 2025; Cox et al., 2025; Ghosh et al., 2023).  
In summary, trehalose offers several strong advantages as a candidate therapeutic for dry AMD. Its ability to activate TFEB and upregulate a broad spectrum of autophagy and lysosomal genes addresses a fundamental defect in RPE cells implicated in the disease. Robust preclinical evidence in both in vitro and animal models suggests that trehalose enhances autophagic flux, improves POS clearance, and confers cytoprotective effects against oxidative stress. Its favorable safety profile and prior use in ocular formulations further support its translational potential. However, critical gaps remain in terms of validating its efficacy in human-derived RPE models, optimizing its dosing and delivery to the posterior segment, and resolving discrepancies regarding its mechanism of action in different experimental contexts. Given these considerations, trehalose represents a promising candidate that merits further investigation—particularly through rigorous dose-response studies, advanced formulation development to enhance retinal delivery, and robust clinical trials in patient populations at risk for dry AMD—to fully establish its therapeutic value (Abokyi et al., 2020; Abokyi & Tse, 2025; Cox et al., 2025; Ghosh et al., 2023).
