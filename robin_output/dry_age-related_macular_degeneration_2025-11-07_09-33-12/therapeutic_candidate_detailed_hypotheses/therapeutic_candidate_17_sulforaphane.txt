Proposal for Sulforaphane (Nrf2 activator; LKT Laboratories Cat. No. S8044)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Sulforaphane is a naturally occurring isothiocyanate that is principally derived from cruciferous vegetables, notably broccoli, cauliflower, kale, and cabbage (Dutta et al., 2023; Lagunas-Rangel, 2024). In its natural state, sulforaphane is present as its glucosinolate precursor, glucoraphanin, which is enzymatically converted to the active form upon tissue disruption by the enzyme myrosinase (Habtemariam, 2024; Mangla et al., 2021). This conversion is a well‐established biochemical process that occurs during food processing or mastication, and it provides the basis for sulforaphane’s identification as a nutraceutical with significant potential in various disease contexts (Houghton, 2019; Lagunas-Rangel, 2024). Sulforaphane belongs to the isothiocyanate class of compounds, a group known for their prominent antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and anticancer activities (Dutta et al., 2023; Habtemariam, 2024). Its discovery and subsequent characterization have positioned it as a compound of interest not only in cancer chemoprevention but also in other chronic disease models including neurodegenerative conditions and ocular pathologies (Ordonez et al., 2021; Habtemariam, 2024). The current therapeutic candidate—in this case, Sulforaphane (Nrf2 activator; LKT Laboratories Cat. No. S8044)—is being evaluated for its potential to modulate specific cellular pathways implicated in the pathogenesis of dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD), particularly its ability to activate the Nrf2 transcription factor in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells at a proposed concentration of 5 µM (Chang et al., 2020; Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019).

Therapeutic History:  
Sulforaphane’s therapeutic history is extensive and spans multiple domains, primarily due to its ability to induce phase II detoxification enzymes and to modulate oxidative stress responses at the cellular level. Preclinical studies have demonstrated its efficacy in inducing apoptosis and mitigating cancer cell proliferation through the modulation of key signaling pathways (Dutta et al., 2023; Tortorella et al., 2015). In addition to its anticancer properties, sulforaphane has been evaluated in models of neurodegeneration, where it has been shown to confer neuroprotection by alleviating oxidative stress and inflammation—a property that is particularly relevant to retinal diseases such as AMD (Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2024). In ocular research, various investigations using in vitro and in vivo models have highlighted sulforaphane’s capacity to activate the Nrf2 pathway in retinal cells, leading to enhanced antioxidant enzyme expression and reduced oxidative damage (Chang et al., 2020; Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019; Chang et al., 2020). Notably, studies employing retinal pigment epithelial cell lines, such as ARPE-19 cells, have documented protective effects of sulforaphane against oxidative insult, where enhanced expression of genes such as heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), glutathione peroxidase (GPX), and glutamate-cysteine ligase modulatory subunit (GCLM) has been observed (Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019; Kubo et al., 2017). Although sulforaphane has been predominantly explored in the context of cancer chemoprevention and neuroprotection, its application in treating ocular conditions such as dry AMD is emerging. Early studies suggest that sulforaphane might reduce lipofuscin accumulation and rescue lysosomal pH abnormalities in A2E-loaded RPE cells, which are hallmark features of dry AMD pathogenesis (Zhang et al., 2024; Chang et al., 2020). Safety profiles established through both in vitro studies and limited clinical trials indicate that sulforaphane is generally well-tolerated, with few adverse effects even upon oral administration, thereby rendering it a promising agent for repurposing in ocular disorders (Ordonez et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024).

Mechanism of Action:  
The therapeutic actions of sulforaphane are mediated primarily through the activation of the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway, a central regulator of antioxidant defense mechanisms. Under basal conditions, Nrf2 is sequestered in the cytosol by its inhibitory partner, Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1), which facilitates its ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation (Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019; Mangla et al., 2021). Sulforaphane exerts its effect by covalently modifying critical cysteine residues on KEAP1, thereby disrupting the KEAP1-Nrf2 complex. This modification impedes Nrf2 degradation and permits its rapid translocation into the nucleus (Habtemariam, 2024; Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019). Once in the nucleus, Nrf2 heterodimerizes with small musculoaponeurotic fibrosarcoma (sMaf) proteins and binds to antioxidant response elements (AREs) in the promoter regions of a multitude of genes, culminating in the transcriptional activation of phase II detoxification enzymes such as HO-1, GPX, and GCLM (Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019; Hu et al., 2024; Hyttinen et al., 2019). In addition to enhancing the antioxidant capacity of the cell, activation of Nrf2 by sulforaphane has been associated with the upregulation of genes implicated in the regulation of autophagy and lysosomal function (Chang et al., 2020; Bergandi et al., 2024). This is particularly salient in the context of RPE biology, where proper lysosomal function is critical for the degradation of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) and the prevention of lipofuscin accumulation. Impaired degradation pathways in RPE cells lead to cellular stress and contribute to the pathogenesis of dry AMD (Zhang et al., 2024; Chang et al., 2020). Moreover, sulforaphane’s ability to reduce pro-inflammatory cytokine production through suppression of mediators like intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) further supports its cytoprotective role in the retina (Habtemariam, 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). The proposed concentration of 5 µM in vitro is supported by studies indicating that such a dose is sufficient to robustly activate Nrf2, thereby inducing an antioxidant response without eliciting toxicity in RPE cells (Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019; Chang et al., 2020). This multifaceted mechanism—encompassing enhanced transcription of antioxidant defense genes, promotion of autophagy, and attenuation of inflammatory signaling—underpins the rationale for deploying sulforaphane as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD (Chang et al., 2020; Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019).

Expected Effect:  
Within the iPSC-derived RPE model of dry AMD, the administration of sulforaphane at 5 µM is hypothesized to yield several beneficial outcomes. First, by engaging the Nrf2 signaling pathway, sulforaphane is expected to increase the transcription of key antioxidant genes such as GCLM, GPX, and HO-1 (Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019; Kubo et al., 2017). This upregulation will enhance the cellular capacity to neutralize reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby mitigating oxidative stress—a well-known contributor to RPE degeneration in dry AMD (Chang et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2024). Second, the improved activation of Nrf2 is anticipated to restore autophagy processes and lysosomal enzyme function within RPE cells, facilitating more efficient degradation of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) and the removal of toxic byproducts such as lipofuscin (Chang et al., 2020; Bergandi et al., 2024). A2E, a major bisretinoid component of lipofuscin, has been implicated in the disruption of lysosomal pH and function; therefore, by rescuing lysosomal acidity and enhancing enzymatic activity, sulforaphane could support the clearance of A2E aggregates and related debris (Zhang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). Third, given its anti-inflammatory attributes, sulforaphane is expected to downregulate cytokine expression and other inflammatory mediators, thus reducing the chronic inflammatory state that exacerbates RPE damage in dry AMD (Habtemariam, 2024; Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019). On a molecular level, the improved phagocytic function of RPE cells, achieved through the normalization of lysosomal and autophagic pathways, is critical for maintaining retinal integrity and function since efficient POS degradation is necessary for visual cycle homeostasis (Chang et al., 2020; Kubo et al., 2017). Furthermore, the favorable safety profile observed in preclinical studies—coupled with evidence of minimal toxicity even at higher concentrations—supports the use of sulforaphane at the proposed dose, making it a mechanistically specific strategy to alleviate oxidative and lysosomal stress in dry AMD models (Ordonez et al., 2021; Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019). Collectively, the expected effects at the molecular, cellular, and functional levels converge on the hypothesis that sulforaphane will both preserve RPE cell viability and enhance cellular clearance mechanisms, thereby countering key pathological features of dry AMD.

Overall Evaluation:  
Sulforaphane represents a promising therapeutic candidate for dry AMD due to its multifaceted mechanism of action, which directly targets key pathological processes involved in retinal degeneration. One of its primary strengths lies in its robust activation of the Nrf2 pathway; this leads to a comprehensive antioxidant response by upregulating detoxification and cytoprotective genes that are essential for neutralizing ROS and ameliorating oxidative stress in RPE cells (Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019; Kubo et al., 2017). The capacity to rescue lysosomal function and promote autophagy is another major advantage, as it addresses the abnormal accumulation of lipofuscin—a hallmark of dry AMD—by restoring the degradation and recycling of photoreceptor outer segments (Chang et al., 2020; Bergandi et al., 2024). Additionally, sulforaphane’s documented anti-inflammatory effects further enhance its therapeutic potential by reducing the expression of inflammatory cytokines that contribute to retinal damage (Habtemariam, 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). The favorable safety profile noted in various studies reinforces the viability of repurposing sulforaphane, particularly since clinical trials in other indications have demonstrated minimal systemic toxicity and good tolerability (Ordonez et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024).

Despite these strengths, several challenges must be acknowledged. A key limitation associated with sulforaphane is its inherently low water solubility and bioavailability, which pose significant obstacles for achieving therapeutic concentrations at the target site, especially in ocular tissues where the blood–ocular barriers prevent easy drug penetration (Zhang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). Current formulations may result in a high first-pass effect and limited tissue penetration upon systemic administration. This challenge necessitates the exploration of novel delivery systems—such as nanoformulations, liposomes, or other topical ocular delivery strategies—that can enhance the stability and bioavailability of sulforaphane (Bergandi et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). Furthermore, while preclinical data in cell culture models and animal studies are promising, there remains an evidence gap regarding the efficacy of sulforaphane in human subjects with dry AMD. Robust clinical trials designed to establish dose-response relationships, optimal administration routes, and long-term safety profiles are essential before the compound can be confidently advanced as a therapeutic intervention for dry AMD (Zhang et al., 2024).

Another area of concern is the scalability and reproducibility of the effect. Variability in bioactivity may arise due to differences in the extraction or synthesis of sulforaphane, as the compound’s activity greatly depends on its purity, the presence of specific enantiomers, and the method of formulation (Lagunas-Rangel, 2024; Tortorella et al., 2015). This variability could potentially influence both the efficacy and safety outcomes in clinical applications. The current hypothesis proposes that a 5 µM concentration is effective for activating Nrf2 in RPE cells; however, translating this concentration from an in vitro model to an in vivo setting presents additional challenges. The effective dose in the retina might differ substantially due to differences in drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion, which must be carefully optimized through pharmacokinetic studies (Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019; Kubo et al., 2017).

In summary, sulforaphane holds compelling potential as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD owing to its well-defined mechanism of action, which encompasses antioxidant activation via Nrf2, restoration of lysosomal and autophagic function, and anti-inflammatory effects. These mechanisms directly target the cellular dysfunctions that underlie dry AMD pathology—including oxidative damage, impaired POS degradation, and chronic inflammation—and thus position sulforaphane as a mechanistically specific strategy to ameliorate disease progression (Chang et al., 2020; Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019). The primary strengths of sulforaphane are its natural origin, its extensive preclinical validation across various disease models, and its capacity to modulate multiple cellular protective pathways simultaneously. However, weaknesses such as poor bioavailability, formulation challenges, and a current lack of definitive clinical data in the context of AMD temper its immediate translational readiness (Zhang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024; Bergandi et al., 2024).

To optimize the clinical translation of sulforaphane for dry AMD, future research directions should include:  
1. The development and validation of advanced drug delivery systems tailored for ocular tissues that can overcome the limitations posed by the blood–ocular barrier and enhance the drug’s bioavailability.  
2. Comprehensive pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic studies in animal models and early-phase clinical trials to determine the optimal dosing regimen that achieves therapeutic efficacy in the retina without compromising safety.  
3. Detailed investigations into the long-term effects of sulforaphane on retinal structure and function, including its impact on photoreceptor survival, RPE cell integrity, and overall visual performance.  
4. Comparative studies to evaluate sulforaphane’s efficacy relative to existing antioxidant and anti-inflammatory therapies in dry AMD, in order to establish its potential niche within the therapeutic landscape.  

Overall, while significant work remains to be done, sulforaphane exhibits a compelling mechanistic rationale and a promising preclinical profile that justify further investigation as a repurposed therapeutic agent for dry age-related macular degeneration. Its ability to activate Nrf2, boost antioxidant defenses, restore autophagy and lysosomal function, and reduce inflammatory mediators provides a multi-pronged approach to address the complex pathology of dry AMD (Chang et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2024; Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019). If formulation and delivery challenges can be effectively overcome, sulforaphane may well represent a viable and innovative strategy to preserve retinal health and counteract the progressive degenerative processes characteristic of dry AMD (Chang et al., 2020; Habtemariam, 2024).

In conclusion, the current body of evidence from biochemical, preclinical, and early clinical studies supports the hypothesis that sulforaphane can enhance Nrf2 activation in RPE cells and improve antioxidant and lysosomal function, thereby potentially alleviating key pathological features of dry AMD. Given its extensive therapeutic history, well-characterized mechanism of action, and favorable safety profile, sulforaphane is a strong candidate deserving further rigorous evaluation and development. Nonetheless, targeted efforts to address its formulation and bioavailability limitations will be critical in shaping its future as a clinically effective treatment for dry AMD (Zhang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024).
