Proposal for Geldanamycin (HSP90 inhibitor; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. G5939)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Geldanamycin is a small-molecule inhibitor of heat shock protein 90 (HSP90) that originally garnered attention as a naturally derived product from Streptomyces species, and it belongs to the benzoquinone ansamycin class of compounds. As a natural product, geldanamycin was first isolated for its antibiotic properties and later found to exert potent effects on cellular protein homeostasis by binding the N-terminal ATP-binding pocket of HSP90. This mode of binding disrupts the chaperone function of HSP90, which is responsible for assisting in the stabilization and maturation of numerous client proteins, including signaling regulators and enzymes. Compounds of this chemical class have historically been investigated for their anticancer potential, given that HSP90 plays a central role in sustaining oncogenic signaling by stabilizing mutated or overexpressed proteins. In the context of ocular research, geldanamycin’s ability to modulate proteostasis and inflammatory cascades has prompted its investigation as a potential therapy for dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD), where dysregulated protein aggregation, impaired autophagy, and chronic inflammation are key pathological features (Piippo et al., 2018).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, geldanamycin and related HSP90 inhibitors have been used primarily as research tools to interrogate the role of molecular chaperones in cancer, neurodegeneration, and cellular stress responses. In preclinical studies, geldanamycin demonstrated the capacity to induce a robust heat shock response characterized by the upregulation of HSP70 and concomitant modulation of client proteins that depend on HSP90. Its inhibition of HSP90 has been extensively explored in models of protein aggregation diseases and in studies of inflammasome regulation in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells. For example, several reports have provided direct experimental evidence that HSP90 inhibition by geldanamycin prevents the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in human RPE models—specifically in ARPE-19 cells, human embryonic stem cell-derived RPE, and primary human RPE—by disrupting the protective HSP90–SGT1 complex that stabilizes NLRP3, thereby triggering autophagy-mediated clearance of the receptor (Piippo et al., 2018). In related studies, geldanamycin was also shown to induce an HSP70 response that can modulate proteostasis and reduce cellular inflammatory stress, although its high toxicity has consistently been reported as a barrier to clinical application in humans (Chen et al., 2021). While geldanamycin itself has not been approved for clinical use in dry AMD, its mechanistic insights have spurred research into repurposing HSP90 inhibitors for ocular indications, albeit with a need for derivatives or alternative candidates (Chen et al., 2021; Piippo et al., 2018).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, HSP90 functions as an ATP-dependent molecular chaperone that is critical for the correct folding, stabilization, and activation of a wide range of client proteins, including kinases, transcription factors, and components of the inflammasome complex such as NLRP3. Under basal conditions, HSP90, together with its co-chaperone SGT1, forms a complex with NLRP3 that maintains the receptor in an inactive yet activation-competent state. Geldanamycin binds to the N-terminal ATP-binding pocket of HSP90, thereby competitively inhibiting its ATPase activity and disrupting its chaperone cycle. This inhibition leads to the dissociation of the HSP90–SGT1 complex, releasing NLRP3. Once liberated from the protective chaperone complex, NLRP3 is highly unstable and becomes a substrate for intracellular degradation mechanisms. In the specific context of human RPE cells, experimental evidence has shown that geldanamycin preferentially promotes the autophagic clearance of NLRP3 rather than its proteasomal degradation. Mechanistic experiments that used proteasome inhibition with MG-132 and lysosomal/autophagy inhibition with bafilomycin A₁ or chloroquine revealed that when autophagy is intact, geldanamycin drives the degradation of NLRP3 via the autophagy pathway, resulting in a robust reduction in caspase-1 activity and IL-1β secretion (Piippo et al., 2018). This HSP90-dependent regulation of NLRP3 is particularly significant because inflammasome activation in RPE cells is implicated in the pathogenesis of dry AMD. In addition, geldanamycin induces the expression of HSP70, a cytoprotective chaperone that further contributes to maintaining proteostasis under stress conditions; however, the increase in HSP70 is a double-edged sword, as it reflects cellular stress responses that could have both beneficial and deleterious effects if dysregulated (Chen et al., 2021; Ryhänen, 2011). Notably, the timing of geldanamycin administration is critical: its inhibitory effect on IL-1β secretion is maximal when added immediately prior to the induction of proteasome or autophagy inhibition, indicating that the HSP90 inhibition must occur before the onset of overwhelming proteostatic stress to be effective (Piippo et al., 2018).

Expected Effect:  
Based on the mechanistic findings, the expected therapeutic effect of geldanamycin in a dry AMD model is multi-pronged. First, by binding to HSP90 and disrupting its stabilization of NLRP3, geldanamycin is anticipated to accelerate the autophagic clearance of NLRP3 complexes. This clearance is expected to blunt the assembly and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, thereby reducing the cleavage of pro-caspase-1 and ultimately diminishing the production and secretion of the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-1β. In the context of dry AMD, where low-grade chronic inflammation and impaired clearance of damaged cellular proteins contribute to disease progression, the suppression of inflammasome activation is a desirable outcome that could help mitigate retinal inflammation and cell death (Piippo et al., 2018). Second, by reducing the persistent activation of the inflammasome, geldanamycin may help preserve lysosomal function, which is critical for the degradation of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) by the RPE. Lysosomal dysfunction and the resultant accumulation of lipofuscin are hallmark features in the pathogenesis of dry AMD; therefore, maintaining efficient lysosomal degradation is key to restoring RPE homeostasis. Although direct evidence linking geldanamycin to enhanced POS digestion is not comprehensively detailed in the current literature excerpts, there is reasonable mechanistic rationale to suggest that improved autophagic flux and decreased inflammatory burden could promote better phagocytic competence in RPE cells (Markitantova & Simirskii, 2025; Piippo et al., 2018). Finally, given that HSP90 inhibition via geldanamycin has been shown to reduce proteostasis stress and modulate key inflammatory pathways, its application in RPE models of dry AMD could result in improved cellular survival and function, potentially slowing or reversing aspects of the degenerative process. It is important to note that the hypothesized effective concentration (EC₅₀ around 500 nM) is based on in vitro studies in RPE cells, and achieving this efficacy in an in vivo ocular setting via intravitreal administration would require careful dosing and pharmacokinetic optimization to mitigate systemic toxicity (Piippo et al., 2018).

Overall Evaluation:  
Geldanamycin represents a promising candidate from a mechanistic perspective for treating dry AMD due to its unique mode of action as an HSP90 inhibitor. The strengths of this therapeutic approach include its ability to directly target a key regulatory protein that stabilizes the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to downregulation of a major pro-inflammatory pathway implicated in dry AMD pathogenesis. Preclinical research in RPE models, including ARPE-19, hESC-derived RPE, and primary human RPE, has consistently demonstrated that geldanamycin can reduce caspase-1 activity and IL-1β secretion by promoting autophagic degradation of NLRP3, which is central to halting the vicious cycle of chronic inflammation (Piippo et al., 2018). This is further supported by studies indicating that proper timing of HSP90 inhibition is crucial to maximize the clearance of NLRP3 and achieve therapeutic benefits before proteostatic stress overwhelms the cellular degradation machinery (Piippo et al., 2018). Additionally, the induction of HSP70 following geldanamycin treatment may confer additional cytoprotective effects by promoting proper protein folding and assisting in the clearance of misfolded proteins, thus helping preserve lysosomal function and potentially improving photoreceptor outer segment (POS) digestion—a process that is compromised in dry AMD (Chen et al., 2021; Ryhänen, 2011).

However, significant challenges limit the clinical translation of geldanamycin. One of the major weaknesses of this therapeutic candidate is its well-documented toxicity profile. Numerous studies have reported that while geldanamycin is effective in modulating HSP90 and reducing inflammasome activation at low nanomolar doses, its inherent cytotoxicity and off-target effects make it unsuitable for direct clinical application. In several experimental settings, geldanamycin has been described as “too toxic for human administration,” with adverse effects observed in both in vitro and in vivo models (Piippo et al., 2018). Such toxicity is believed to arise in part from the quinone moiety of the molecule, which can engage in redox cycling and generate reactive oxygen species, thereby exacerbating cellular stress and impairing normal proteostasis. Furthermore, while the small-molecule nature of geldanamycin is an advantage in terms of drug delivery—especially for routes such as intravitreal injection—the narrow therapeutic window means that fine-tuning the dose to achieve the desired biological effect without incurring toxicity remains a significant hurdle. There is also the possibility that global inhibition of HSP90 could destabilize multiple client proteins beyond NLRP3, leading to unintended perturbations in vital signaling pathways and cellular homeostasis (Chen et al., 2021).

In addition, despite the promising preclinical data supporting the use of geldanamycin for reducing inflammasome activation in RPE cells, there is a noticeable gap in the literature concerning its impact on other critical aspects of dry AMD pathology—specifically, the direct enhancement of photoreceptor outer segment (POS) digestion. Although it is hypothesized that preserving lysosomal function via inhibition of chronic inflammasome activity should translate into improved phagocytosis of POS by RPE cells, definitive experimental evidence demonstrating that geldanamycin treatment leads to enhanced POS clearance in AMD models is lacking (Markitantova & Simirskii, 2025; Piippo et al., 2018). This represents an area where further targeted studies are required.

Overall, geldanamycin’s mechanism of action is compelling from a proof-of-concept perspective; it elucidates how HSP90 inhibition can modulate the stability of key inflammatory mediators such as NLRP3 and thereby attenuate detrimental inflammatory cascades in RPE cells. The detailed biochemical insights provided by studies using geldanamycin have been invaluable for understanding the interplay between proteostasis, autophagy, and inflammation in the context of dry AMD. However, the clinical translation of geldanamycin as a therapeutic candidate is considerably hampered by its toxicity profile and the potential for off-target effects. Consequently, while geldanamycin offers important mechanistic validation for targeting HSP90 as a strategy to modulate NLRP3 inflammasome activity, repurposing efforts for dry AMD would likely need to focus on less toxic analogues or more selective HSP90 inhibitors (such as TAS-116) that preserve the beneficial effects on proteostasis without incurring significant cytotoxicity (Piippo et al., 2018). In summary, geldanamycin is a scientifically sound candidate from the perspective of modulating key pathogenic mechanisms in dry AMD, yet its translational potential is limited by safety concerns that must be addressed either through structural modifications or by identifying alternative inhibitors within the HSP90 inhibitor class that demonstrate improved ocular and systemic tolerability.
