Proposal for Metoprolol (β₁-adrenergic blocker; AstraZeneca)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Metoprolol is a second-generation, β1-selective adrenergic receptor antagonist developed within the broader historical evolution of β-blockers that began with Sir James Black’s introduction of β-adrenergic blockade for angina and expanded into major cardiovascular indications over subsequent decades (Opie, 2013; Mandiricha & Rochmanti, 2020). Within this class, β1-selective antagonists such as metoprolol competitively inhibit catecholamine binding at β1-adrenergic receptors, attenuating Gs–adenylyl cyclase–cAMP signaling and downstream protein kinase A (PKA) effects in tissues where β1-ARs are functionally expressed, most prominently the heart (Böhm et al., 2023; Mandiricha & Rochmanti, 2020). β-blockers as a class are synthetic small molecules with heterogeneous structures and pharmacokinetic properties (lipophilicity, bioavailability, hepatic versus renal clearance, intrinsic sympathomimetic activity), which shape their tissue penetration and side-effect profiles; metoprolol is moderately lipophilic and is predominantly cleared via hepatic CYP2D6-mediated metabolism (Panjrath & Messerli, 2006; Opie, 2013).

Therapeutically, β-blockers (including metoprolol) are widely used for hypertension (historically), angina pectoris, arrhythmia rate control, post–myocardial infarction secondary prevention, and chronic heart failure, with robust evidence for mortality and morbidity reduction in selected cardiovascular settings (Opie, 2013). In ophthalmology, β-adrenergic antagonists became a major class for open-angle glaucoma due to their ability to lower intraocular pressure (IOP) by reducing aqueous humor production via ciliary body β-adrenergic receptors; the archetypal agent is the nonselective β-blocker timolol, whereas the β1-selective agent betaxolol was developed to mitigate β2-mediated respiratory adverse effects (Nartey et al., 2021; Nocentini & Supuran, 2019). Importantly for posterior-segment drug delivery, early clinical work has evaluated intravitreal metoprolol tartrate at low dose with safety-focused endpoints, establishing preliminary tolerability and paving the way for hypothesis-driven ocular repurposing studies (Chaves et al., 2021).

Therapeutic History  
Systemic cardiovascular use  
Metoprolol has long-standing use as a β1-selective blocker for ischemic heart disease and heart failure; it is among the best-studied β-blockers for postinfarct protection and chronic heart failure alongside carvedilol and bisoprolol, reducing arrhythmic deaths and improving survival when properly titrated in stable systolic heart failure cohorts (Opie, 2013). Post–myocardial infarction, guideline-supported β-blockade with agents such as metoprolol succinate reduces long-term mortality by approximately a quarter in aggregate analyses, with additive benefit when combined with renin–angiotensin blockade (Opie, 2013). In hypertension, β-blockers have lost primacy as first-line therapy in uncomplicated disease due to inferior stroke prevention and metabolic adverse profiles compared with alternative classes, although β1-selective agents remain integral in specific comorbid contexts (e.g., ischemic heart disease, tachyarrhythmias) (Panjrath & Messerli, 2006; Opie, 2013). Pharmacologically, metoprolol’s hepatic metabolism via CYP2D6 yields clinically relevant drug–drug interaction risk (e.g., with paroxetine/fluoxetine), with interindividual variability in exposure that has been documented in pharmacogenetic studies and long-term PK observations (Ali et al., 2019; Nieminen et al., 2005; Opie, 2013).

Ophthalmic class use and metoprolol-specific ocular experience  
In ophthalmology, topical β-adrenergic antagonists are established agents to lower IOP, reducing aqueous humor production via antagonism of β-receptors in the ciliary body and iris/ciliary processes; timolol has been the archetype since 1978, and betaxolol (β1-selective) was introduced to minimize β2-related bronchospasm risks, albeit with somewhat reduced IOP-lowering efficacy relative to timolol (Nartey et al., 2021; Nocentini & Supuran, 2019). Betaxolol is repeatedly cited for neuroprotective actions in ocular models (e.g., slowing retinal changes after ischemia/reperfusion), underscoring that some β-blockers possess non-IOP biological effects on retinal cells (Nartey et al., 2021). Multiple sources list metoprolol among β1-selective agents and, in some reports, among β-blockers available for topical ophthalmic use; however, detailed clinical IOP-lowering data, efficacy comparisons, or neuroprotective endpoints specific to metoprolol in glaucoma are not provided in the materials at hand (Nartey et al., 2021). Topical β-blockers can cause systemic adverse effects because of nasolacrimal absorption without first-pass metabolism, including bronchospasm and bradyarrhythmias, necessitating caution in patients with reactive airway disease or conduction disorders and motivation for strategies to reduce systemic exposure (Vander Zanden et al., 2001; Nocentini & Supuran, 2019).

Of particular relevance to posterior-segment indications, a small phase I clinical study delivered off-label intravitreal metoprolol tartrate (50 μg/0.05 mL) in five eyes with circumscribed choroidal hemangioma, with safety assessments including full-field ERG (ISCEV protocol), OCT-based retinal thickness and choroidal thickness, anterior segment/vitreous inflammation, BCVA, and systemic blood pressure; the investigators reported no ERG evidence of toxicity, no clinical inflammation, stable IOP, stable choroidal thickness, no visual acuity deterioration, and modest reductions in retinal thickness or intra/subretinal fluid in a subset at four weeks (Chaves et al., 2021). Parallel rabbit studies did not show acute retinal toxicity at 50–100 μg intravitreal doses, supporting the human dose selection as biologically tolerable; nevertheless, limitations include very small sample size, single-dose exposure, short follow-up, and lack of dose–response or repeated-administration data (Chaves et al., 2021). The same group and others frame β-adrenergic blockade as an emerging target for neovascular or vascular ocular lesions, with propranolol providing historical precedent (infantile hemangiomas systemically; anecdotal ocular cases), while β1-selective metoprolol is explored for intraocular use owing to physicochemical compatibility and safety signals (Böhm et al., 2023; Chaves et al., 2021).

History in retinal/degenerative disease models  
Outside of pressure lowering and ocular vascular lesions, the provided sources report that metoprolol demonstrated neuroprotective effects in a rat model of retinal degeneration when given intraperitoneally or orally, and that metoprolol was not toxic to cultured retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells in vitro, implying a tolerable profile at the RPE level (Chaves et al., 2021). While this signals potential relevance to retinal cell survival, the evidence is preliminary, systemic rather than local in the degeneration model, and not specific to age-related macular degeneration subtypes; no clinical data in dry AMD were identified in the supplied materials (Chaves et al., 2021). The literature here does not document trials or case series of β1-selective antagonists for dry AMD, geographic atrophy, or RPE phagocytic dysfunction endpoints, so any linkage is inferential based on class mechanisms and exploratory safety/biology rather than disease-directed outcomes (Chaves et al., 2021; Nartey et al., 2021).

Mechanism of Action  
Primary pharmacology and signaling  
β1-adrenergic receptors are G-protein–coupled receptors classically coupled to Gαs, stimulating adenylyl cyclase, increasing intracellular cAMP, and activating PKA with broad downstream effects on ion channels, contractile machinery, and metabolism; antagonists such as metoprolol prevent catecholamine binding and blunt these signaling cascades in β1-expressing tissues (Böhm et al., 2023). β1-ARs are predominantly expressed in the heart, congruent with the robust chronotropic and inotropic effects of catecholamines and the established therapeutic actions of β1-selective blockade in cardiovascular disease; classically, blockade reduces cardiac output and renin release, lowering myocardial oxygen demand and ameliorating ischemic symptoms (Mandiricha & Rochmanti, 2020). Lipophilicity governs CNS penetration and neuropsychiatric side effects across the class, with more lipophilic agents crossing the blood–brain barrier more readily and potentially engaging central β-AR or other targets; metoprolol exhibits moderate lipid solubility, implying some CNS penetration and reinforcing that β-adrenergic mechanisms relevant to neurobiology are plausible with this agent (Opie, 2013; Panjrath & Messerli, 2006).

Ocular mechanisms and β-adrenergic pharmacology  
In the eye, β-adrenergic receptors in the ciliary body and related anterior segment tissues regulate aqueous humor production, and β-adrenergic antagonists reduce IOP by suppressing aqueous humor secretion; timolol’s nonselective β1/β2 blockade is highly effective for IOP lowering, while β1-selective betaxolol is generally less potent in IOP reduction but carries a reduced risk of β2-mediated respiratory adverse events (Nocentini & Supuran, 2019; Nartey et al., 2021). Beyond IOP, betaxolol displays neuroprotective properties in ocular models, including slowing of retinal changes after ischemia/reperfusion injury—a finding that suggests at least some β-blockers can confer direct retinal cell benefits independent of aqueous suppression, potentially via calcium channel modulation or other non-adrenergic mechanisms (Grieshaber & Flammer, 2010; Nartey et al., 2021). In posterior segment disease, β-adrenergic modulation has been hypothesized to affect angiogenesis and neural damage processes, with propranolol historically associated with ocular vascular lesion regression and preliminary trials pivoting toward exploring β1-selective metoprolol intravitreally due to formulation advantages; within this frame, β-ARs constitute a therapeutic target space extending beyond anterior segment fluid dynamics (Chaves et al., 2021).

Pharmacokinetic and safety considerations  
Metoprolol is extensively metabolized by CYP2D6, with genotype and drug–drug interactions modulating exposure; while these features are central to systemic administration, intravitreal delivery aims to limit systemic exposure, and early phase I data reported no significant changes in systemic blood pressure, suggesting minimal hemodynamic spillover at the tested ocular dose (Chaves et al., 2021; Opie, 2013). Importantly, an animal study with intravitreal propranolol (not metoprolol) detected photopic ERG amplitude reductions in rabbits at 28 days, emphasizing the necessity of agent- and dose-specific retinal electrophysiologic safety evaluation for β-blockers administered intraocularly; metoprolol at 50 μg intravitreally did not demonstrate ERG toxicity in small human series and rabbits, but long-term and repeated-dose safety remain untested (Chaves et al., 2021).

Relevance to RPE biology and dry AMD  
The supplied literature confirms that metoprolol is not toxic to RPE cells in vitro and that systemic metoprolol has neuroprotective effects in a rat retinal degeneration model; however, the materials do not document β1-AR expression in RPE, metoprolol’s modulation of RPE cAMP/PKA signaling, or effects on actin cytoskeleton dynamics, phagocytic cup formation, or photoreceptor outer segment (POS) internalization in RPE (Chaves et al., 2021). Likewise, while β-adrenergic pharmacology is invoked as a potential target in retinal vascular disease and ocular tumors, no evidence is provided here linking β1-selective antagonism to enhanced RPE phagocytic function, reduced cytoskeletal stiffness, or amelioration of dry AMD pathology, and no clinical evaluations in dry AMD are presented in the supplied texts (Böhm et al., 2023; Chaves et al., 2021).

Expected Effect  
Assay-level expectation under the proposed hypothesis  
The proposal hypothesizes that metoprolol, via β1-AR blockade in RPE, will reduce cAMP levels, alleviate cAMP-driven cytoskeletal stiffness, enhance actin remodeling, and thereby improve POS phagocytosis under stress conditions (e.g., in ARPE-19 assays), with an IC50 in the tens of nanomolar range; the supplied literature does not include data validating β1-AR expression in RPE, metoprolol’s potency at RPE β1-AR, or its effects on RPE actin dynamics or phagocytic cup formation (Chaves et al., 2021). The available ocular evidence base for metoprolol consists of safety-oriented intravitreal studies in choroidal hemangioma, in vitro RPE non-toxicity, and class-level ocular pharmacology centered on aqueous humor production and neuroprotective properties of betaxolol rather than RPE phagocytosis (Chaves et al., 2021; Nartey et al., 2021). Therefore, while β-adrenergic GPCR blockade would be expected to reduce cAMP in β1-expressing cells, the downstream link from cAMP reduction to RPE cytoskeletal remodeling and increased phagocytosis is not substantiated by the provided materials; accordingly, the expected effect in a phagocytosis assay remains speculative given the evidence presented here (Böhm et al., 2023).

Cellular expression and disease relevance  
The hypothesis requires that β1-AR is expressed and functionally coupled in the RPE, which is not documented in the present set of sources; instead, the ocular β-AR literature provided focuses on ciliary body (aqueous secretion) and, more broadly, retinal neuroprotection and vascular modulation at the tissue level (Grieshaber & Flammer, 2010; Nocentini & Supuran, 2019). Although metoprolol’s lack of RPE cytotoxicity supports feasibility for RPE-directed assays, translation to dry AMD hinges on improving RPE-mediated processes relevant to disease (e.g., POS clearance), which are not addressed in the current citations; moreover, no dry AMD or geographic atrophy outcomes have been studied with metoprolol in clinical or preclinical AMD models in the provided context (Chaves et al., 2021).

Delivery and dosing  
Given the anterior segment systemic absorption risks of topical β-blockers and the desire to target posterior RPE, intravitreal delivery is mechanistically attractive; the phase I trial indicates that a 50 μg/0.05 mL intravitreal dose is acutely tolerable with no ERG toxicity at four weeks, slight reductions in retinal thickness in chronic choroidal hemangioma, and no meaningful systemic blood pressure changes, which collectively support feasibility of posterior-segment dosing for exploratory mechanistic studies (Chaves et al., 2021). However, dose–response, repeat-dose safety, and long-term retinal electrophysiological effects remain unresolved and are particularly important given the ERG effects observed with intravitreal propranolol in animals, underscoring the necessity of cautious, stepwise dose escalation and comprehensive functional readouts if proceeding (Chaves et al., 2021).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
- Known pharmacology and druggability: Metoprolol is a well-characterized β1-selective antagonist with decades of systemic clinical experience and a clear molecular mechanism of action centered on β1-AR antagonism of Gs–AC–cAMP signaling; its class has demonstrable ocular pharmacology, establishing druggability of ocular β-adrenergic targets (Böhm et al., 2023; Nocentini & Supuran, 2019).  
- Ocular feasibility and early safety: Intravitreal metoprolol tartrate at 50 μg/0.05 mL has early human safety signals (no ERG toxicity, no inflammation, no VA deterioration at four weeks) and parallel animal data without acute retinal toxicity at 50–100 μg, supporting the feasibility of posterior-segment delivery for mechanistic or efficacy studies (Chaves et al., 2021).  
- RPE tolerability and retinal neuroprotection signals: In vitro, metoprolol did not exhibit toxicity to RPE cells, and in vivo systemic dosing showed neuroprotective effects in a rat retinal degeneration model, suggesting a favorable biological profile in retinal cell contexts, albeit not AMD-specific (Chaves et al., 2021).  
- Ocular β-blocker precedent with neuroprotective actions: Betaxolol (β1-selective) exhibits neuroprotective effects in ocular models beyond IOP lowering, indicating that at least some β1 blockers can confer retinal cell protection via mechanisms that may be independent of aqueous suppression, a conceptual precedent for exploring β1-targeted biology in posterior ocular tissues (Grieshaber & Flammer, 2010; Nartey et al., 2021).

Weaknesses and key evidence gaps for dry AMD  
- Absence of RPE β1-AR functional data: The central hypothesis requires β1-AR expression and functional coupling in RPE regulating cAMP-dependent actin dynamics and phagocytosis; the provided literature does not document β1-AR expression in RPE, nor metoprolol effects on RPE actin remodeling, phagocytic cup formation, or POS internalization, making the mechanistic premise unsubstantiated by the present citations (Chaves et al., 2021).  
- No dry AMD–specific preclinical or clinical data: There are no dry AMD or geographic atrophy studies—preclinical or clinical—using metoprolol in the provided materials; consequently, disease relevance and translational signal are absent (Chaves et al., 2021; Nartey et al., 2021).  
- Limited intravitreal safety characterization: Human intravitreal data are phase I, single-dose, and short-term; repeated dosing, long-term ERG stability, and dose–response safety are not assessed, and animal data with intravitreal propranolol reveal potential electrophysiologic risks, necessitating cautious development (Chaves et al., 2021).  
- Class heterogeneity and target specificity: Ocular β-blocker data demonstrating neuroprotection are centered on betaxolol; translating these effects to metoprolol without mechanistic support may be nontrivial given potential off-target (e.g., calcium channel) contributions unique to specific molecules (Grieshaber & Flammer, 2010; Nartey et al., 2021).  
- Systemic exposure considerations in non-intravitreal routes: Topical β-blockers can produce clinically significant systemic adverse effects via nasolacrimal absorption; while intravitreal dosing mitigates this risk, any exploration of topical delivery to RPE would require careful systemic safety mitigation (Vander Zanden et al., 2001; Nocentini & Supuran, 2019).

Risk–benefit and development implications  
On balance, metoprolol has an encouraging early ocular safety profile by intravitreal administration at low dose and a favorable RPE tolerability signal, but the mechanistic and disease-relevant evidence for dry AMD is currently insufficient to justify efficacy-focused clinical development; instead, a staged preclinical program to test the RPE phagocytosis hypothesis is warranted before advancing (Chaves et al., 2021). Specifically:  
- Mechanism validation: Confirm β1-AR expression and signaling in human RPE (primary RPE and ARPE-19), quantify cAMP responses to β-agonists and metoprolol antagonism, and interrogate actin dynamics and phagocytic cup formation using live-cell imaging and POS uptake assays under stress paradigms relevant to RPE dysfunction; no such evidence is provided in the current literature set (Chaves et al., 2021).  
- Safety pharmacology: Extend intravitreal safety to repeated dosing and longer follow-up with ERG, OCT, microperimetry, and visual function endpoints, including dose-ranging to define a minimal biologically active and maximal tolerated intravitreal exposure; the phase I trial highlights these gaps explicitly (Chaves et al., 2021).  
- Disease-model relevance: In the absence of dry AMD data, test efficacy in retinal degeneration or RPE stress models that recapitulate phagocytic impairment and RPE atrophy features, while monitoring for neuroprotective or anti-inflammatory readouts suggested by class literature; the existing animal work with systemic metoprolol in retinal degeneration supports feasibility but is not disease-specific (Chaves et al., 2021).

Given the present evidence, metoprolol should be considered a plausible exploratory tool compound for posterior-segment β1-AR biology in the retina/RPE with a preliminary intravitreal safety margin at low doses, but not yet a validated therapeutic candidate for dry AMD; only if RPE β1-AR signaling is shown to regulate phagocytosis and actin remodeling in a way that metoprolol favorably modulates and if such effects translate to structural/functional benefit in relevant models should a dry AMD program proceed (Chaves et al., 2021; Nartey et al., 2021). Until then, the proposed mechanism—cAMP-driven cytoskeletal modulation to enhance POS internalization by RPE via β1 blockade—remains hypothetical in the context of the supplied literature, and the development path should prioritize rigorous mechanistic and safety groundwork before considering clinical translation (Böhm et al., 2023; Chaves et al., 2021).
