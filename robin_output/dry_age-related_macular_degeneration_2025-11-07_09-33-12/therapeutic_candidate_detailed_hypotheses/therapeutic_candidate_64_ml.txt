Proposal for ML-SA1 (TRPML1 agonist; Tocris Cat. No. 5336)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
ML‐SA1 is a fully synthetic small molecule designed as an agonist of the transient receptor potential mucolipin 1 (TRPML1) channel. It belongs to the class of mucolipin synthetic agonists, which were initially derived as analogs of earlier lead compounds such as SF‐51 and SF‐22. These compounds were discovered using high‐throughput screening approaches aimed at identifying activators of TRPML channels, and ML‐SA1 was subsequently optimized for improved potency and selectivity in mammalian systems (Feng et al., 2014; Shen et al., 2012). As a synthetic compound, ML‐SA1 exemplifies a rational drug design strategy in which the molecule is engineered to mimic the action of the endogenous lipid agonist phosphatidylinositol 3,5‐bisphosphate [PI(3,5)P₂] by directly binding to the channel’s pore region. This binding stabilizes TRPML1 in its open conformation, thereby facilitating lysosomal Ca²⁺ release. The chemical class represented by ML‐SA1 has historically been employed as a research tool to interrogate lysosomal physiology and calcium homeostasis in diverse cell types, including neuronal and retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells (Gómez et al., 2018).  

Therapeutic History:  
Previous studies have demonstrated that ML‐SA1 robustly elevates cytosolic calcium levels in several cellular models by activating TRPML1 channels. In particular, ML‐SA1 has been applied to multiple RPE cell models—including mouse RPE, human embryonic stem cell‐derived RPE, and the ARPE‐19 cell line—to assess its ability to restore lysosomal calcium signaling and normalize lysosomal function (Gómez et al., 2018). In these experiments, ML‐SA1 elicited rapid, robust, and reversible Ca²⁺ signals that were completely absent in TRPML1 knockout cells, thereby confirming target engagement and specificity (Gómez et al., 2018). Beyond RPE biology, ML‐SA1 has been evaluated in neuronal models of lysosomal dysfunction, including experimental systems relevant to Parkinson’s disease and Alzheimer’s disease, where it mediated correction of defective lysosomal acidification and improved autophagic flux (Hui et al., 2019; Pollmanns et al., 2022). Although no clinical trials have yet investigated ML‐SA1 directly in patients with dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD), its demonstrated efficacy in rescuing lysosomal Ca²⁺ signaling—in conditions that share underlying pathophysiological features with dry AMD such as lysosomal storage and autophagy blockade—suggests that its repurposing may yield therapeutic benefits in this disease context (Open Targets Platform, 2025). To date, the use of ML‐SA1 has predominantly been within experimental research settings rather than in clinical practice or veterinary medicine, and its repurposing for dry AMD represents an innovative and rationale‐driven approach rooted in the overlap between TRPML1 dysfunction and RPE pathology (Feng et al., 2014; Cao et al., 2017).  

Mechanism of Action:  
The therapeutic action of ML‐SA1 is centered upon its ability to directly activate TRPML1 channels located on lysosomal membranes. TRPML1 plays a critical role in regulating lysosomal calcium homeostasis by mediating Ca²⁺ efflux from the lysosomal lumen into the cytosol. ML‐SA1 binds directly to TRPML1—likely within a hydrophobic pocket formed at the S5–S6 transmembrane domain interface—and stabilizes the channel’s open state (Feng et al., 2014). This activation occurs independently of exogenous PI(3,5)P₂ in mammalian cells, setting ML‐SA1 apart from the endogenous regulatory mechanisms that otherwise depend on dynamic fluctuations in phosphoinositide levels (Feng et al., 2014; Gómez et al., 2018). As a result of channel activation, lysosomal Ca²⁺ is released into the cytosol, triggering a cascade of calcium‐dependent signaling pathways. One critical downstream effect is the promotion of lysosomal membrane fission and fusion events, processes that are essential for proper lysosomal trafficking and the maturation of autophagosomes into autolysosomes (Cao et al., 2017; Li & Li, 2022).  

Beyond its immediate role in calcium mobilization, ML‐SA1‐mediated activation of TRPML1 also facilitates the activation of transcription factor EB (TFEB), a master regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy. Increased cytosolic Ca²⁺ can activate calcineurin, which in turn dephosphorylates TFEB, enabling its translocation to the nucleus where it upregulates the expression of genes involved in lysosomal function and autophagy (Pollmanns et al., 2022). In some models, ML‐SA1 treatment has been shown to improve the processing and activity of lysosomal enzymes such as cathepsin D, thereby contributing to normalization of lysosomal pH and enhanced protein degradation (Hui et al., 2019). Importantly, the ability of ML‐SA1 to restore lysosomal Ca²⁺ signaling directly targets the pathological cascade observed when TRPML1 is deficient or inhibited—a scenario that is reminiscent of the lysosomal storage, impaired autophagy, and stalled phagolysosomal degradation evident in dry AMD RPE cells (Gómez et al., 2018; Pamonag, 2021).  

Expected Effect:  
Based on the observed mechanisms in diverse cellular models, it is expected that ML‐SA1 will re‐establish normal lysosomal function in RPE cells afflicted by dry AMD. In the proposed assay, the activation of TRPML1 by ML‐SA1 at concentrations near its EC₅₀ (approximately 5 µM) should restore lysosomal Ca²⁺ efflux, thereby driving several beneficial downstream effects. First, the induced calcium release is anticipated to promote lysosomal membrane dynamics that favor fission and fusion events, ultimately leading to improved lysosomal trafficking and an enhanced capacity for phagolysosomal degradation. This is particularly relevant for the degradation of photoreceptor outer segments (POS), the routine phagocytic burden of RPE cells that, when inadequately processed, contributes to lipofuscin accumulation and retinal degeneration (Gómez et al., 2018).  

Second, by normalizing Ca²⁺ signaling, ML‐SA1 is expected to reinstate proper lysosomal acidification. Acidification is crucial for the optimal activity of lysosomal enzymes such as cathepsin D; ML‐SA1 has been shown in various models to restore cathepsin D processing, thereby resetting the enzymatic degradation cascade necessary for POS turnover (Hui et al., 2019). Such restoration of acidification and enzyme activity would directly counteract the pathological accumulation of undegraded material—a hallmark of dry AMD.  

Third, the downstream activation of TFEB induced by ML‐SA1’s modulation of lysosomal calcium may trigger an upregulation of the entire lysosomal-autophagy gene network. This transcriptional reprogramming is vital for augmenting both the number and function of lysosomes, thereby enhancing the overall cellular clearance capacity in RPE cells. Because ample evidence suggests that TRPML1 is expressed in RPE cells and that its dysfunction contributes directly to the autophagy block observed in dry AMD (Pamonag, 2021; Gómez et al., 2018), the activation of this pathway by ML‐SA1 is expected to result in a net improvement in POS degradation and a reduction in toxic debris accumulation. Ultimately, the integrated effects of improved lysosomal trafficking, normalized pH, enhanced enzymatic processing, and boosted autophagic flux are hypothesized to alleviate key pathological features of dry AMD, potentially slowing or even reversing degenerative changes in the retinal pigment epithelium (Gómez et al., 2018).  

Overall Evaluation:  
Our comprehensive literature review indicates that ML‐SA1 is a scientifically compelling candidate for repurposing in the treatment of dry age‐related macular degeneration. One of its principal strengths lies in its well‐defined mechanism of action: by directly activating TRPML1 channels, ML‐SA1 has been shown to restore lysosomal Ca²⁺ homeostasis—a central defect in several models of lysosomal storage and autophagy dysfunction (Gómez et al., 2018; Feng et al., 2014). The robust experimental data obtained in RPE cell models and other relevant systems supports the hypothesis that ML‐SA1 can reverse deficits in lysosomal trafficking, acidification, and autophagosome maturation. The compound’s ability to elicit rapid, reproducible, and reversible Ca²⁺ signals is particularly encouraging, as it suggests that pharmacological modulation of TRPML1 can be finely tuned through dosing adjustments (Gómez et al., 2018; Cao et al., 2017).  

Furthermore, the extensive research demonstrating ML‐SA1’s effects in cellular models of lysosomal storage diseases—including those markers that mimic the pathological lipid accumulation and autophagy impairment found in dry AMD—provides additional rationale for its application in RPE cells (Feng et al., 2014; Pollmanns et al., 2022; Hui et al., 2019). The fact that ML‐SA1 has also been evaluated in non-ocular contexts (such as neuronal models of Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s diseases) where it facilitated autophagic clearance and improved lysosomal functions underscores the versatility of its mechanism and hints at broader therapeutic utility (Hui et al., 2019; Pollmanns et al., 2022).  

Nonetheless, several challenges must be acknowledged. One notable weakness is that ML‐SA1’s efficacy can be compromised by lysosomal lipid accumulation—a common feature of advanced dry AMD. Studies suggest that in cells with significant lipid deposition, such as those accumulating sphingomyelin or other inhibitory lipids, the activation of TRPML1 by ML‐SA1 is attenuated (Gómez et al., 2018). This raises concerns that in patients with advanced dry AMD, the therapeutic window may be narrower or may require combination strategies to first reduce lipid burden. Additionally, while the in vitro EC₅₀ is promising (around 5 µM), there remains uncertainty regarding the pharmacokinetic profile, bioavailability, tissue penetration (particularly across the blood‐retina barrier), and potential off-target effects of ML‐SA1 when administered in vivo (Li & Li, 2022).  

Another area of concern is the current lack of direct clinical data pertaining to ML‐SA1 in dry AMD. Although preclinical studies in RPE cell models provide a solid foundation, translation to in vivo models of AMD—and eventually to human clinical trials—will require additional investigation. The absence of any reported clinical trials specifically targeting dry AMD with TRPML1 agonists (as confirmed by database searches such as those reflected in the Open Targets Platform) indicates that this is still a largely uncharted therapeutic area requiring further validation (Open Targets Platform, 2025). Moreover, chronic activation of TRPML1 by ML‐SA1 may have unforeseen consequences related to intracellular calcium homeostasis and autophagic balance, which warrant careful evaluation in long-term studies (Hui et al., 2019; Pollmanns et al., 2022).  

In summary, ML‐SA1 represents a highly promising candidate for the repurposing in dry AMD based on its ability to rescue lysosomal Ca²⁺ signaling, enhance autophagic clearance, and restore normal lysosomal pH and trafficking in RPE cells. Its synthetic origin and well‐characterized mechanism provide a strong mechanistic rationale for its use. However, issues related to lipid-mediated inhibition of TRPML1, pharmacokinetic considerations, and the current lack of in vivo data in AMD models must be addressed. Overall, the candidate shows significant potential, but a focused program of preclinical validation—including animal models of dry AMD, detailed pharmacokinetic and toxicological profiling, and exploration of strategies to mitigate lysosomal lipid accumulation—is essential before advancing ML‐SA1 to clinical trials for dry AMD (Gómez et al., 2018; Feng et al., 2014).
