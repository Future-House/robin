Proposal for AMD3100 (CXCR4 antagonist; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. A5602)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
AMD3100 (also known as plerixafor) is a small‐molecule, bicyclam compound originally discovered and synthesized as a CXCR4 antagonist. It belongs to a class of compounds designed to block the interaction between the chemokine ligand SDF‐1 (CXCL12) and its receptor CXCR4. This class of compounds was initially developed for their antiviral potential—specifically to inhibit HIV‐1 entry into cells—because CXCR4 serves as a co‐receptor for viral entry. Subsequent studies showed that through its high affinity for CXCR4 (with IC₅₀ values in the low nanomolar range, and our current hypothesis citing ~20 nM in RPE cells), AMD3100 can effectively block the downstream signaling of the SDF‐1/CXCR4 axis. This signaling cascade is known to regulate key cellular processes such as chemotaxis, cell survival, proliferation, and inflammatory gene expression. With its synthetic origin and established profile as a CXCR4 antagonist, AMD3100 has been repurposed beyond its initial anti‐HIV goals to include hematopoietic stem cell mobilization and experimental oncology. Its potential re‐utilization in ocular diseases, particularly in dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD), capitalizes on its ability to modulate inflammatory signaling pathways implicated in retinal degeneration (Tahirovic et al., 2020; Smoragiewicz, 2019).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, AMD3100 emerged from research aimed at blocking HIV infection, but its clinical development pivoted after it was found to robustly mobilize hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells from the bone marrow. This discovery led to its approval by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as an adjuvant therapy for autologous stem cell transplantation in patients with non‐Hodgkin’s lymphoma and multiple myeloma. Its use in stem cell mobilization has been thoroughly characterized in clinical trials with documented pharmacokinetics and a reasonable safety profile in systemic administration (Tahirovic et al., 2020; Smoragiewicz, 2019). In addition to these applications, AMD3100 has been investigated in various preclinical models of cancer, where its inhibition of CXCR4-mediated signaling has been linked to reduced tumor cell invasion, decreased angiogenesis, and altered immune cell trafficking (Andtbacka et al., 2022; Cho et al., 2013). In the ocular context, although AMD3100 has not been used directly for treating dry AMD, its effects on CXCR4 signaling have been addressed in studies focusing on glaucomatous models. For example, investigations in trabecular meshwork cells from glaucomatous tissue have demonstrated that AMD3100, when administered at 1 mM concentration, completely abrogates the protective effects of CXCL12 and inadvertently induces apoptosis, highlighting the dual role of CXCR4 signaling in ocular tissues (Denoyer et al., 2012). Moreover, systemic administration studies in non‐ocular models such as the SOD1^G93A ALS mouse model have shown that chronic AMD3100 treatment can yield neuroprotective and anti‐inflammatory benefits (Rabinovich‐Nikitin et al., 2016), which further adds to its therapeutic appeal but also underscores the importance of tissue-specific dosing and context. These historical uses underscore AMD3100’s well-defined pharmacological profile while simultaneously illustrating that translating its benefits to the RPE in dry AMD will require reconciling its established systemic effects with the often finely balanced roles of the CXCR4 axis in retinal tissue repair and inflammation (Cai et al., 2014; Saiman et al., 2015).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, AMD3100 exerts its primary pharmacological action by selectively binding to the CXCR4 receptor and competitively inhibiting the binding of its natural ligand, SDF‐1 (CXCL12). In doing so, it prevents receptor activation and the subsequent engagement of intracellular signaling cascades mediated by G-protein coupled receptor activation. The downstream effects typically include modulation of pathways such as PI3K/Akt, Ras/ERK, and, in many cases, alterations in intracellular calcium fluxes that contribute to chemotaxis and cell survival (Bai et al., 2016; Colasanti et al., 2025). By inhibiting these cascades, AMD3100 hinders not only the migration of cells toward SDF‐1 gradients but also the pro‐inflammatory and pro‐angiogenic gene expression programs that are otherwise activated upon CXCR4 engagement. In cancer models, this mechanism translates into reduced tumor cell invasion and inhibition of angiogenesis, while in immune cells it modulates chemotactic responses and inflammatory cell trafficking (Cho et al., 2013). Importantly, CXCR4 is expressed in multiple ocular cell types, including retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells. In RPE, the SDF‐1/CXCR4 axis has been implicated in cellular migration, cytokine secretion, and potentially in the regulation of the phagocytic machinery necessary for the clearance of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) (Enzmann et al., 2017; Canonica et al., 2021). The hypothesis driving the current proposal suggests that chronic activation of CXCR4 in RPE cells, possibly driven by persistent inflammatory stimuli such as TNF‐α, leads to a pro‐inflammatory state that downregulates key phagocytic receptors like MERTK and compromises lysosomal enzyme activity. By blocking the binding of SDF‐1 to CXCR4, AMD3100 is expected to interrupt this deleterious signaling cascade, resulting in normalization of MERTK expression and lysosomal enzyme levels, and thereby restoring the essential phagocytic function of the RPE (Colasanti et al., 2025). However, the complexity of CXCR4 signaling is underscored by studies in other ocular tissues where its inhibition has led to apoptosis of trabecular cells, suggesting that the outcome of CXCR4 blockade may be highly dependent on the cell type and the local microenvironment (Denoyer et al., 2012). Therefore, while the mechanistic rationale is strong, careful evaluation of AMD3100’s specific molecular interactions in RPE cells will be required to delineate beneficial effects from potential adverse outcomes.

Expected Effect:  
The proposed therapeutic strategy hypothesizes that the blockade of CXCR4 by AMD3100 in RPE cells will lead to two major beneficial outcomes in the context of dry age-related macular degeneration: (1) inhibition of pro-inflammatory signaling that otherwise promotes aberrant cellular migration and deleterious inflammatory gene expression, and (2) restoration of the phagocytic capacity of RPE cells through upregulation of key receptors and enzymes necessary for the efficient clearance of photoreceptor outer segments (POS). In dry AMD, a hallmark of disease progression is the accumulation of cellular debris and the failure of RPE cells to effectively clear POS due to impaired phagocytosis—a process that is critical for sustaining photoreceptor health and visual function. Evidence suggests that chronic activation of the SDF‐1/CXCR4 axis in the retinal microenvironment can lead to elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines and subsequent downregulation of MERTK, a receptor tyrosine kinase essential for the recognition and internalization of shed POS (Enzmann et al., 2017; Canonica et al., 2021). In vitro models, such as TNF‐α–treated ARPE-19 cells, have demonstrated that inflammatory stress leads to diminished phagocytic activity, and it is hypothesized that blocking CXCR4 signaling with AMD3100 could counteract this effect by normalizing MERTK levels and restoring lysosomal function. In an ideal scenario, treatment with AMD3100 would reverse the pro-inflammatory signaling cascade by preventing SDF‐1 binding, reduce aberrant RPE migration, and allow the cells to maintain their structural polarity and phagocytic efficiency. Importantly, the RPE expresses CXCR4 receptors on their surface, as demonstrated by immunocytochemical studies, and the receptor’s activation has been linked to altered cellular motility and inflammatory responses (Canonica et al., 2021; Enzmann et al., 2017). The expected pharmacodynamic outcome is that, upon AMD3100 treatment, RPE cells under inflammatory stress conditions would exhibit increased expression of MERTK and lysosomal enzymes, leading to improved clearance of damaged photoreceptor outer segments, which is critical for photoreceptor survival and overall retinal health. While data from other cell types suggest the possibility of safely modulating these pathways, caution is warranted given the evidence from trabecular meshwork cells where CXCR4 antagonism disrupted protective signaling (Denoyer et al., 2012). Hence, the key to success will lie in establishing the proper dosing and delivery mechanisms that provide sufficient blockade of deleterious CXCR4 signaling in RPE without compromising the receptor’s potentially beneficial roles in tissue repair and homeostasis.

Overall Evaluation:  
Overall, AMD3100 presents an intriguing candidate for repurposing as a therapeutic for dry age-related macular degeneration given its well-defined mechanism of action, extensive preclinical and clinical history, and a known safety profile in non-ocular indications such as hematopoietic stem cell mobilization and cancer therapy (Tahirovic et al., 2020; Smoragiewicz, 2019). The central hypothesis—that chronic SDF‐1/CXCR4 signaling in RPE cells contributes to inflammation-driven downregulation of MERTK and consequent impairment of POS phagocytosis—addresses a critical pathogenic mechanism in dry AMD. Blocking this pathway with AMD3100 could, in theory, restore the phagocytic machinery of the RPE, thereby reducing the accumulation of toxic cellular debris and slowing disease progression (Colasanti et al., 2025).

One of the strengths of AMD3100 as a therapeutic candidate lies in its extensive clinical use and established pharmacokinetic profile; its FDA approval for stem cell mobilization means that much is already known about its systemic effects, dosing parameters, and potential toxicities when administered appropriately (Tahirovic et al., 2020; Rabinovich-Nikitin et al., 2016). Moreover, the molecular mechanism—competitive antagonism at the CXCR4 receptor—has been demonstrated across multiple studies and various cell types, providing a robust theoretical framework for its use in modulating pathological signaling cascades in RPE cells (Bai et al., 2016; Colasanti et al., 2025). The concept of repurposing a well-characterized agent such as AMD3100 for dry AMD is attractive from a drug development standpoint because it could potentially shorten the translational timeline and reduce development risks related to novel chemical entities.

However, there are significant challenges and weaknesses that must be addressed before AMD3100 can be considered a promising candidate for dry AMD. First and foremost, the role of SDF‐1/CXCR4 signaling in ocular tissues appears to be multifaceted. While in RPE cells chronic activation may indeed drive a pro-inflammatory and phagocytosis-impairing phenotype, in other ocular tissues such as the trabecular meshwork, CXCR4 signaling has been shown to exert cytoprotective effects. Studies in glaucomatous trabecular cells have demonstrated that blockade of CXCR4 by AMD3100 not only abrogates the beneficial actions of CXCL12 but may also actively induce apoptosis, highlighting a potential risk that the protective aspects of CXCR4 signaling could be compromised (Denoyer et al., 2012). This tissue-specific duality raises concerns that non-selective or systemic inhibition of CXCR4 might lead to unintended adverse effects in the retina and other ocular compartments. Therefore, any consideration of AMD3100 for dry AMD will require extremely careful dosing studies and potentially localized delivery strategies that avoid systemic over-blockade.

Another key consideration is the current lack of direct evidence supporting the efficacy of AMD3100 in restoring MERTK expression or lysosomal enzyme levels in RPE cells, specifically in the context of dry AMD. While preliminary in vitro observations in TNF‐α–treated ARPE-19 cells are promising, these studies remain preliminary, and the translation of these effects into meaningful functional outcomes in vivo has yet to be established. The complexity of dry AMD pathogenesis—which involves not only inflammatory processes but also oxidative stress, complement dysregulation, and altered cellular metabolism—means that targeting a single pathway may not be sufficient to recapitulate the full spectrum of therapeutic benefit required for clinical efficacy (Colasanti et al., 2025).

Furthermore, the potential for AMD3100 to affect cell migration and even cellular differentiation via CXCR4 inhibition might have unpredictable consequences on the RPE’s capacity for repair. For instance, the SDF‐1/CXCR4 axis has been implicated in the recruitment of bone marrow–derived progenitor cells to sites of RPE injury, a process that is thought to contribute to endogenous repair mechanisms (Enzmann et al., 2017; Canonica et al., 2021). Inhibiting CXCR4 could inadvertently interfere with these reparative processes if not finely tuned. It is, therefore, imperative that any preclinical evaluation of AMD3100 for dry AMD includes rigorous assessments of not only phagocytic function and inflammatory profile but also the impact on RPE cell survival, differentiation, and regenerative capacity.

In addition to cell-specific concerns, the delivery method for AMD3100 in the context of dry AMD is another critical factor. The compound has been used systemically, but ocular diseases typically require either intravitreal or other localized delivery methods to achieve therapeutic concentrations while minimizing systemic exposure. Prior studies involving AMD3100 in ocular models—mostly in the context of glaucoma—have used subconjunctival injections at relatively high concentrations (e.g., 1 mM), which resulted in localized apoptotic effects (Denoyer et al., 2012). This suggests that the concentration and mode of delivery will need to be carefully optimized for the RPE to ensure that sufficient drug levels are achieved to block aberrant CXCR4 signaling without tipping the balance of protective chemokine functions.

Moreover, while the preclinical data from non-ocular models such as cancer and ALS are encouraging regarding the anti-inflammatory and anti-migratory effects of AMD3100 (Rabinovich-Nikitin et al., 2016; Andtbacka et al., 2022), these contexts differ significantly from the microenvironment of the aging retina. The retinal tissue, particularly in dry AMD, is characterized by complex interactions among the RPE, photoreceptors, Bruch’s membrane, and the choriocapillaris. The interplay of these structures with age-related oxidative stress and immune dysregulation means that any intervention must be evaluated in models that faithfully recapitulate these conditions. There is a paucity of direct literature on the use of AMD3100 in dry AMD models, which suggests that a substantial body of preclinical work is required before a clear signal of benefit can be ascertained.

In summary, AMD3100 has significant strengths as a candidate for repurposing in dry AMD. Its well-documented mechanism of competitive antagonism at CXCR4, combined with an established clinical safety profile in systemic applications and a clear rationale for targeting the SDF‐1/CXCR4 axis in inflammatory conditions, supports further investigation of its use in the retinal pigment epithelium (Tahirovic et al., 2020; Smoragiewicz, 2019; Bai et al., 2016). However, key weaknesses include evidence from ocular models that indicate potential cytotoxicity when protective CXCR4 signals are indiscriminately blocked (Denoyer et al., 2012), as well as a lack of direct preclinical data in dry AMD or RPE-specific phagocytic assays. These concerns necessitate a cautious yet exploratory approach, with initial studies focused on establishing the optimal local dosing regimen, confirming that AMD3100 can indeed restore MERTK expression and phagocytic function in ARPE-19 cell models, and ultimately validating these findings in animal models of dry AMD that incorporate stressors such as TNF‐α or oxidative injury.

Overall Evaluation:  
In conclusion, while AMD3100 stands as one of the most thoroughly characterized CXCR4 antagonists with a solid clinical and preclinical background, its application to dry AMD remains largely untested. The theoretical basis—that chronic SDF‐1/CXCR4 activation in RPE drives inflammatory gene expression leading to diminished phagocytic capacity, and that blocking this pathway will restore homeostatic functions—is compelling and addresses a known pathogenic mechanism in dry AMD (Colasanti et al., 2025). The strengths of AMD3100 include its potent CXCR4 binding affinity, the wealth of pharmacological data supporting its mode of action, and its demonstrated ability to modulate inflammatory and migratory responses in various tissues. Moreover, the clinical safety data from its use in hematopoietic stem cell mobilization provide some reassurance regarding its tolerability when used in appropriate dosing regimens (Tahirovic et al., 2020; Smoragiewicz, 2019).

On the other hand, several significant weaknesses must be acknowledged. Studies in ocular models have shown that CXCR4 blockade can eliminate protective signals that are crucial for cell survival, as evidenced by the induction of apoptosis in trabecular meshwork cells when treated with AMD3100 at high concentrations (Denoyer et al., 2012). In addition, there is a risk that by inhibiting CXCR4 signaling, AMD3100 might interfere with endogenous repair mechanisms mediated by the recruitment of bone marrow–derived progenitor cells, a process that may be beneficial in RPE regeneration (Enzmann et al., 2017; Canonica et al., 2021). The lack of direct data demonstrating restoration of MERTK and improved phagocytosis in RPE cells further complicates the evaluation. Because dry AMD is multifactorial, it is unclear whether modulation of a single pathway—albeit an important one—will be sufficient to yield a meaningful clinical benefit without causing collateral damage to other aspects of RPE function.

Given these considerations, our recommendation is to pursue rigorous preclinical studies specifically tailored to dry AMD. These studies should encompass detailed in vitro analyses using ARPE-19 and primary human RPE cells under inflammatory and oxidative stress conditions to validate whether AMD3100 can reliably restore MERTK expression and lysosomal function, thereby enhancing POS clearance. It will also be critical to establish the appropriate concentration window that achieves therapeutic blockade of deleterious signaling while preserving necessary CXCR4-mediated protective effects. Parallel in vivo studies in animal models of dry AMD should focus on local ocular delivery methods—potentially through intravitreal or controlled-release formulations—to minimize systemic exposure and optimize retinal bioavailability.

In conclusion, AMD3100 represents an exciting and scientifically rational candidate for repurposing in dry AMD based on its mechanism of CXCR4 antagonism and the hypothesized link between chronic SDF‐1/CXCR4 activation and impaired RPE phagocytosis. However, given the mixed evidence from ocular safety studies and the complexity of CXCR4 signaling in retinal homeostasis, further systematic preclinical evaluation is essential before advancing to clinical trials. The balance between inhibiting pathological inflammation and preserving or even harnessing protective regenerative signals will determine the ultimate viability of AMD3100 as a therapeutic agent for dry age‐related macular degeneration (Denoyer et al., 2012; Rabinovich-Nikitin et al., 2016; Bai et al., 2016).
