Proposal for Fasudil (Rho kinase inhibitor; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. F5635)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Fasudil is a small‐molecule, synthetic isoquinoline derivative that functions as a Rho‐associated coiled‐coil forming kinase (ROCK) inhibitor. Originally discovered and subsequently developed for clinical use in the treatment of cerebral vasospasm following subarachnoid hemorrhage, fasudil represents one of the earliest examples of ROCK inhibitors entering the clinic (Halasz & Townes-Anderson, 2016). As a member of the ROCK inhibitor class, it has been synthesized and formulated to target the ATP‐binding pocket of both ROCK isoforms (ROCK1 and ROCK2), thereby effectively competing with ATP and suppressing the kinase activity that is normally responsible for phosphorylating downstream molecular substrates such as the myosin light chain (MLC) and LIM kinase (Catral et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2022). The compound’s synthesis generally follows established medicinal chemistry protocols for heterocyclic compounds and includes derivatization steps that yield a potent inhibitor with an IC₅₀ for ROCK inhibition in the submicromolar range, which in the context of retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) function has been postulated to be around 300 nM (Halasz & Townes-Anderson, 2016; Xiao et al., 2022). Fasudil’s chemical structure and its physicochemical properties allow for formulation into ophthalmic preparations, such as intravitreal injections or potentially sustained-release depot systems, making it an interesting candidate for repurposing in ocular diseases. This class of compounds has historically been used to modulate cytoskeletal dynamics, vascular tone, and cellular contractility in both vascular and neural tissues, and the same properties are being leveraged now to target the pathological stiffening and impaired phagocytic function of RPE cells in dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) (Croze et al., 2014; Halasz & Townes-Anderson, 2016).

Therapeutic History:  
Fasudil’s therapeutic history spans multiple clinical and preclinical applications that underscore its broad efficacy in modulating ROCK-mediated pathways. Clinically, fasudil has been approved and widely used in Japan since 1995 for the treatment of cerebral vasospasm after subarachnoid hemorrhage, owing to its vasodilatory and anti-contractile properties (Halasz & Townes-Anderson, 2016). Its clinical use has expanded into experimental paradigms beyond cerebral applications; for instance, fasudil has shown promise in ocular conditions such as diabetic macular edema, where intravitreal administration in combination with bevacizumab demonstrated feasibility and tolerability, and where its ability to modulate inflammatory responses and vascular permeability were beneficial (Mateos-Olivares et al., 2021; Rothschild et al., 2017). Furthermore, other members of the ROCK inhibitor class have been investigated for ocular applications, including glaucoma and even experimental models of photoreceptor synapse stabilization following retinal detachment (Townes-Anderson et al., 2017; Yamaguchi et al., 2017). Although fasudil itself has not yet been directly applied in clinical trials for dry AMD, its mechanism of action and safety profile in ocular tissues have been well documented in multiple studies. Animal studies in diabetic retinopathy models have also demonstrated that fasudil can reverse detrimental cytoskeletal changes and restore barrier integrity in RPE cells (Rothschild et al., 2017). Additionally, preclinical data indicate that ROCK inhibitors can improve wound repair responses and prevent epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in RPE cells, which is a hallmark of cellular dysfunction in geographic atrophy – the advanced stage of dry AMD (Bailey-Steinitz, 2021; Moura-Coelho et al., 2019). Collectively, while fasudil’s historic use has largely focused on vascular and diabetic complications, its repurposing for dry AMD is supported by mechanistic parallels in RPE cytoskeletal plasticity, phagocytosis, and anti-inflammatory modulation (Halasz & Townes-Anderson, 2016; Yamaguchi et al., 2017).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, fasudil acts by binding to the ATP-binding site of ROCK enzymes, thereby competitively inhibiting their kinase activity (Halasz & Townes-Anderson, 2016). Under normal cellular conditions, active ROCK phosphorylates several substrates including the myosin light chain (MLC) and myosin phosphatase target subunit 1 (MYPT1), which leads to enhanced actomyosin contractility, stabilization of actin stress fibers, and formation of focal adhesions (Catral et al., 2024; Rothschild et al., 2017). In the context of age-related retinopathies, there is mounting evidence that hyperactivity of the ROCK pathway leads to increased contractile force within the RPE cytoskeleton. This hypercontractility contributes to reduced cellular flexibility and impairs the formation of the phagocytic cups that are critical for the internalization of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) – a process that is essential for retinal homeostasis and visual cycle maintenance (Bailey-Steinitz, 2021; Rothschild et al., 2017). Fasudil’s inhibition of ROCK thereby mitigates these cytoskeletal alterations by decreasing the phosphorylation levels of downstream targets, which in turn reduces actomyosin contraction and promotes a more plastic and dynamic actin architecture (Catral et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2022).

Furthermore, ROCK inhibition has been shown to enhance cell adhesion and reduce apoptotic signaling in stressed RPE cells, thereby improving wound repair and the overall cellular microenvironment (Bailey-Steinitz, 2021; Moura-Coelho et al., 2019). Biochemical experiments have demonstrated that when ROCK activity is suppressed, RPE cells exhibit a reduction in stress fiber formation and an increase in the formation of normal cell–cell junctions, which is crucial for the maintenance of the outer blood-retinal barrier (Rothschild et al., 2017). In addition to its effects on the actin cytoskeleton, fasudil and other ROCK inhibitors have been reported to modulate inflammatory and fibrotic pathways by reducing the phosphorylation of Smad 2/3 in response to TGF-β1 signaling; this effect may further protect RPE cells from deleterious pro-fibrotic and inflammatory cascades that are known to exacerbate dry AMD pathology (Halasz & Townes-Anderson, 2016; Moura-Coelho et al., 2019). Several studies also indicate that ROCK inhibition promotes RPE differentiation and pigmentation, as evidenced by increased expression of key proteins such as RPE65, which is indispensable for the visual cycle and meso-zeaxanthin biosynthesis (Bailey-Steinitz, 2021). Given that oxidative stress in the aging retina exacerbates ROCK activation, fasudil’s ability to mitigate these effects through cytoskeletal remodeling and anti-inflammatory actions underlies its proposed mechanism of action in improving POS internalization in dry AMD (Halasz & Townes-Anderson, 2016; Rothschild et al., 2017).

Expected Effect:  
The central hypothesis for repurposing fasudil for dry AMD is that inhibition of ROCK in the RPE will lead to normalization of abnormal cytoskeletal tension, thereby enhancing the formation of phagocytic cups required for efficient POS internalization (Bailey-Steinitz, 2021; Rothschild et al., 2017). In aged RPE cells from patients with dry AMD, persistent ROCK hyperactivity is thought to cause increased actomyosin contraction; this leads to a “stiff” cytoskeletal state that hinders membrane dynamics essential for phagocytosis (Halasz & Townes-Anderson, 2016; Rothschild et al., 2017). By reducing ROCK-mediated phosphorylation of MLC and other substrates, fasudil is expected to relieve this contractility, restore a more flexible actin network, and ultimately allow for enhanced phagocytic function. Preclinical studies have already demonstrated that use of ROCK inhibitors like Y-27632 can increase phagocytic activity by RPE cells, leading to improved clearance of POS debris – a key function that deteriorates in dry AMD (Bailey-Steinitz, 2021; Croze et al., 2014).

In the proposed assay, RPE cells under oxidative stress—an in vitro proxy for the aging microenvironment seen in dry AMD—will be treated with fasudil. The anticipated outcome is that fasudil will enhance cytoskeletal plasticity, thereby facilitating the formation of phagocytic cups and improving the uptake of POS, as measured by quantifiable phagocytosis assays (Bailey-Steinitz, 2021; Rothschild et al., 2017). Moreover, fasudil’s reported effects in enhancing cell adhesion and promoting a balanced extracellular matrix deposition are expected to provide additional benefits to RPE cell morphology and barrier integrity, indirectly supporting improved phagocytic function (Moura-Coelho et al., 2019). Given that key RPE-specific genes and proteins involved in phagocytosis, including integrin αvβ5 and MFG-E8, are expressed in these cells, normalization of the cytoskeletal machinery via ROCK inhibition is likely to translate into a functional recovery of POS clearance capacity (Bailey-Steinitz, 2021; Croze et al., 2014). Thus, the overall effect of fasudil treatment is expected to counteract the age-associated impairment in phagocytosis, reduce accumulation of waste material such as drusen, and potentially slow the progression of retinal degeneration in dry AMD (Halasz & Townes-Anderson, 2016).

Overall Evaluation:  
In summary, the repurposing of fasudil for the treatment of dry age-related macular degeneration is supported by a substantial mechanistic rationale and a body of preclinical evidence indicating that ROCK inhibition can positively modulate RPE cellular function. The strengths of fasudil as a therapeutic candidate include its well-established clinical safety profile—stemming from decades of use in cerebrovascular conditions in Japan—as well as its robust capacity to modulate actin-cytoskeletal dynamics, reduce excessive cell contraction, and enhance processes such as cell adhesion and phagocytosis among RPE cells (Catral et al., 2024; Halasz & Townes-Anderson, 2016). The ability of fasudil to normalize the abnormal, stiff cytoskeletal state present in aged RPE cells is particularly compelling given the central role of impaired POS internalization in the pathogenesis of dry AMD (Bailey-Steinitz, 2021; Rothschild et al., 2017).

Moreover, similar ROCK inhibitors have demonstrated promising effects in ocular models of retinal degeneration and diabetic retinopathy, where the restoration of RPE function and cytoskeletal composition has correlated with improved retinal architecture and cell survival (Moura-Coelho et al., 2019; Rothschild et al., 2017). Fasudil’s potential to also attenuate inflammatory and fibrotic signaling pathways via downstream effects on TGF-β/Smad signaling may add further benefit by curbing the detrimental microenvironment that exacerbates RPE dysfunction in dry AMD (Halasz & Townes-Anderson, 2016; Moura-Coelho et al., 2019). The proposed mechanism—centered on relieving ROCK-induced actomyosin contraction and restoring the phagocytic capacity of RPE cells—addresses a key pathophysiological deficit in dry AMD, namely the impaired clearance of photoreceptor outer segments that leads to drusen accumulation and subsequent cell degeneration (Bailey-Steinitz, 2021; Rothschild et al., 2017).

However, there are also some weaknesses and challenges associated with using fasudil as a treatment for dry AMD. A major concern is the relatively nonselective nature of fasudil compared to more recently developed ROCK inhibitors that offer isoform specificity (e.g., selective ROCK2 inhibitors) (Alexander, 2017; Yamaguchi et al., 2017). Since ROCK signaling is implicated in multiple cellular processes beyond RPE cytoskeletal dynamics—including vascular regulation, neuronal survival, and immune cell polarization—a nonselective inhibitor may inadvertently affect these other pathways, potentially leading to off-target effects. In particular, the downregulation of thrombospondin 1 (THBS1) observed with some ROCK inhibitors, which is associated with anti-angiogenic processes, could theoretically predispose patients to neovascular complications if not carefully controlled (Bailey-Steinitz, 2021; Catral et al., 2024).

Another potential limitation is the pharmacokinetic profile of fasudil in ocular tissues. Fasudil is known to have a short plasma half-life (approximately 0.3 hours), although its active metabolite, hydroxyfasudil, has a moderately longer half-life (approximately 2.9 hours) (Futterknecht et al., 2024; Halasz & Townes-Anderson, 2016). For effective treatment of dry AMD—where chronic, sustained modulation of ROCK activity may be necessary—injection strategies such as intravitreal administration or depot formulations may be required to maintain therapeutic levels over an extended period. Although intravitreal delivery of fasudil has been demonstrated in diabetic macular edema and other experimental models (Rothschild et al., 2017), further studies will be needed to optimize the dosage, frequency, and formulation for the specific demands of long-term retinal therapy in dry AMD.

Furthermore, while the preclinical evidence regarding the benefits of ROCK inhibition in RPE cells is promising, there remains a lack of direct clinical data demonstrating the efficacy of fasudil in patients with dry AMD. Most studies to date have focused on the use of ROCK inhibitors in models of diabetic retinopathy, neovascular AMD, or other retinal conditions, and the translation of these findings to the dry, atrophic form of AMD is not yet fully established (Halasz & Townes-Anderson, 2016; Rothschild et al., 2017). This gap underscores the need for focused, well-designed clinical trials and further preclinical studies using models that accurately recapitulate the pathophysiology of dry AMD.

Moreover, it will be essential to monitor whether the positive effects on cytoskeletal plasticity and phagocytosis translate into meaningful clinical endpoints such as improved visual acuity or slowed progression of geographic atrophy. Biomarkers of RPE health, phagocytic activity, and retinal integrity will need to be identified and validated in the context of fasudil treatment (Bailey-Steinitz, 2021; Croze et al., 2014).

In conclusion, the available literature strongly supports the mechanistic rationale for using fasudil to target ROCK-mediated dysfunction in RPE cells, which is a critical aspect of dry AMD pathogenesis (Catral et al., 2024; Halasz & Townes-Anderson, 2016). Its established safety profile in other clinical scenarios, coupled with evidence from preclinical models demonstrating improved cytoskeletal regulation, enhanced phagocytic capacity, and attenuated inflammation in retinal cells, makes fasudil a promising therapeutic candidate for repurposing in dry AMD (Moura-Coelho et al., 2019; Rothschild et al., 2017). Nonetheless, challenges related to drug-specific pharmacokinetics, the risk of off-target effects due to its nonselectivity, and the current lack of direct clinical evidence in dry AMD warrant cautious further investigation. The next steps should involve optimization of ocular delivery methods—potentially through sustained-release formulations—and the initiation of preclinical studies in animal models of dry AMD to assess functional outcomes. If these studies prove successful, they would pave the way for early-phase clinical trials aimed at evaluating the efficacy, safety, and tolerability of fasudil in patients with dry AMD (Rothschild et al., 2017; Yamaguchi et al., 2017).

Overall, while significant work remains to be done, the integration of biochemical, cellular, and clinical data from the broader ROCK inhibitor literature provides a compelling argument for further evaluation of fasudil as a potential repurposed treatment for dry AMD. The strategy to inhibit ROCK in RPE cells to restore cytoskeletal dynamics, enhance phagocytosis, and reduce oxidative and inflammatory stress fits well with current understanding of dry AMD pathophysiology and represents an innovative approach that could address a critical unmet need in AMD therapeutics (Bailey-Steinitz, 2021; Catral et al., 2024; Halasz & Townes-Anderson, 2016).
