Proposal for Brexpiprazole (5-HT₁A agonist/5-HT₂A antagonist; Otsuka pharmaceutical)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Brexpiprazole is a synthetic, orally available fifth‐generation antipsychotic developed by Otsuka Pharmaceutical that belongs to the class of serotonin–dopamine activity modulators (SDAMs). It was discovered and developed as an improvement over earlier dopamine partial agonists in order to achieve balanced modulation of neurotransmission. Chemically, brexpiprazole was designed as a dihydrocarbostyril derivative that retains partial agonistic properties at dopamine D₂ receptors while simultaneously exhibiting potent partial agonism at serotonin 5-HT₁A receptors and antagonism at serotonin 5-HT₂A receptors (Das et al., 2016). This balanced receptor profile has been exploited primarily for psychiatric indications, but the receptor distribution of serotonin receptors is not limited to neuronal tissues. In particular, the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) expresses several serotonin receptor subtypes, and there is compelling biochemical evidence that serotonergic signaling influences inflammatory gene expression and cellular homeostasis in these cells (Biswal et al., 2015). As such, brexpiprazole’s well‐defined receptor interactions position it as a promising candidate to modulate pathological pathways in dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD).

Therapeutic History:  
Brexpiprazole has an established history for its use in central nervous system disorders. It has been approved for the treatment of schizophrenia and as an adjunct therapy in major depressive disorder owing to its favorable safety and tolerability profiles, which include a low risk of extrapyramidal side effects and modest metabolic liabilities (Das et al., 2016; Fabre et al., 2022). To date, however, no clinical trials or preclinical studies have directly examined brexpiprazole for the treatment of retinal degenerative conditions such as dry AMD. Nevertheless, multiple studies have investigated the therapeutic potential of 5-HT₁A receptor agonists in retinal models. For example, research utilizing 8-OH-DPAT—a prototypical 5-HT₁A agonist—demonstrated significant antioxidant protection in RPE cells, reduction in oxidative stress markers, and preservation of retinal architecture under conditions of mitochondrial oxidative stress (Biswal et al., 2015). Other compounds with selective 5-HT₁A agonist properties, such as tandospirone, have been evaluated in retinal models, albeit with mixed clinical outcomes (Buschini et al., 2015). This preclinical precedence establishes an indirect rationale for repurposing brexpiprazole in dry AMD even if direct ocular applications have not been previously explored.

Mechanism of Action:  
Brexpiprazole exerts its pharmacological effects primarily through dual modulation of serotonergic receptors. Its partial agonism at 5-HT₁A receptors, with reported potency in the low nanomolar range (EC₅₀ ~ 5 nM as hypothesized), is thought to trigger intracellular cascades that promote cellular survival and antioxidant defenses. Activation of the 5-HT₁A receptor in other tissues has been associated with upregulation of key antioxidant enzymes—including metallothionein-1 (MT-1), heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), NADPH quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NqO1), catalase, and superoxide dismutase-1 (SOD1)—thereby mitigating oxidative stress and reducing mitochondrial damage (Biswal et al., 2015). In RPE cells—the cell type critical for photoreceptor outer segment (POS) clearance—the 5-HT₁A receptor is endogenously expressed, and its activation has been linked to attenuation of inflammatory signaling. Preclinical studies using other 5-HT₁A agonists in ARPE-19 cells have shown reductions in oxidative stress markers and inflammatory mediators, as well as preservation of tight junction proteins (Biswal et al., 2015).

Simultaneously, brexpiprazole’s antagonism at 5-HT₂A receptors may complement these effects by further modulating inflammatory responses. Blockade of 5-HT₂A receptors has been associated with reduced microglial activation and decreased complement deposition in the outer retina, contributing to neuroprotection in models of retinal degeneration (Biswal et al., 2015; Das et al., 2016). This dual receptor effect is proposed to result in an overall anti-inflammatory microenvironment in the RPE, which is crucial because chronic inflammation is a key pathological feature in dry AMD. Moreover, the compound’s modulation of serotonergic signaling may indirectly support the maintenance of phagocytic receptors such as MERTK and lysosomal enzymes like cathepsin D, which are essential for the normal clearance of POS (Biswal et al., 2015). Although direct studies with brexpiprazole in RPE cells are lacking, these mechanistic insights derived from similar 5-HT₁A agonists underpin the hypothesis that brexpiprazole could reduce production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6 and TNF-α) and restore normal phagocytic and lysosomal function (Biswal et al., 2015).

Expected Effect:  
In an in vitro assay using ARPE-19 cells or isolated RPE cells under conditions of complement-induced stress, brexpiprazole is expected to act by modulating serotonergic receptor signaling to yield a multi-faceted protective effect. Specifically, its 5-HT₁A agonism should trigger intracellular survival pathways that result in the upregulation of antioxidant defenses analogous to the responses observed with 8-OH-DPAT in similar models (Biswal et al., 2015). This effect is anticipated to lead to a reduction in oxidative stress markers, such as 8-OH-deoxyguanosine or nitrotyrosine adducts, which are typically elevated in conditions of mitochondrial oxidative injury (Biswal et al., 2015).

Additionally, brexpiprazole is hypothesized to reduce the expression of key pro-inflammatory cytokines—specifically IL-6 and TNF-α—through receptor-mediated modulation of inflammatory signaling cascades. In a cell culture model where RPE cells are exposed to oxidative and complement-mediated insults, a drop in these cytokine levels would be indicative of an amelioration of stress-induced inflammatory responses (Biswal et al., 2015; Wright & Ambati, 2016). This anti-inflammatory effect is critical because uncontrolled inflammation not only perpetuates oxidative damage but also disrupts the barrier and phagocytic functions of the RPE.

Furthermore, the candidate is expected to preserve or restore the expression of MERTK, a receptor tyrosine kinase crucial for the phagocytosis of POS, as well as cathepsin D, a lysosomal protease necessary for the degradation of internalized material (Biswal et al., 2015; Fischer, 2016). Successful maintenance of these proteins would directly support the clearance functions of RPE cells, an essential element for retinal homeostasis that is compromised in dry AMD. This improved phagocytic capacity, coupled with reduced cytokine production, would likely result in overall preservation of RPE structure and function, as reflected in enhanced visual function in preclinical models (Biswal et al., 2015).

Moreover, although the clinical studies of other serotonergic compounds such as tandospirone have yielded mixed results—with at least one phase III trial for geographic atrophy being discontinued due to lack of efficacy (Buschini et al., 2015)—the favorable pharmacokinetic properties and established safety profile of brexpiprazole in other indications provide a strong rationale for its repurposing, particularly if its ocular penetration can be demonstrated in relevant preclinical models (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.).

Overall Evaluation:  
Brexpiprazole offers several attractive strengths as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD. Its dual mechanism—partial agonism at 5-HT₁A receptors paired with antagonism at 5-HT₂A receptors—is well aligned with the emerging understanding of how serotonergic signaling influences retinal cell survival, oxidative stress, and inflammation (Biswal et al., 2015). The wealth of preclinical evidence supporting the role of 5-HT₁A receptor activation in reducing oxidative damage and preserving RPE function under stress conditions provides a sound mechanistic basis for expecting similar protective effects from brexpiprazole. In addition, the targeting of phagocytic receptor expression (i.e., MERTK) and lysosomal enzymes (i.e., cathepsin D) represents a novel strategy to address one of the fundamental dysfunctions in dry AMD: impaired clearance of photoreceptor outer segments (Biswal et al., 2015).

Brexpiprazole’s established safety profile and pharmacokinetics in psychiatric populations further underscore its potential for repurposing. Its high oral bioavailability and documented tolerability in long-term studies, along with evidence that similar compounds can penetrate ocular tissues when administered systemically, add to the candidate’s appeal (Das et al., 2016; Fabre et al., 2022). This is particularly relevant given the chronic nature of dry AMD and the need for therapies that can be safely administered over extended periods.

However, several significant weaknesses and gaps warrant cautious consideration. First, direct evidence linking brexpiprazole to improved RPE function—specifically, its effects on inflammatory signaling markers IL-6 and TNF-α, and on the preservation of MERTK and cathepsin D expression—remains theoretical and extrapolated from studies using other 5-HT₁A agonists like 8-OH-DPAT (Biswal et al., 2015). This means that while the underlying mechanistic hypothesis is plausible, there is a pressing need for targeted in vitro and in vivo studies focused on brexpiprazole in ocular models. In particular, experiments using ARPE-19 cells under conditions of oxidative or complement-induced stress must be conducted to confirm that brexpiprazole can indeed modulate cytokine levels and restore phagocytic function in these cells.

Another shortcoming is the uncertainty regarding ocular penetration. Although the systemic administration of 5-HT₁A agonists in animal models has led to beneficial retinal outcomes (Biswal et al., 2015), brexpiprazole’s distribution into ocular tissues has not been directly evaluated. It is essential to ascertain the extent to which brexpiprazole reaches the RPE at therapeutically relevant concentrations, particularly given that dosage regimens for psychiatric conditions may not be directly translatable to ocular therapy (Biswal et al., 2015).

Furthermore, while the anti-inflammatory hypothesis is supported by the general role of serotonergic signaling in RPE cells, the direct measurement of inflammatory cytokines and downstream phagocytic markers in response to brexpiprazole treatment remains to be demonstrated. Given that earlier clinical attempts using tandospirone for AMD were unsuccessful (Buschini et al., 2015), there is a need to critically assess whether brexpiprazole’s receptor profile confers advantages that overcome the limitations observed with other 5-HT₁A agonists. Additionally, potential off-target effects in the ocular environment—such as those arising from modulation of other receptor systems—must be thoroughly evaluated.

In summary, brexpiprazole presents a scientifically sound and innovative approach to the treatment of dry AMD by virtue of its serotonergic receptor modulation. Its dual action on 5-HT₁A and 5-HT₂A receptors is supported by a robust body of preclinical evidence indicating that 5-HT₁A receptor activation can reduce oxidative stress and inflammation in RPE cells, while also preserving essential functions like POS clearance (Biswal et al., 2015). The candidate’s excellent safety profile in psychiatric applications and potential for systemic administration further enhance its appeal. Nevertheless, the absence of direct experimental data linking brexpiprazole to improved phagocytosis, reduced IL-6 and TNF-α production, and maintained lysosomal enzyme expression in RPE cells represents a critical gap that must be addressed. As such, while the repurposing of brexpiprazole for dry AMD holds promise, a series of preclinical experiments—encompassing in vitro ARPE-19 cell assays, in vivo ocular pharmacokinetic studies, and efficacy models of retinal degeneration—are required before progressing to clinical trials. Overall, given the mechanistic rationale and the encouraging safety and pharmacological characteristics of brexpiprazole, further research is justified to validate its application in dry AMD, provided that the aforementioned knowledge gaps are systematically addressed (Das et al., 2016; Biswal et al., 2015).
