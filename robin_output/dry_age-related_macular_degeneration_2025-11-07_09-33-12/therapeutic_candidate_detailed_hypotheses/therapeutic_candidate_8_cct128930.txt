Proposal for CCT128930 (AKT2 inhibitor; SelleckChem Cat. No. S1151)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
CCT128930 is a synthetic small molecule that belongs to the pyrrolopyrimidine class and was initially discovered and optimized for its potent ATP‐competitive inhibition of AKT kinases, particularly targeting the AKT2 isoform (Adel et al., 2018a, pp. 9–11; Adel et al., 2018b, pp. 6–8). Its discovery was achieved through a combination of fragment-based screening and structure-based drug design strategies, which allowed researchers to identify and refine a scaffold capable of binding within the ATP pocket of kinases such as AKT and PDK1 (Adel et al., 2018a, pp. 7–9). The molecular design of CCT128930 incorporates a substituted 2-anilino-7H-pyrrolopyrimidine core that confers high affinity and selectivity, as evidenced by its low nanomolar IC₅₀ value—the reported potency for AKT inhibition is around 6–10 nM (Adel et al., 2018b, pp. 6–8; Yap et al., 2011, pp. 3–5). Originally developed for oncology purposes, where aberrant AKT signaling drives uncontrolled cell proliferation, the compound demonstrated significant antiproliferative effects in multiple tumor cell lines with some studies noting efficacy comparable to established inhibitors such as Erlotinib (Adel et al., 2018a, pp. 9–11; Adel et al., 2018b, pp. 8–9). The synthetic origins and chemical tractability of the pyrrolopyrimidine scaffold have historically permitted further chemical modifications to optimize pharmacokinetics and minimize off-target activity, traits that are now valuable in the context of repurposing efforts. In summary, CCT128930 is a robustly characterized ATP-competitive inhibitor with a synthetic pedigree grounded in modern medicinal chemistry, which originally found application in cancer therapy yet holds promise for other diseases where AKT2-mediated signaling is implicated (Adel et al., 2018b, pp. 6–8; Yap et al., 2011, pp. 3–5).  

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, CCT128930 was developed within an oncology paradigm as researchers sought to inhibit aberrant AKT signaling—a driver of cell survival, proliferation, and metabolism—in various cancer models (Adel et al., 2018a, pp. 9–11; Adel et al., 2018b, pp. 6–8). Preclinical studies demonstrated that by inhibiting the phosphorylation of key downstream targets such as PRAS40 and FOXO3a, CCT128930 could induce cell cycle arrest and reduce tumor growth in vitro and in xenograft models (Adel et al., 2018a, pp. 9–11; Adel et al., 2018b, pp. 6–8). Its antiproliferative efficacy was further underpinned by its ability to compete effectively with ATP for binding to the kinase domain of AKT, a mode of action that led to promising results in early-phase oncology trials. Despite these encouraging effects, further clinical development was halted during phase I studies primarily because of concerns regarding off-target activity and toxicities that limited its therapeutic window (Fang et al., 2013, pp. 36–40; Adel et al., 2018b, pp. 6–8). Notably, before the current repurposing hypothesis was proposed, CCT128930 had not been utilized for any ocular indications, including dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD), nor had it been evaluated in veterinary settings for similar degenerative diseases. Emerging literature on the role of AKT2 in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) dysfunction, particularly through its phosphorylation of transcription factor EB (TFEB), now provides a mechanistic basis for repurposing this compound. Previous studies have linked AKT2 activity with impaired lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy in RPE cells—a critical pathological mechanism in dry AMD (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2). Thus, while the therapeutic history of CCT128930 is rooted in oncology, its well-documented pharmacology, including preclinical pharmacokinetics, tissue penetration (notably CNS penetration), and kinase selectivity, supports its potential application in ocular conditions through localized delivery approaches such as intravitreal injection (Fang et al., 2013, pp. 36–40; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 4–6).  

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, CCT128930 exerts its effects primarily by acting as an ATP-competitive inhibitor of AKT kinases, binding to the ATP-binding site within the kinase domain to block the phosphorylation of downstream targets (Yap et al., 2011, pp. 3–5; Adel et al., 2018a, pp. 7–9). Its mode of action is underscored by its high degree of selectivity for AKT2 over other kinases such as protein kinase A (PKA), which is critical given the need to minimize off-target effects in sensitive tissues like the retina (Yap et al., 2011, pp. 3–5; Adel et al., 2018a, pp. 7–9). In normal cellular physiology, AKT2 phosphorylates a variety of substrates, including regulatory proteins that modulate cell survival, metabolism, and growth. One particularly important substrate is transcription factor EB (TFEB), a master regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy. Under standard conditions, TFEB translocates from the cytosol to the nucleus where it drives the expression of numerous genes involved in lysosome formation and the autophagic process. However, in the setting of chronic AKT2 activation—a scenario evident in RPE cells in dry AMD—TFEB becomes phosphorylated at specific serine residues (for example, Ser467), which results in its retention in the cytosol and subsequent inactivation (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 4–6; Ghosh et al., 2023, pp. 1–5). This aberrant phosphorylation leads to reduced autophagic flux and lysosomal dysfunction, impairing the RPE’s capacity to degrade photoreceptor outer segments (POS) effectively (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 8–10).  

By selectively inhibiting AKT2, CCT128930 is hypothesized to reduce the phosphorylation of TFEB, thereby liberating it from its cytosolic sequestration and allowing its translocation into the nucleus (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 2–4). Once in the nucleus, TFEB can stimulate the transcription of lysosomal and autophagy-related genes, enhancing the degradative capacity of RPE cells through increased lysosomal biogenesis and autophagic flux (Ghosh et al., 2024, p. 6; Karim et al., 2020, pp. 10–12). Structural studies of pyrrolopyrimidine inhibitors reveal that the binding of CCT128930 involves specific hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions with key amino acid residues in the ATP-binding pocket of AKT2, leading to its functional inactivation (Adel et al., 2018b, pp. 6–8; Yap et al., 2011, pp. 3–5). This interaction ensures that even at low concentrations, the compound can effectively disrupt AKT2-mediated signaling cascades. The interruption of this signaling cascade is particularly important in the RPE context, where sustained AKT2 activation contributes to the accumulation of cellular debris and the formation of drusen—hallmark features of dry AMD (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 4–6). Consequently, the molecular events set in motion by CCT128930—namely, the reduction of TFEB phosphorylation and the subsequent activation of lysosomal and autophagy genes—provide a coherent therapeutic rationale for its repurposing in the treatment of dry AMD.  

Expected Effect:  
In the proposed experimental and therapeutic setting, the central expectation is that CCT128930 will inhibit AKT2-mediated TFEB phosphorylation in RPE cells, thus alleviating the cytosolic retention of TFEB and promoting its nuclear translocation (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 4–6; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2). This mechanistic modulation is anticipated to reinvigorate the transcription of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagic genes that are critical for the degradation of POS—a continuous and essential process in the maintenance of retinal health. In dry AMD, the inability of RPE cells to effectively process and clear accumulated photoreceptor debris leads to the formation of drusen and subsequent retinal degeneration. By restoring TFEB activity, treatment with CCT128930 is expected to enhance lysosomal function and lead to improved clearance of these cellular wastes, thereby potentially halting or reversing the progression of disease pathology (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7).  

Experimental assays to confirm this expected effect would involve measuring the levels of nuclear TFEB through immunocytochemistry and subcellular fractionation, alongside quantitative PCR assessments for the expression of lysosome-associated genes. Enhanced autophagic flux could be monitored by evaluating markers such as LC3-II conversion and p62 degradation. Given the compound’s IC₅₀ of approximately 6–10 nM, it is anticipated that effective concentrations can be achieved in vitro at low nanomolar doses, with further adjustments made for optimized intravitreal formulations (Yap et al., 2011, pp. 3–5; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2). Furthermore, since RPE cells derived from CFH mutant models have been shown to exhibit greater dysregulation of the AKT2/TFEB signaling pathway, efficacious treatment with CCT128930 should yield measurable improvements in surrogate endpoints such as restored lysosomal enzyme activity and reduced accumulation of POS debris (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 8–10). Such outcomes would not only validate the underlying hypothesis but also establish critical proof-of-concept data to support the transition of CCT128930 into further preclinical and, eventually, clinical studies for dry AMD.  

Overall Evaluation:  
The overall evaluation of CCT128930 as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry age-related macular degeneration is cautiously optimistic, informed by a combination of robust mechanistic rationale and a well-characterized pharmacological profile derived from prior oncology research. Strengths of the candidate include its potent ATP-competitive inhibition of AKT2, evidenced by its low nanomolar IC₅₀, and its demonstrated selectivity over other kinases such as PKA, which is critical in reducing the risk of off-target effects (Adel et al., 2018b, pp. 6–8; Yap et al., 2011, pp. 3–5). The molecular rationale for its use in dry AMD hinges on the role of AKT2 in promoting the phosphorylation and cytosolic retention of TFEB—a transcription factor that, when activated, orchestrates lysosomal biogenesis and the autophagic clearance of photoreceptor outer segments (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 4–6; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2). By reversing this phosphorylation, CCT128930 is expected to restore TFEB nuclear translocation and thereby rejuvenate the intrinsic degradation pathways within RPE cells, which are compromised in dry AMD (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 2–4; Ghosh et al., 2024, p. 6).  

However, there are notable challenges and weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. Chief among these is the historical context of CCT128930’s clinical development in oncology, where its advancement was ultimately discontinued due to off-target toxicity and suboptimal safety profiles (Fang et al., 2013, pp. 36–40). Although intravitreal delivery is proposed to localize the drug’s effects and minimize systemic exposure, the unique architecture and sensitivity of the retina demand that comprehensive ocular toxicology studies be undertaken before clinical application. In addition, the extrapolation from cancer cell line models to RPE physiology is not trivial; the intracellular signaling milieu and compensatory mechanisms in RPE cells may differ significantly from those in proliferative tumor cells, potentially affecting the efficacy and safety of AKT2 inhibition in this new context (Yap et al., 2011, pp. 1–2; Adel et al., 2018b, pp. 6–8).  

Moreover, the efficiency of CCT128930 in achieving the desired pharmacodynamic outcome in RPE cells will depend heavily on its ability to penetrate the retinal layers and persist at effective concentrations for a sufficient duration. Although available preclinical pharmacokinetics suggest favorable CNS penetration, detailed studies focused specifically on ocular distribution, metabolic stability in vitreous humor, and clearance mechanisms are critical (Fang et al., 2013, pp. 36–40; Guo et al., 2021, pp. 9–11). Additionally, inter-individual variability in disease pathology—particularly in patients with genetic predispositions such as the CFH Y402H variant—may necessitate personalized approaches or combination therapies to fully harness the compound’s therapeutic potential (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 4–6; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7).  

In the context of overall therapeutic strategy, the repurposing of CCT128930 offers a novel and mechanistically targeted approach to dry AMD—a disease area where current therapeutic options are limited. The focus on restoring autophagic and lysosomal function addresses a key pathological hallmark of dry AMD, potentially offering a disease-modifying rather than merely symptomatic intervention (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 4–6). Nonetheless, the transition from preclinical promise to clinical reality will require a dedicated effort to optimize drug delivery, thoroughly assess safety in the ocular setting, and define the most responsive patient populations. If these challenges can be overcome, CCT128930 has the potential to be a transformative therapy for dry AMD, representing both a smart and targeted application of existing pharmacological knowledge as well as an innovative shift in treatment paradigms (Fang et al., 2013, pp. 36–40; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2).  

Potential Developments and Future Directions:  
A critical next step involves designing robust preclinical studies that closely mimic the clinical scenarios encountered in dry AMD. Studies should begin with in vitro investigations using primary RPE cells and induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived RPE models, particularly those harboring CFH mutations known to accentuate the dysregulation of the AKT2/TFEB pathway (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2). These experiments will need to quantify key parameters such as nuclear translocation of TFEB, lysosomal gene expression, and autophagic flux using standard biomarkers (Karim et al., 2020, pp. 10–12; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 8–10). In parallel, it will be essential to develop in vivo animal models of dry AMD to assess the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of CCT128930 following intravitreal injection. Parameters such as drug retention in the vitreous, retinal penetration, and clearance rates must be rigorously evaluated to establish an optimal dosing regimen that balances efficacy with safety (Fang et al., 2013, pp. 36–40; Guo et al., 2021, pp. 9–11).  

Innovative drug delivery approaches should also be explored. For example, encapsulating CCT128930 in biodegradable nanoparticles or liposomal carriers could facilitate a sustained release profile, reducing the frequency of intravitreal injections and minimizing peak-related toxicity. Such delivery systems have shown promise in improving the local bioavailability of other ocular drugs and may similarly enhance the therapeutic index of CCT128930 in the retinal environment (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7; Guo et al., 2021, pp. 9–11).  

Rigorously addressing the potential for off-target toxicity is another priority. Detailed in vitro and in vivo toxicity studies should monitor retinal structure and function using imaging techniques such as optical coherence tomography (OCT), fundus autofluorescence, and electrophysiological evaluations (Fang et al., 2013, pp. 36–40; Yap et al., 2011, pp. 9–10). These evaluations will be critical to ensuring that prolonged inhibition of AKT2 does not inadvertently compromise other essential signaling pathways within the retina.  

Furthermore, the development of validated biomarkers for target engagement is crucial. Such biomarkers could include quantifiable metrics of TFEB nuclear localization, lysosomal enzyme activity, or even specific transcriptomic changes associated with enhanced autophagy. These surrogate endpoints would not only help in the preclinical assessment of drug efficacy but could also serve as early indicators of therapeutic response in clinical trials (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2; Adel et al., 2018b, pp. 6–8).  

Translational Potential and Risk Mitigation:  
The repurposing strategy for CCT128930 necessitates a comprehensive risk mitigation framework that leverages its strong pharmacological attributes while addressing potential safety concerns. Localized intravitreal delivery offers a means to concentrate the drug’s action within the eye while minimizing systemic exposure—a critical consideration given the adverse events observed in earlier oncology trials (Fang et al., 2013, pp. 36–40; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2). At the same time, continued medicinal chemistry efforts may yield derivatives with an improved safety profile that are even more suited to the ocular environment. Structure–activity relationship (SAR) studies can identify chemical modifications that retain the desired potency in AKT2 inhibition while reducing potential off-target interactions, thereby refining the therapeutic index for chronic ocular use (Adel et al., 2018b, pp. 6–8; Yap et al., 2011, pp. 3–5).  

In parallel, integrating robust pharmacodynamic biomarkers into preclinical and clinical studies will allow for the fine-tuning of dosing regimens and early detection of potential adverse effects. Such biomarkers could include imaging findings related to retinal morphology changes or molecular indicators of restored autophagic function in RPE cells, all of which would contribute to ensuring that the efficacy of CCT128930 is achieved without compromising retinal integrity (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 4–6).  

Collaboration across disciplines—bringing together experts in pharmacology, ophthalmology, and drug delivery science—will be paramount for addressing the unique challenges posed by repurposing an oncology drug for ocular conditions. These interdisciplinary efforts will help design and implement the necessary preclinical studies to evaluate not only drug efficacy but also long-term safety in the delicate retinal environment.  

Regulatory Considerations:  
Repurposing CCT128930 for dry AMD will require comprehensive preclinical data that address the compound’s ocular pharmacokinetics, toxicity profiles, and immunogenicity following repeated intravitreal dosing. Given its previous clinical history in oncology, regulators will likely demand additional evidence to ensure that the risks observed in systemic administration do not extend to the ocular context (Fang et al., 2013, pp. 36–40; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2). Early engagement with regulatory agencies is recommended to outline the repurposing strategy, propose appropriate endpoints for clinical trials, and define acceptable risk thresholds. The integration of validated biomarkers for therapeutic response and the use of advanced drug delivery platforms could also help streamline the regulatory pathway by providing robust, multidimensional evidence of both efficacy and safety (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 4–6).  

Comparative Analysis with Other Therapeutics:  
In the landscape of treatments for age-related macular degeneration, particularly the dry form, current strategies predominantly focus on mitigating inflammation, oxidative stress, or neovascularization in the case of wet AMD. However, few therapies directly target the underlying cellular clearance deficits that characterize dry AMD. The mechanism of action of CCT128930, which centers on the inhibition of AKT2 to restore TFEB function and enhance autophagy, represents a novel approach that directly addresses the impaired lysosomal degradation essential for RPE health (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 4–6; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2). While other candidates have been evaluated for their ability to modulate autophagy, the precise targeting of the AKT2/TFEB axis distinguishes CCT128930 from these agents and offers the possibility of a more durable, disease-modifying effect. In light of the limited efficacy of current treatments for dry AMD, a drug candidate that can substantially improve the intrinsic degradative capacity of the RPE would represent a significant advancement over existing modalities (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2; Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7).  

Integration with Current Treatment Paradigms and Future Research Directions:  
Given the unmet need in dry AMD—where available treatments are largely supportive rather than restorative—the integration of a molecule like CCT128930 into the clinical armamentarium holds considerable appeal. Its repurposing would represent a shift toward targeting the core pathology of RPE degradation and lysosomal dysfunction, rather than simply addressing downstream outcomes. Future research should prioritize studies that confirm the beneficial effects of AKT2 inhibition on TFEB activation in RPE cells, particularly using models that recapitulate the genetic and metabolic nuances of dry AMD. Investigations should extend from cell culture systems to animal models, examining functional endpoints such as retinal morphology, visual acuity, and markers of photoreceptor outer segment turnover (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2; Ghosh et al., 2024, p. 6). Additionally, research into advanced drug delivery methods—such as encapsulation in biodegradable nanoparticles—to sustain intravitreal drug levels over time will be critical for translating the strong in vitro effects of CCT128930 into meaningful clinical outcomes (Falcão et al., 2025, pp. 5–7; Guo et al., 2021, pp. 9–11).  

Considerations for Dosing and Patient Selection:  
A particularly important aspect in the potential repurposing of CCT128930 is the need to define an optimal dosing regimen that achieves sustained AKT2 inhibition in the retina without compromising cellular viability. The compound’s low nanomolar potency suggests that effective concentrations may be achievable with limited drug exposure, thereby reducing the likelihood of toxicity. However, the unique pharmacokinetic properties of the eye, including the barriers to drug diffusion and the turnover rates of the vitreous humor, necessitate careful dose optimization studies in animal models. Furthermore, patient selection may play a critical role in therapeutic success. For example, individuals with genetic predispositions—such as those carrying the CFH Y402H variant—may exhibit more pronounced dysregulation of the AKT2/TFEB pathway and could therefore derive greater benefit from treatment with CCT128930. Stratifying patients based on genetic and molecular markers may help in tailoring therapies to achieve maximal therapeutic benefit (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 4–6).  

Assessment of Potential Adverse Events:  
While the inhibition of AKT2 holds promise for restoring lysosomal function in RPE cells, it is imperative to assess potential adverse events associated with CCT128930. The compound’s history in oncology, where off-target toxicity and systemic adverse effects led to discontinuation, underscores the need for a careful reevaluation of its safety profile in an ocular setting (Fang et al., 2013, pp. 36–40; Yap et al., 2011, pp. 9–10). The specific effects of sustained AKT2 inhibition on various retinal cell types—including photoreceptors, ganglion cells, and Müller glia—must be systematically investigated using both histological and functional assays. Electroretinography, imaging modalities, and molecular assessments of cellular stress markers will be essential components of any comprehensive preclinical safety evaluation. These studies will help ensure that the benefits of improved autophagic clearance are not overshadowed by unwanted disruptions of retinal homeostasis.  

Integration with Ongoing Research in the Field:  
The contemporary research focus on autophagy and lysosomal biogenesis as therapeutic targets in dry AMD aligns well with the proposed mechanism underlying CCT128930’s repurposing. Emerging studies have consistently shown that restoring lysosomal function through modulation of TFEB activity can ameliorate RPE dysfunction and reduce the progression of retinal degeneration. In this context, the targeted inhibition of AKT2 to relieve TFEB phosphorylation represents a novel and highly specific strategy that addresses a key pathological node in AMD. Collaboration between research groups working on molecular pathways in RPE cells and experts in drug delivery and clinical ophthalmology will be crucial for the successful translation of this approach. The convergence of insights from multiple disciplines serves to reinforce the potential impact of CCT128930 and encourages further exploration through well-designed preclinical studies (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 2–4).  

Conclusion and Strategic Recommendations:  
In conclusion, the repurposing of CCT128930 for the treatment of dry age-related macular degeneration is underpinned by a robust mechanistic rationale and a comprehensive body of preclinical data derived from oncology research. Its potent and selective inhibition of AKT2—with consequent reduction in TFEB phosphorylation—offers a novel therapeutic strategy to restore lysosomal function and autophagic clearance in RPE cells, addressing a fundamental pathological mechanism in dry AMD (Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 4–6; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2). While the compound’s chemical and pharmacological attributes are well documented, several challenges remain, including the need to optimize ocular delivery, establish a favorable safety profile, and verify efficacy in relevant preclinical models. A comprehensive research strategy that includes in vitro validation using genetically representative RPE models, in vivo pharmacokinetic and toxicological assessments, and the development of advanced sustained-release formulations is recommended to mitigate risks and maximize therapeutic potential (Fang et al., 2013, pp. 36–40; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 4–6).  

Moreover, given the limited treatment options currently available for dry AMD, a condition where restoring RPE function could profoundly impact disease progression, advancing CCT128930 into a dedicated preclinical development program is both scientifically and clinically justified. Early-phase clinical studies, designed with a strong emphasis on biomarker-driven endpoints and safety, will be critical to establishing whether the encouraging in vitro and preclinical findings can be reproduced in patients. By carefully addressing the challenges of drug delivery and off-target toxicity, and by harnessing innovative approaches to patient stratification and treatment monitoring, CCT128930 could ultimately evolve into a transformative therapy for dry AMD. As such, its inclusion in the drug development pipeline is recommended, pending further rigorous investigation and validation of its ocular efficacy and safety profile (Fang et al., 2013, pp. 36–40; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 4–6; Adel et al., 2018b, pp. 6–8).  

Ultimately, the repurposing of CCT128930 embodies a forward-thinking strategy in drug development, leveraging established pharmacological principles and preexisting safety data from oncology to address a pressing unmet need in ophthalmology. Through a multidisciplinary approach that integrates advanced drug formulation, precise molecular targeting, and patient-specific strategies, this therapeutic candidate has the potential to restore impaired autophagic and lysosomal processes in the RPE, thereby offering a novel modality for the treatment of dry AMD. The translational journey for CCT128930 will require meticulous preclinical characterization, careful clinical trial planning, and close collaboration with regulatory agencies, but the promise of a mechanism-based intervention that targets the root cause of RPE degeneration provides a compelling impetus for further exploration (Fang et al., 2013, pp. 36–40; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 1–2; Ghosh et al., 2024, pp. 4–6; Adel et al., 2018b, pp. 6–8).
