Proposal for Celastrol (NF-κB/Nrf2 modulator; Santa Cruz Biotech Cat. No. sc-204268)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Celastrol is a naturally occurring pentacyclic triterpenoid compound originally isolated from the root extracts of Tripterygium wilfordii Hook F, commonly known as the “Thunder God Vine” (Gu et al., 2018; Cascao et al., 2017). This compound belongs to the quinone methide triterpenoid class, a group of bioactive natural products that have been used in traditional Chinese medicine for centuries to alleviate inflammatory, autoimmune, and neurodegenerative disorders (Law, 2022; Chan et al., 2024). Celastrol’s unique chemical structure, characterized by electrophilic centers that enable covalent interactions with nucleophilic cysteine residues on target proteins, confers upon it a multi-targeted mechanism of action. Its triterpenoid nature places it among a class of compounds known for their broad anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticancer properties, which have been applied in diverse preclinical settings ranging from rheumatoid arthritis and Crohn’s disease to neurodegenerative conditions such as Parkinson’s disease (Bian et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2019). The candidate under consideration, provided by Santa Cruz Biotech (Cat. No. sc-204268), is being evaluated for repurposing in dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD), a disease characterized by chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and impairment in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) function (Zhang et al., 2019; Du et al., 2019).

Therapeutic History:  
Celastrol’s therapeutic history spans several decades and includes extensive preclinical research across multiple disease models. Historically, extracts from Tripterygium wilfordii have been utilized in traditional medicine for conditions marked by inflammation and autoimmunity. Modern biochemical investigations have validated celastrol’s anti-inflammatory and antioxidant actions, particularly through its ability to suppress proinflammatory cytokine production by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway (Zhang et al., 2019). In ocular research, celastrol has been shown to provide significant protection in models of retinal injury. For example, in studies using human ARPE-19 cells—a widely accepted in vitro model for RPE—the compound has demonstrated cytoprotective effects against hydrogen peroxide-induced oxidative stress, reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, autophagy, and apoptosis while enhancing cell survival (Du et al., 2019). Additionally, preclinical animal studies have demonstrated that celastrol can protect retinal ganglion cells from optic nerve crush–induced degeneration, underscoring its neuroprotective potential within ocular tissues (Kyung et al., 2015; Law, 2022). Although direct clinical evaluations in dry AMD are few, the compound’s efficacy in analogous retinal degenerative conditions, including its ability to modulate inflammatory responses and oxidative stress in retinal cells, provides a compelling rationale for its repurposing in dry AMD (Zhao et al., 2022; Gu et al., 2018). Moreover, its application in other chronic inflammatory diseases and early-phase clinical investigations in rheumatoid arthritis have established proof-of-concept that celastrol can be used safely at carefully controlled dosages, although challenges with bioavailability and systemic toxicity have been repeatedly noted (Cascao et al., 2017).

Mechanism of Action:  
Celastrol exerts its cellular effects through a dual mechanism that targets two critical signaling pathways implicated in dry AMD pathogenesis: the nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) pathway and the nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway. On the inflammatory front, celastrol inhibits the NF-κB signaling cascade. Under normal conditions, NF-κB is held inactive in the cytoplasm by inhibitory proteins (IκBs). Upon inflammatory stimulation, IκBs are phosphorylated and degraded, allowing NF-κB to translocate to the nucleus and induce transcription of proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-8 (IL-8), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) (Zhang et al., 2019). Celastrol has been shown to block the phosphorylation of key regulatory proteins such as IKKα/β and IκBα, thereby preventing nuclear translocation of the p65 subunit and subsequent activation of inflammatory gene transcription in RPE cells (Zhang et al., 2019). This results in a marked reduction in cytokine secretion—a finding that is crucial for attenuating the chronic inflammatory milieu present in dry AMD. Simultaneously, celastrol activates the Nrf2 signaling pathway. Under oxidative stress conditions, Nrf2 dissociates from its cytoplasmic chaperone Keap1 and translocates to the nucleus, where it binds to antioxidant response elements (ARE) and promotes the transcription of a battery of antioxidant enzymes such as heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), NAD(P)H quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1), and glutamate-cysteine ligase catalysis (Catanzaro et al., 2020; Du et al., 2019). Activation of Nrf2 not only enhances the antioxidant capacity of RPE cells but also supports cellular defense mechanisms against oxidative insults, which are central to the progression of dry AMD. Further contributing to its protective profile, celastrol has been implicated in modulating SIRT3 expression—a mitochondrial sirtuin involved in the regulation of metabolic and oxidative stress responses—and in promoting autophagy by upregulating markers such as LC3II/LC3I ratio and Beclin-1 (Du et al., 2019). The compound’s electrophilic properties permit it to form covalent adducts with target proteins, thereby impairing the activity of key proinflammatory mediators while simultaneously bolstering the cell’s intrinsic antioxidant machinery (Venkatesha & Moudgil, 2016; Law, 2022). This comprehensive modulation of both inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways underpins celastrol’s potential utility in preserving RPE function under the duress of chronic stressors typical of dry AMD.

Expected Effect:  
The primary hypothesis driving the repurposing of celastrol for dry AMD is that, at an effective concentration (approximately EC₅₀ ~1 µM), the compound will exert a dual molecular effect in RPE cells by inhibiting NF-κB and activating Nrf2. In an in vitro assay using ARPE-19 cells, celastrol is expected to reduce the expression and secretion of proinflammatory cytokines—such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α—by interfering with the phosphorylation events necessary for NF-κB activation (Zhang et al., 2019). This reduction in cytokine levels is crucial given that inflammatory mediators are known to downregulate the expression of phagocytic receptors such as MERTK, which are essential for the clearance of photoreceptor outer segments (POS). Impaired POS clearance leads to the accumulation of toxic cellular debris and lipofuscin, contributing significantly to RPE dysfunction and progression of dry AMD (Venkatesha & Moudgil, 2016; Zhang et al., 2019). Concurrently, the activation of the Nrf2 pathway by celastrol will upregulate antioxidant enzymes including HO-1, thereby enhancing the cell’s ability to neutralize ROS and mitigate oxidative damage (Catanzaro et al., 2020; Du et al., 2019). Enhanced antioxidant defenses contribute to the maintenance of mitochondrial integrity and facilitate proper lysosomal function and autophagic flux in the RPE. This is critical not only for cellular survival under oxidative stress but also for the preservation of proper phagocytic functions necessary for efficient POS clearance (Du et al., 2019). Additionally, studies indicate that improvements in mitochondrial function via SIRT3 upregulation—in part mediated by celastrol—may further support the energy-dependent processes underlying phagocytosis and intracellular recycling (Du et al., 2019). As a result, the expected outcomes in the proposed assay are multi-fold: a decrease in inflammatory signaling, an increase in cellular antioxidant capacity, preservation or restoration of phagocytic receptor expression (such as MERTK), and overall enhanced clearance of POS debris by RPE cells. Taken together, these effects would slow or even halt the degenerative processes characteristic of dry AMD, thereby preserving retinal structure and function.

Overall Evaluation:  
Celastrol represents a promising therapeutic candidate for dry AMD based on its multi-targeted mechanisms that directly address the disease’s central pathophysiological processes—namely, chronic inflammation and oxidative stress. One of the fundamental strengths of celastrol is its dual modulatory action. By inhibiting the NF-κB pathway, celastrol effectively lowers the production of proinflammatory cytokines that are linked to the downregulation of MERTK and disruption of RPE phagocytic function (Zhang et al., 2019). This mechanism is particularly relevant for AMD, where uncontrolled inflammation contributes not only to RPE degeneration but also to subsequent retinal dysfunction. Simultaneously, the activation of the Nrf2 pathway and upregulation of antioxidant enzymes such as HO-1 play a pivotal role in neutralizing the excessive oxidative stress that drives RPE cell damage in dry AMD (Catanzaro et al., 2020; Du et al., 2019). Additionally, the ability of celastrol to modulate SIRT3 expression and enhance autophagic processes provides further mechanistic support for its potential to restore the balance between cellular degradation pathways and survival, thereby supporting the clearance of photoreceptor outer segments and maintenance of retinal health (Du et al., 2019).  

From a therapeutic history perspective, celastrol has been extensively studied in preclinical models of inflammation, oxidative stress, and ocular diseases. Its protective effects in ARPE-19 cells, including reduced ROS production, decreased apoptosis, and improved autophagy, are particularly encouraging for its repurposing in dry AMD (Du et al., 2019). Moreover, in vivo studies demonstrating neuroprotective effects in retinal ganglion cell models and protection against light-induced retinal degeneration further underscore its potential ocular benefits (Kyung et al., 2015; Law, 2022). These data collectively suggest that celastrol’s mechanisms of action intersect effectively with the molecular dysfunctions observed in dry AMD, making it a candidate with strong scientific rationale for further investigation.

Despite these strengths, several weaknesses and challenges must be considered. A primary limitation of celastrol is its poor water solubility and low bioavailability, which have been highlighted as significant hurdles in its clinical development (Cascao et al., 2017; Chan et al., 2024). The narrow therapeutic window and potential systemic toxicity observed at higher doses necessitate careful dose optimization and the development of advanced drug delivery systems—such as nanocarriers or liposomal formulations—that could improve its ocular bioavailability and target specificity (Cascao et al., 2017; Cheng et al., 2021). Additionally, while there is a substantial body of data supporting celastrol’s anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, most of these studies have been conducted in vitro or in non-AMD-specific animal models. Therefore, direct evidence of its efficacy in dry AMD models—particularly those that recapitulate the complex interplay between oxidative stress, inflammation, and impaired phagocytic function in the RPE—is still limited (Zhao et al., 2022; Gu et al., 2018).  

The hypothesis underlying the proposed research is compelling: by inhibiting NF-κB signaling and activating Nrf2-mediated antioxidant responses, celastrol could restore the balance between inflammation and cellular defense mechanisms in RPE cells, ultimately preserving the expression of phagocytic receptors like MERTK and enhancing lysosomal function. This restoration is expected to improve the clearance of photoreceptor outer segments, a process critically impaired in dry AMD (Venkatesha & Moudgil, 2016; Du et al., 2019). However, it is important to note that the actual in vitro and in vivo efficacy of celastrol in improving POS clearance and maintaining RPE function must be rigorously demonstrated through additional experiments. Furthermore, while there are encouraging indications regarding ocular safety from rodent models, the translation of these findings to human clinical settings involves further validation regarding pharmacokinetic properties, dosing regimens, and long-term safety profiles (Kyung et al., 2015; Gu et al., 2018).

In summary, celastrol offers a robust mechanistic rationale for repurposing in the treatment of dry AMD by simultaneously targeting key pathological processes—chronic inflammation mediated by NF-κB and oxidative stress mitigated by Nrf2. The compound’s ability to enhance antioxidant defenses while suppressing deleterious inflammatory cascades holds considerable promise for preserving RPE function, maintaining photoreceptor outer segment clearance, and ultimately slowing the progression of retinal degeneration. Nonetheless, challenges related to its formulation, delivery, and potential systemic toxicity are significant and must be addressed through advanced drug development strategies and further preclinical testing in AMD-specific models. Given these considerations, celastrol stands as an attractive but complex candidate for dry AMD therapy, warranting a comprehensive research program to optimize its clinical potential while mitigating associated risks (Cascao et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2019).

Overall, the evidence supports continued investigation into celastrol’s application in dry AMD, particularly focusing on detailed pharmacodynamic studies in RPE systems, optimization of ocular delivery methods, and rigorous safety and efficacy evaluations in well-established dry AMD animal models. If these hurdles can be successfully overcome, celastrol may emerge as a valuable addition to the currently limited therapeutic arsenal available for the treatment of dry AMD, offering hope for addressing a significant unmet medical need in age-related retinal degeneration (Zhang et al., 2019; Du et al., 2019).
