Proposal for Recombinant human Protein S (Pros1; R&D Systems Cat. No. 1098-PS)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Recombinant human Protein S, also known as Pros1, is a naturally occurring vitamin K–dependent plasma glycoprotein that functions both as an anticoagulant and as a ligand for members of the TAM (Tyro3/Axl/Mer) receptor family. Its biological origins trace back to its purification from human plasma, with early characterizations dating to its discovery in the late 1970s, and over time it has been elucidated to play critical roles not only in blood coagulation but also in efferocytosis and cellular clearance mechanisms. The recombinant form is synthesized using modern expression systems under strict good manufacturing practice (GMP) conditions, ensuring batch‐to‐batch consistency and purity for research and potential clinical applications. Protein S belongs to the therapeutic class of biologics, and like other recombinant proteins, it is designed to mimic the endogenous molecule’s activity. This class of compounds has historically been applied in contexts ranging from coagulation regulation to modulation of immune responses in inflammatory settings, with Protein S specifically recognized for its ability to act as a bridging molecule between apoptotic cells and phagocytes by binding phosphatidylserine via its N‐terminal Gla domain and engaging TAM receptors through its C‐terminal domains (Rothlin et al., 2015).

Therapeutic History:  
Protein S has been extensively studied for its role as an anticoagulant in hemostasis, where it potentiates activated protein C to inhibit coagulation factors and maintain vascular homeostasis. In addition to its role in the coagulation cascade, Protein S also exerts non‐coagulation effects through its function as a ligand for TAM receptors, particularly MerTK and Tyro3. Over the past two decades, biochemical and cell-based studies have highlighted its ability to facilitate efferocytosis in macrophages and retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells by bridging phosphatidylserine on apoptotic cell surfaces to MerTK receptors, thereby promoting internalization and clearance (Lumbroso et al., 2018; Lemke, 2015). In the context of retinal biology, several published reports have demonstrated that Protein S stimulates photoreceptor outer segment (POS) phagocytosis by RPE cells. For instance, studies have shown a dose-dependent stimulatory effect of Protein S on RPE phagocytosis, with evidence that its expression follows a circadian bimodal rhythm matching the peak of phagocytic activity in the retina (Nandrot, 2018). Although the majority of therapeutic uses of Protein S have been traditionally focused on coagulation or its immunomodulatory roles, its involvement in retinal maintenance—particularly the clearance of spent photoreceptor outer segments—has made it an intriguing candidate for repurposing in dry age-related macular degeneration (dry AMD). To date, however, there are no reports of clinical use of recombinant human Protein S in dry AMD or similar retinal diseases, and its potential remains evaluated only in preclinical RPE and in vivo models (Parinot et al., 2024; Lumbroso et al., 2018).

Mechanism of Action:  
The therapeutic activity of Protein S in this context hinges on its well-characterized molecular interaction with cell-surface receptors in the TAM family, particularly MerTK. Protein S contains a gamma-carboxylated N-terminal Gla domain that confers Ca²⁺-dependent binding to phosphatidylserine (PtdSer), an “eat-me” signal externalized on apoptotic cells and discarded photoreceptor outer segments. Once bound to PtdSer, Protein S serves as a bridging molecule by engaging with immunoglobulin-like domains on the extracellular portion of the MerTK receptor via its C-terminal SHBG (sex hormone-binding globulin) domains (Lemke, 2015; Lumbroso et al., 2018). This ligand–receptor interaction induces MerTK dimerization and subsequent trans-autophosphorylation of its intracellular kinase domains, thereby triggering downstream signaling cascades essential for phagocytosis. The activated receptor recruits adaptor proteins such as Vav and components of the p130Cas/CrkII/Dock180 complex, which ultimately leads to the activation of the Rho family GTPase Rac. Rac activation triggers the WAVE regulatory complex and subsequent Arp2/3-mediated actin polymerization, ultimately driving the membrane remodeling necessary for engulfing outer segments (Dransfield et al., 2015; Nandrot, 2018).

Importantly, recent mechanistic studies have suggested that Protein S and Gas6, another TAM ligand, can have opposing roles in RPE phagocytosis. Evidence indicates that while Protein S acts as a stimulatory ligand for MerTK-mediated phagocytosis (enhancing binding and internalization of POS), Gas6 may exert inhibitory effects by promoting the cleavage of the extracellular domain of MerTK, thereby generating a soluble receptor (sMerTK) that acts as a decoy (Nandrot, 2018; Parinot et al., 2024). Site-directed mutagenesis experiments have further revealed that Protein S and Gas6 recognize distinct amino acid residues on the Ig-like domains of MerTK, supporting the idea that Protein S might preferentially activate MerTK via a unique binding mode that minimizes receptor cleavage and sustains intracellular signaling for effective phagocytosis (Nandrot, 2018; Parinot et al., 2024). Additionally, experimental data indicate that Protein S alone may be sufficient to elicit phagocytosis by RPE cells, which is an essential feature given that genetic knockout studies show that mice lacking both ligands exhibit severe retinal degeneration similar to that observed in MerTK-deficient models (Nandrot, 2018; Parinot et al., 2024). The differential temporal expression of Protein S relative to Gas6—where Protein S levels increase just before and during the phagocytic peak in the interphotoreceptor matrix—further supports its specialized role in ensuring precise circadian regulation of phagocytosis by RPE cells (Nandrot, 2018; Parinot et al., 2024).

Expected Effect:  
Based on the above mechanistic insights, it is hypothesized that exogenous administration of recombinant human Protein S at a concentration of 5 µg/mL will strongly stimulate MerTK-mediated phagocytosis in RPE cells. In vitro, this therapeutic intervention is expected to enhance both the binding and internalization phases of photoreceptor outer segment (POS) uptake. The anticipated experimental outcome is a robust increase in phagocytic activity in RPE cell culture models, which are typically used to recapitulate the phagocytosis defects observed in dry AMD—where impaired clearance of POS contributes to accumulation of toxic debris and subsequent retinal degeneration (Nandrot, 2018; Parinot et al., 2024). Mechanistically, the addition of exogenous Protein S is expected to reduce the cleavage of membrane-bound MerTK, thereby maintaining high levels of active receptor on the RPE surface; this effect is crucial since increased soluble MerTK (sMerTK) has been associated with decreased phagocytic efficiency (Parinot et al., 2024; Lieffrig et al., 2023). In models of MERTK mutation, which mimic aspects of dry AMD pathogenesis, Protein S supplementation has been shown to rescue defective phagocytosis, thus restoring the internalization of outer segments to near-normal levels (Parinot et al., 2024). Since the normal functioning RPE relies on a finely balanced interplay between multiple phagocytic signals—including those mediated by integrins and other bridging molecules such as MFG-E8—the specific targeting of MerTK activation by Protein S is expected to complement these pathways without triggering competing inhibitory signals from Gas6, which has been reported to have higher affinity for MerTK but may paradoxically inhibit phagocytosis through enhanced receptor cleavage (Parinot et al., 2024).

Furthermore, the proposed biological activity is supported by the temporal and spatial expression patterns observed in vivo; Protein S is not only produced by RPE cells but also by photoreceptors themselves, and its rhythmic expression in the interphotoreceptor matrix suggests that it is naturally poised to regulate the daily burst of phagocytosis required for photoreceptor maintenance (Nandrot, 2018; Parinot et al., 2024). The anticipated improvement in phagocytic clearance is expected to alleviate the accumulation of POS debris—a key pathogenic feature implicated in the progression of dry AMD—and may also reduce the resultant inflammatory milieu that contributes to RPE atrophy and photoreceptor demise (Nandrot, 2018; Lumbroso et al., 2018).

Overall Evaluation:  
The overall potential of recombinant human Protein S as a therapeutic candidate for dry age-related macular degeneration is promising but not without challenges. One key strength lies in the fundamental and evolutionarily conserved role of Protein S in mediating efferocytosis and phagocytosis through MerTK activation; this function is particularly critical in the RPE, where continuous clearance of photoreceptor outer segments is essential for retinal homeostasis (Nandrot, 2018; Parinot et al., 2024). The existing preclinical literature provides strong support that Protein S has a stimulatory effect on POS phagocytosis by RPE cells and that its loss or dysregulation is associated with pathogenic retinal changes that mirror those seen in dry AMD (Lumbroso et al., 2018; Parinot et al., 2024). In addition, the fact that recombinant human Protein S is GMP-manufacturable with favorable pharmacokinetic properties increases its attractiveness as a candidate for intravitreal or subretinal delivery, which allows for highly localized therapeutic effects while minimizing systemic exposure and potential off-target bleeding risks that might be associated with its role in coagulation (Lemke, 2015; Rothlin et al., 2015).

Another significant strength is the mechanistic rationale suggesting that Protein S, unlike Gas6, may preferentially activate MerTK without inducing deleterious receptor cleavage. Studies have demonstrated that while Gas6 can inhibit RPE phagocytosis by augmenting the cleavage of MerTK’s extracellular domain and generating a decoy receptor, Protein S acts to reduce this cleavage and thereby preserve receptor-mediated signaling necessary for efficient phagocytosis (Nandrot, 2018; Parinot et al., 2024). Furthermore, protein engineering and mutagenesis studies indicate that the unique binding determinants utilized by Protein S may be exploited to design therapeutic strategies that selectively amplify beneficial signaling cascades in the diseased retina (Nandrot, 2018; Parinot et al., 2024).

Notwithstanding these promising aspects, there are notable weaknesses and uncertainties that must be addressed in further preclinical development. One major concern is the complex interplay between Gas6 and Protein S in modulating MerTK activity. Several studies have indicated that while Protein S is capable of stimulating phagocytosis, Gas6 is expressed at substantially higher levels in retinal tissues and might dominate the receptor activation milieu under certain conditions (Parinot et al., 2024). This raises the question of whether exogenously delivered Protein S can indeed override the inhibitory influence of endogenous Gas6 or if it must be co-administered with other agents to effectively shift the balance toward MerTK activation. Additionally, while the hypothesis is based on in vitro findings in cultured RPE cell models, the translation of these results into an in vivo setting remains uncertain given the heterogeneity and complex microenvironment of the retina in dry AMD, which involves oxidative stress, complement activation, and chronic inflammation (Nandrot, 2018; Parinot et al., 2024).

Another potential downside relates to the multifunctional nature of Protein S. Its well-established anticoagulant activity, while advantageous in managing thrombotic disorders, could pose a risk if systemic leakage occurs following ocular administration. Although intravitreal delivery is likely to confine the drug’s distribution to the eye, careful pharmacokinetic and toxicity studies are essential to ensure that any systemic absorption does not lead to bleeding complications or interfere with hemostasis elsewhere in the body (Lemke, 2015; Rothlin et al., 2015). Moreover, the exact composition and post-translational modifications of the recombinant protein must be meticulously controlled to maintain its intended bioactivity; for instance, the vitamin K–dependent gamma-carboxylation of the Gla domain is critical for its phosphatidylserine binding and subsequent receptor activation, and any perturbation in this process during manufacturing could compromise its therapeutic efficacy (Rothlin et al., 2015; Tasnim, 2020).

There is also the possibility that in the diseased state of dry AMD, RPE cells might have an altered responsiveness to TAM receptor ligands due to chronic oxidative damage or inflammatory insults, thereby diminishing the therapeutic effect of Protein S. Thus, while the restoration of phagocytosis by Protein S appears mechanistically sound in controlled cell culture systems, its real-world impact on rescuing retinal function and halting disease progression will depend on the ability to deliver the protein at effective concentrations and for sustained durations. In addition, optimal dosing studies are needed to determine whether 5 µg/mL is indeed the best concentration for stimulating phagocytosis in damaged RPE cells, as pharmacodynamic variability among patients could require individualized dosing regimens (Parinot et al., 2024).

In summary, recombinant human Protein S represents a novel and highly rational approach for addressing the impaired phagocytosis observed in dry age-related macular degeneration. Its biological function as a MerTK ligand and its ability to rescue phagocytic deficits in RPE models are compelling, and the mechanistic differentiation from Gas6 provides a unique therapeutic avenue that could potentially restore the delicate balance of photoreceptor outer segment turnover in the retina (Nandrot, 2018; Parinot et al., 2024; Lemke, 2015). The strengths of the candidate include its endogenous nature, the availability of robust recombinant production methods, and a solid mechanistic framework that implicates preferential MerTK activation as a means to boost phagocytosis. Conversely, the weaknesses center on the uncertain dominance of Protein S over Gas6 in a complex in vivo environment, potential off-target effects related to its anticoagulant activity, and the challenges inherent in translating in vitro RPE cell model data into effective clinical outcomes in patients with dry AMD.

Given the current literature, further detailed preclinical studies are warranted to assess the efficacy of recombinant human Protein S in appropriate animal models of dry AMD, to optimize dosing strategies, and to address any safety concerns that may arise from its systemic pharmacology. Nonetheless, the mechanistic data supporting its role in promoting the clearance of photoreceptor debris and in potentially mitigating the downstream inflammatory responses that drive RPE degeneration suggest that this candidate holds considerable promise for inclusion in the therapeutic pipeline targeting dry AMD (Nandrot, 2018; Parinot et al., 2024). The overall evaluation is that while there is substantial preclinical mechanistic support for the use of recombinant Protein S in stimulating MerTK-mediated POS phagocytosis and rescuing RPE function, careful further experimentation is essential to validate these findings in clinically relevant settings before committing to a full clinical development program.
