Proposal for Agonist anti-MERTK antibody (Merestinib-like; R&D Systems Cat. No. MAB8916)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
The proposed therapeutic candidate is a bivalent agonist anti-MerTK antibody (Merestinib-like; R&D Systems Cat. No. MAB8916) designed to target the extracellular domain of the Mer tyrosine kinase (MerTK) receptor. MerTK is a receptor tyrosine kinase expressed in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells that plays a critical role in the daily clearance of photoreceptor outer segments (POS). The antibody is engineered to mimic the binding of natural ligands, such as Growth Arrest–Specific 6 (Gas6), by binding with high affinity (EC₅₀ ~ 10 nM as hypothesized) and promoting receptor dimerization, which is essential for activating downstream signaling cascades involved in phagocytosis. As a monoclonal antibody, it falls within a class of biologics that have been used successfully in oncology, autoimmune disorders, and other degenerative diseases. Historically, therapeutic antibodies have been harnessed not only for neutralizing targets but also for agonizing receptors to restore deficient signaling. Here, the candidate is repurposed for dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) based on extensive preclinical evidence demonstrating that MerTK activation enhances actin-mediated phagocytic cup formation and promotes the efficient uptake of POS debris—a function that becomes compromised in aging RPE (Albert et al., 2015).

The synthesis of such an antibody typically involves recombinant DNA techniques wherein the variable regions, optimized for high-affinity binding to the MerTK extracellular domain, are fused to constant regions of immunoglobulins to generate a full-length IgG. The bivalency inherent to IgG molecules is particularly advantageous because it promotes receptor cross-linking, a prerequisite for MerTK autophosphorylation and full activation of its signaling functions (Ghosh et al., 2024; Artero-Castro et al., 2021). In this context, the candidate can be considered a repurposed biologic with a new application in the modulation of RPE function in dry AMD—a disease for which few effective treatments currently exist.  

Therapeutic History:  
The significance of MerTK in retinal pigment epithelium physiology has been firmly established in both preclinical and ex vivo studies. Pioneering work has shown that MerTK is essential for POS phagocytosis, as evidenced by experiments in which MerTK deficiency in animal models such as the Royal College of Surgeons (RCS) rat leads to the accumulation of photoreceptor debris and subsequent retinal degeneration (Albert et al., 2015; Lew et al., 2020). In addition, studies using human embryonic stem cell–derived RPE and gene-corrected patient-derived hiPSC-RPE have demonstrated that restoring MerTK expression correlates with a significant recovery of phagocytic capacity, with phagocytosis rates in corrected cells approaching those seen in healthy controls (Almedawar et al., 2020; Artero-Castro et al., 2021).  

While many prior studies have investigated the use of natural ligands, such as Gas6 or Protein S, to stimulate MerTK-mediated phagocytosis (Albert et al., 2015; Cao et al., 2016), there is comparatively little clinical or preclinical data evaluating an antibody-based agonist approach to directly activate MerTK in the context of dry AMD. Research employing small molecule modulators—for example, the use of tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA) that can restore MerTK phosphorylation in RPE cells (Murase et al., 2015)—provides additional evidence that pharmacological activation of MerTK can reverse phagocytic deficits. However, antibody-mediated receptor agonism represents a unique modality that offers the potential for high specificity and controlled activation, with a dosing regimen that could be similar to intravitreal injections already common in retinal therapeutics. Despite a strong theoretical rationale, direct clinical or veterinary application of MerTK agonists for dry AMD remains to be established, making this candidate an innovative repurposing strategy targeting a well-validated mechanism in RPE homeostasis (Albert et al., 2015; Ghosh et al., 2024; Li, 2013).

Mechanism of Action:  
MerTK is a member of the TAM (Tyro3, Axl, MerTK) receptor family and is integral to the phagocytic clearance of POS debris—a daily task required to maintain retinal homeostasis and photoreceptor health. Under physiological conditions, MerTK is activated when its extracellular domain binds to its natural ligands (e.g., Gas6), which leads to receptor dimerization and subsequent autophosphorylation on key tyrosine residues (such as Tyr-867). This phosphorylation event creates docking sites for intracellular adaptor proteins and kinases, including GRB2, phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K), and focal adhesion kinase (FAK). Downstream of these interactions, small GTPases such as Rac1 are activated, triggering actin cytoskeletal reorganization necessary for the formation of the phagocytic cup that physically engulfs the POS (Albert et al., 2015; Ghosh et al., 2024; Szatmári-Tóth, 2016).  

The candidate antibody is designed to bind to the MerTK extracellular domain in a bivalent manner, effectively mimicking the natural ligand’s mode of receptor engagement. By cross-linking MerTK molecules on the surface of RPE cells, the antibody induces receptor dimerization, leading to robust autophosphorylation. This activation is expected to enhance intracellular signaling pathways that govern both phagocytosis and the anti-inflammatory functions of MerTK, a dual role critical for maintaining retinal integrity (Ghosh et al., 2024; Murase et al., 2015). Notably, studies have demonstrated that when MerTK is activated—either through natural ligand engagement or by compounds that promote its phosphorylation—RPE cells display increased uptake of photoreceptor outer segments, thereby preventing the toxic accumulation of cellular debris that is characteristic of dry AMD (Albert et al., 2015; Cao et al., 2016).  

At the molecular level, the cross-linking effect is central to the candidate’s mechanism of action; the bivalent antibody not only occupies the ligand binding site but also forces an active dimer configuration that is necessary to initiate downstream signaling. This mechanism is analogous to how engineered agonist antibodies have been employed in other receptor systems to restore deficient signaling pathways. Furthermore, MerTK activation has been linked to the modulation of anti-inflammatory cascades, which contributes to cell survival and tissue homeostasis—a particularly relevant benefit in the context of retinal degeneration where secondary inflammation plays a major causative role (Murase et al., 2015; Albert et al., 2015).  

Expected Effect:  
In in vitro assays employing human RPE cells, including primary cultures and hiPSC-derived RPE, the expected outcome following treatment with the agonist anti-MerTK antibody is multifaceted. First, engagement of the antibody with MerTK should result in a dose-dependent increase in receptor autophosphorylation, which can be measured using phospho-specific antibodies against key tyrosine residues (e.g., Tyr-867). This biochemical readout will serve as a direct indicator of receptor activation. Second, the activation of MerTK is anticipated to stimulate downstream signaling pathways that culminate in the reorganization of the actin cytoskeleton via Rac1 activation. This cytoskeletal remodeling is critical for the formation of the phagocytic cup—a dynamic structure that facilitates the engulfment and internalization of photoreceptor outer segments (Albert et al., 2015; Ghosh et al., 2024; Szatmári-Tóth, 2016).  

Functionally, enhanced MerTK signaling is predicted to lead to increased phagocytic activity in RPE cells. This improvement could be quantified using established phagocytosis assays that involve adding labeled photoreceptor outer segments (or surrogate particles such as pHrodo-conjugated substrates) to cultured cells. For example, studies have shown that restoring MerTK expression in MERTK-deficient cells via gene correction can recover phagocytosis rates to approximately 73% of those observed in healthy cells (Artero-Castro et al., 2021). By directly stimulating MerTK with the candidate antibody, one would expect to see a similarly significant enhancement of POS clearance in RPE cells, potentially mitigating the buildup of lipofuscin and other toxic debris that drive the degenerative process in dry AMD (Artero-Castro et al., 2021; Albert et al., 2015).  

Moreover, given MerTK’s known anti-inflammatory roles, antibody-mediated activation might also suppress the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and limit microglial activation—a secondary benefit that could help stabilize the retinal environment. The combination of improved debris clearance and reduced inflammation is particularly appealing in dry AMD, where the failure of RPE phagocytosis initiates a cascade of cellular stress and immune activation (Ghosh et al., 2024; Lieffrig et al., 2023). The overall expected effect is not only the enhancement of phagocytic activity but also the restoration of a more homeostatic and anti-inflammatory state in the RPE, potentially delaying or even reversing the progression of retinal degeneration.  

The effect is anticipated to be measurable at multiple levels: biochemical activation of MerTK (through increased phosphorylation levels), morphological changes in RPE cells (such as enhanced formation of actin-rich phagocytic cups), and functional recovery of phagocytic ability as demonstrated by quantitative in vitro POS uptake assays. In preclinical animal models, it is conceivable that such improvements in RPE function would translate to decreased subretinal debris accumulation, preservation of photoreceptor integrity, and ultimately improved visual function.  

Overall Evaluation:  
The overall evaluation of the agonist anti-MerTK antibody candidate—MAB8916—reveals a promising yet challenging therapeutic approach for dry AMD. One of the foremost strengths of this candidate is the robust preclinical evidence supporting the central role of MerTK in RPE cell physiology and its critical involvement in the daily clearance of photoreceptor outer segments. Data from multiple studies have firmly established that compromised MerTK function, whether due to genetic mutations, reduced expression, or receptor cleavage, leads to the accumulation of cellular debris, the formation of toxic lipofuscin, and subsequent retinal degeneration (Albert et al., 2015; Puddu et al., 2022). Restoring or enhancing MerTK activity, therefore, represents a highly attractive intervention point in the context of dry AMD.  

The candidate’s mechanism—using a bivalent antibody to mimic the natural agonist Gas6—offers several advantages. It promises a high degree of specificity, as the antibody is designed to bind exclusively to the MerTK extracellular domain, thereby reducing the likelihood of off-target effects. The bivalent nature facilitates receptor dimerization, a prerequisite for effective signaling, and may provide a more sustained activation compared to monovalent ligands or small molecules that could be rapidly cleared from the vitreous (Ghosh et al., 2024; Albert et al., 2015). Moreover, antibody-based therapies have a well-established clinical track record in ophthalmology, as exemplified by anti-VEGF agents used in neovascular AMD; this precedent suggests that intravitreal delivery of a MerTK agonist could be feasible from both a formulation and safety perspective.

However, the candidate is not without potential weaknesses and challenges. One significant concern is the inherent complexity of MerTK signaling, which plays dual roles in promoting phagocytosis and suppressing inflammation. While activation of MerTK is beneficial for enhancing POS uptake, there is a risk that excessive or prolonged stimulation could lead to receptor desensitization or dysregulation of downstream pathways. For instance, unrestrained MerTK activity might alter the delicate balance between phagocytosis and anti-inflammatory signaling, potentially resulting in unexpected cellular stress or adverse immune reactions (Hamm et al., 2022; Law et al., 2015). Furthermore, the redundancy within the TAM receptor family—particularly the compensatory interplay between MerTK, AXL, and TYRO3—raises the question of whether agonism of MerTK alone will be sufficient to fully restore phagocytic function in a disease state that is also influenced by changes in the expression of these other receptors (Tasnim, 2020; Ghosh et al., 2024).  

Safety considerations are paramount. Preclinical studies using MerTK inhibitors have demonstrated that inhibition of this receptor causes severe retinal toxicity due to the blockade of essential phagocytic processes (Hamm et al., 2022; Law et al., 2015). Although the proposed strategy is the converse—seeking to enhance MerTK activity—the fine line separating therapeutic activation from potential overstimulation must be carefully navigated. The dosing regimen, duration of exposure, and biodistribution of the antibody will all need to be rigorously optimized in appropriate animal models before progressing to clinical trials. Immunogenicity is another potential drawback; as with all biologics, the possibility of eliciting an immune response, particularly in the immune-privileged environment of the eye, cannot be discounted and will require thorough investigation.

From a translational perspective, the relative novelty of employing an agonist antibody for MerTK activation in dry AMD implies that there is limited historical data on which to base dosing strategies or predict long-term outcomes (Albert et al., 2015; Murase et al., 2015; Li, 2013). Although analogous approaches in other therapeutic areas provide a useful framework, the unique biology of the retina and the multifactorial nature of dry AMD necessitate careful and comprehensive preclinical validation. This includes detailed pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic studies, assessment of retinal structure and function in animal models (such as the RCS rat or other models of AMD), and exploration of potential synergistic or antagonistic interactions with other retinal cell signaling pathways.

Despite these challenges, the targeted approach of using an agonist anti-MerTK antibody is highly attractive. The preclinical evidence underscores that even modest enhancements in MerTK-mediated phagocytosis can lead to significant improvements in RPE function and potentially slow the progression of AMD. For instance, studies in which MerTK expression was restored or augmented in deficient models resulted in a significant recovery of phagocytic capacity and a reduction in the accumulation of toxic debris (Artero-Castro et al., 2021; Cao et al., 2016). Such findings provide a strong mechanistic rationale for the therapeutic hypothesis being tested here. Moreover, by directly addressing a primary pathogenic mechanism in dry AMD—namely, the failure of RPE cells to clear shed photoreceptor outer segments—the candidate offers a targeted intervention that could complement or even surpass currently available treatments, which largely focus on managing neovascular complications rather than halting the underlying degenerative process.

In summary, the agonist anti-MerTK antibody candidate (MAB8916) presents a novel, mechanism-based therapeutic strategy for dry AMD. Its design to mimic the natural ligand Gas6 and directly stimulate MerTK signaling aligns well with the extensive body of preclinical evidence highlighting the central role of MerTK in RPE phagocytosis and retinal homeostasis (Albert et al., 2015; Ghosh et al., 2024; Albert et al., 2015). The anticipated outcomes include increased MerTK phosphorylation, enhanced actin-cup formation, and improved POS uptake, which together would address the defective clearance mechanism that underlies progressive retinal degeneration in dry AMD. While there are challenges associated with ensuring a balanced activation of MerTK without triggering adverse effects, the strategy benefits from a strong scientific rationale and the potential for high specificity. Further investigation in advanced in vitro models (such as primary human RPE cultures and hiPSC-derived RPE systems) and in vivo studies in animal models of retinal degeneration will be crucial to establish the safety, dosing, and efficacy profile of this candidate before it can be considered for clinical development.

Overall, the candidate’s strengths lie in its targeted mechanism of action, its potential to restore a key physiological process in the RPE, and the promising preclinical data that underscore the importance of MerTK in retinal health (Ghosh et al., 2024; Artero-Castro et al., 2021; Albert et al., 2015). Conversely, weaknesses include the limited direct evidence for antibody-mediated MerTK agonism in AMD, the potential for off-target or excessive activation effects, and the need for thorough safety and immunogenicity assessments. Given these factors, the agonist anti-MerTK antibody represents a compelling and innovative therapeutic avenue that warrants further comprehensive investigation as a candidate for treating dry age-related macular degeneration (Murase et al., 2015; Hamm et al., 2022; Law et al., 2015; Artero-Castro et al., 2021).
