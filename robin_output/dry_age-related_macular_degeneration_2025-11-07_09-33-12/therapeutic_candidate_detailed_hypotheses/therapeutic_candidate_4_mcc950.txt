Proposal for MCC950 (NLRP3 inhibitor; InvivoGen Cat. No. tlrl-mcc)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
MCC950 is a synthetic diarylsulfonylurea small molecule originally identified from an IL-1β processing inhibitor chemotype and later defined as a highly potent and selective inhibitor of the NLRP3 inflammasome complex (Coll et al., 2015). Within the inflammasome‐inhibitor landscape, MCC950 is representative of sulfonylurea‐based NLRP3 inhibitors that block canonical and noncanonical NLRP3 activation while sparing other inflammasomes (AIM2, NLRC4, NLRP1) and upstream innate pathways (e.g., TLR-NF-κB priming) (Coll et al., 2015; Akhtar-Schäfer et al., 2018). Mechanistically, MCC950 acts directly on NLRP3 to prevent assembly of the adaptor ASC (apoptosis‐associated speck-like protein containing a CARD) into oligomeric “specks,” thereby preventing caspase-1 activation and maturation of IL-1β and IL-18 (Akhtar-Schäfer et al., 2018). Follow-on mechanistic analyses indicate MCC950 binds the NLRP3 NACHT domain to inhibit its ATPase function and thereby prevent NLRP3 activation; mechanistic models include prevention of ASC oligomerization and disruption of required conformations for NEK7–NLRP3 cooperation during sensor activation (Akhtar-Schäfer et al., 2018; Zheng et al., 2023).

This class (selective NLRP3 inhibitors) has been broadly leveraged in preclinical inflammatory disease models to selectively blunt inflammasome‐driven IL-1 family cytokine maturation and pyroptosis without pan‐suppressing innate immune sensing, with documented in vivo efficacy in autoimmune encephalomyelitis, CAPS (NLRP3 gain‐of‐function), spontaneous colitis, atherosclerosis, and myocardial infarction models (Coll et al., 2015; Perera et al., 2018; Zeng et al., 2021; Wooff et al., 2020). In ocular biology, several sulfonylurea NLRP3 inhibitors—including MCC950 and chemically related CRID3/IFM compounds—reduce IL-1β secretion and cell lysis in human RPE models and ex vivo mouse RPE/choroid eye cups under diverse AMD‐relevant triggers (lysosomal membrane permeabilization, oxidative/photooxidative stress, and P2X7 activation) (Wang et al., 2019; Korhonen, 2024).

Therapeutic History  
Systemic and non‐ocular indications. MCC950 has repeatedly shown robust pharmacodynamic suppression of inflammasome outputs and disease modification in vivo. Oral dosing in a spontaneous colitis model suppressed IL-1β/IL-18 production ex vivo and improved clinical/histopathologic indices (Perera et al., 2018). In APC‐driven autoimmune encephalomyelitis and in CAPS, MCC950 reduced IL-1β production and ameliorated disease; in CAPS it rescued neonatal lethality (Coll et al., 2015). In ApoE-deficient mice, MCC950 reduced atherosclerotic plaque burden, intraplaque macrophages, and systemic TNF-α/IL-1β/IL-6/IL-18 (Zeng et al., 2021). Across these studies MCC950 demonstrates in vivo target engagement, cytokine suppression, and disease modification with high selectivity for NLRP3 (Coll et al., 2015; Perera et al., 2018; Zeng et al., 2021). However, translational reviews emphasize that many NLRP3 inhibitors—including MCC950‐class agents—face liabilities for organ penetration (including eye/brain) and potential liver toxicity, underscoring formulation/delivery and safety risks that must be addressed for ocular use (Ortega et al., 2024).

Ocular and retinal contexts. In the ischemic OIR model, MCC950 lowered retinal neovascularization and vascular leakage and shifted an aberrant cytokine balance (decreased IL-1β activity with increased IL-18 activity), with accompanying reductions in angiogenesis‐ and pericyte‐associated signaling molecules (VEGF, VEGFR1/2, MMP2/9, PDGFR-β, Ang2) and evidence of inflammasome activation localized predominantly to macrophages (F4/80+) over endothelium (Sui et al., 2020a; Sui et al., 2020b; Sui et al., 2020c). In a glaucoma model (DBA/2J), MCC950 reduced NLRP3 and cleaved caspase-1 in retina and mitigated retinal ganglion cell loss independently of intraocular pressure, indicating on‐target retinal neuroinflammation suppression (Li, 2020).

By contrast, in a laser‐induced CNV model (wet AMD), short‐course MCC950 lowered IL-1β transcript/protein but paradoxically increased total CNV lesion volume and recruitment of microglia and monocyte‐derived macrophages; ERG a- and b-waves were unchanged, suggesting no gross electrophysiological toxicity under those conditions (Dieckmann et al., 2024a; Dieckmann et al., 2024b). This aligns with genetic evidence that inflammasome–IL-1β signaling promotes CNV largely through macrophages/microglia and that non‐NLRP3 inflammasomes also contribute, implying that selectively inhibiting NLRP3 may have complex, context‐dependent effects in neovascular disease (Marneros, 2023a; Marneros, 2023b).

In dry‐AMD–relevant photo‐oxidative damage (PD), intravitreal MCC950 failed to rescue function/structure and high‐dose MCC950 worsened ERG amplitudes: 20 µM showed no functional change versus PBS and did not alter photoreceptor death or IBA-1+ microglia counts; 100 µM significantly reduced ERG function (Wooff et al., 2020b). In the same study, Casp1/11−/− mice exhibited strong protection, whereas Nlrp3−/− mice showed only partial functional preservation, and pharmacologic NLRP3 inhibition or Nlrp3 siRNA provided no benefit (and in some readouts reduced function), leading to the conclusion that downstream caspase-1 is a dominant driver whereas NLRP3 may be neither necessary nor sufficient in this model (Wooff et al., 2020a; Wooff et al., 2020b). The authors also highlighted uncertainties about NLRP3 localization (predominantly INL/GCL rather than RPE), protein detection sensitivity, and the absence of ocular pharmacokinetic data for MCC950 (Wooff et al., 2020b).

RPE cell and ex vivo ocular models. In IL-1α–primed ARPE-19 and primary human RPE (pRPE), sulfonylurea NLRP3 inhibitors reduced inflammasome activation and cell injury across diverse triggers relevant to GA, including LMP (Leu-Leu-OMe), oxidative stress (H₂O₂), lipofuscin/photooxidative insult (HNE-POS + blue light), and P2X7 activation (BzATP); in Abca4−/− eye cups, selective NLRP3 inhibitors suppressed IL-1β and LDH release and preserved RPE morphology after blue‐light irradiation (Wang et al., 2019; Korhonen, 2024). A 2024 synthesis that included MCC950 reports that MCC950, IFM-632, and IFM-514 each decreased IL-1α-primed RPE inflammasome activation independent of the activation trigger, consistent with target class effects in RPE models (Korhonen, 2024). These in vitro/ex vivo data strongly support that NLRP3 activity can be engaged in RPE and that selective, small‐molecule inhibition suppresses canonical outputs (IL-1β, LDH) under multiple AMD‐relevant insults. However, detailed analyses of autophagic flux restoration or lysosomal integrity rescue after MCC950 are not reported in these excerpts (Wang et al., 2019).

Emerging genetics for AMD‐relevant deposits. In a monogenic EFEMP1 R345W knockin model (ML/DHRD) that recapitulates basal laminar deposits (BLamDs), as well as in aged WT mice, whole‐animal deletion of Nlrp3 or Casp1 significantly reduced BLamD size/coverage, normalized proinflammatory retinal cytokine profiles, and reduced microglial activation; these data provide genetic causality linking NLRP3–caspase-1 activity to deposit formation and inflammatory milieu (Ortega et al., 2024a; Ortega et al., 2024b; Ortega et al., 2024c). The same preprint cautions that NLRP3 inhibitors frequently suffer inadequate ocular penetration and potential liver toxicity, and emphasizes the need to determine whether ocular‐local versus systemic NLRP3 suppression is required (Ortega et al., 2024b).

Mechanism of Action  
Molecular target and binding mechanism. NLRP3 is a tripartite sensor (PYD–NACHT–LRR) that oligomerizes upon priming and activation to recruit ASC via PYD–PYD interactions, which in turn polymerizes and recruits pro-caspase-1 via CARD–CARD interactions to form an active inflammasome; active caspase-1 cleaves pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 and the pore‐forming effector gasdermin D (GSDMD) to elicit cytokine secretion and pyroptosis (Coll et al., 2015; Ildefonso et al., 2016). MCC950 binds NLRP3 and prevents its ATPase activity (NACHT domain), disrupting a conformational step required for oligomerization and ASC speck assembly; in cellular systems, MCC950 suppresses both canonical (e.g., K⁺ efflux, lysosomal rupture, mitochondrial ROS) and noncanonical (caspase-11–triggered) activation pathways (Coll et al., 2015; Akhtar-Schäfer et al., 2018). Notably, MCC950 does not broadly interfere with upstream priming (NF-κB–dependent transcription of NLRP3 and cytokine precursors), K⁺ or Ca²⁺ flux, or NLRP3–ASC direct binding in simple biochemical assays; rather, it functionally prevents ASC oligomerization and caspase-1 processing, reflecting target engagement at the level of sensor activation/oligomerization (Coll et al., 2015; Akhtar-Schäfer et al., 2018). Selectivity is strong: activity against AIM2, NLRC4, and NLRP1 inflammasomes is not observed, and broad target screening reveals only weak off-target activity (Coll et al., 2015; Perera et al., 2018).

Cell-context specificity in the retina and RPE. Expression of NLRP3 and caspase-1 in the retina is cell-type and stimulus dependent. In vitro, microglia robustly upregulate Nlrp3, Casp1, and Il1b upon stimulation; Müller cells can increase Nlrp3 and Il1b without matching Casp1 elevation; photoreceptor‐line cells upregulate Nlrp3 while reducing Casp1; ARPE-19 cells often show undetectable NLRP3 protein and variable inflammasome readouts under some conditions (Wooff et al., 2020a). In situ, NLRP3 labeling is minimal in dim-reared mouse retinas but becomes detectable in the INL/GCL after PD, and AMD donor tissue shows NLRP3 in INL/GCL, suggesting inner-retina/microglial inflammasome contributions that complicate direct linkage to outer retina/RPE degeneration (Wooff et al., 2020a; Wooff et al., 2020b). Conversely, multiple human and preclinical reports demonstrate NLRP3 upregulation in macular RPE of AMD eyes with strong increases in pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 transcripts, co-localization of NLRP3 with autophagosome-like structures, and mitochondrial damage/autophagy signatures—though the literature is conflicted by antibody specificity and cell line variability (Wang et al., 2016a; Wang et al., 2016b; Marneros, 2023b). Taken together, these data imply that in AMD-relevant contexts, NLRP3 activation can occur in RPE and in inner-retinal microglia/Müller/immune cells; drug effects will be inherently cell-type dependent.

Pathway-level effects in retinal models. In ischemic retinopathy (OIR), NLRP3 activation (NLRP3/ASC/CASP1 upregulation and ASC specks) is observed primarily in macrophages and endothelium, and MCC950 reversal of an increased IL-1β/decreased IL-18 pattern is associated with reduced neovascularization and leakage, implicating vascular-cell responses to inflammasome cytokines as drivers of pathology (Sui et al., 2020a; Sui et al., 2020b). In wet AMD CNV, inflammasome activation is localized mainly to infiltrating macrophages and microglia, and IL-1β is a principal driver of lesion growth; however, pharmacological NLRP3 inhibition increased lesion size in one study despite reduced IL-1β, suggesting additional non-NLRP3 inflammasomes and compensatory mechanisms (or cell-intrinsic NLRP3 roles) shape outcomes (Marneros, 2023a; Dieckmann et al., 2024a; Dieckmann et al., 2024b).

Expected Effect  
Proposed assay and mechanism. The proposal posits that MCC950 will specifically inhibit NLRP3 assembly in RPE (IC₅₀ ~7.5 nM), reducing IL-1β/IL-18 secretion, preventing inflammasome-induced lysosomal damage, preserving autophagic flux, and thereby enhancing photoreceptor outer segment (POS) clearance. Mechanistic underpinnings exist for the first components of this cascade: (i) MCC950 demonstrates nanomolar potency and high selectivity for NLRP3 and suppresses downstream IL-1β/IL-18 (Coll et al., 2015; Akhtar-Schäfer et al., 2018); (ii) in IL-1α–primed RPE models (ARPE-19 and pRPE), sulfonylurea NLRP3 inhibitors—including MCC950 in compiled data—suppress IL-1β and LDH release across LMP, oxidative stress, lipofuscin/photooxidative, and P2X7 activation paradigms, and preserve ex vivo Abca4−/− RPE morphology under blue-light irradiation (Wang et al., 2019; Korhonen, 2024). These findings support the expected reduction of canonical inflammasome outputs in RPE assays under multiple AMD-like stimuli.

Lysosome/autophagy preservation and POS clearance. Autophagy is a key negative regulator of NLRP3 activation: it limits ROS and damaged mitochondria, targets assembled inflammasomes for degradation, and suppresses IL-1β responses; conversely, autophagy suppression increases inflammasome activation and pyroptosis in RPE (Celkova et al., 2015). AMD-relevant triggers—including lipofuscin bisretinoids (e.g., A2E) and oxidative stress—cause lysosomal stress/LMP and NLRP3 activation in RPE, and caspase-1 inhibition can prevent RPE death in LMP contexts (Ildefonso et al., 2016). However, direct evidence that MCC950 restores autophagic flux or lysosomal integrity in RPE is not reported in the cited primary RPE inhibitor studies; the RPE evidence centers on IL-1β and LDH suppression and morphological preservation in ex vivo tissue but does not include autophagy- or lysosome-specific readouts or POS phagocytosis assays (Wang et al., 2019). Therefore, while it is plausible that NLRP3 suppression will indirectly favor autophagy homeostasis in stressed RPE by damping inflammasome-driven pyroptotic signaling (and thereby reduce further lysosomal damage), the proposed “preservation of autophagy and enhanced POS clearance” remains unproven for MCC950 and should be tested explicitly (Celkova et al., 2015).

Disease- and cell-type context expectations. In dry AMD models that feature outer-retinal/RPE degeneration, the translation of RPE in vitro suppression of NLRP3 to in vivo benefit is uncertain. In PD (a dry-AMD–relevant model), intravitreal MCC950 failed to confer protection and at higher dose impaired retinal function; Nlrp3−/− mice showed only partial protection while Casp1/11−/− mice were strongly protected, arguing that downstream caspase-1 and/or other inflammasomes may be the dominant drivers in photoreceptor death (Wooff et al., 2020a; Wooff et al., 2020b). In EFEMP1 R345W and aged WT models, genetic inactivation of Nlrp3 or Casp1 reduced basal laminar deposit burden and inflammatory signatures, supporting target pathway relevance for drusen/BLamD biology, but pharmacological MCC950 efficacy in these genetic models has not been reported (Ortega et al., 2024a; Ortega et al., 2024b; Ortega et al., 2024c). Importantly, if inflammasome activation in vivo is dominated by subretinal macrophages/microglia in some contexts (e.g., autophagy-impaired RPE signals that activate macrophage inflammasomes), the expected pharmacologic effect of MCC950 may depend more on drug exposure to myeloid cells in the subretinal/choroidal space than on direct RPE engagement (Liu et al., 2016).

Finally, IL-18 biology is context-dependent: while IL-1β is broadly pro-angiogenic and disruptive, IL-18 can be protective/anti-angiogenic in neovascular settings, and AMD-relevant studies have reported discordant roles for IL-18 across disease phenotypes (Celkova et al., 2015; Ildefonso et al., 2016). Given that NLRP3 blockade reduces both IL-1β and IL-18, net effects on tissue outcomes may vary by disease stage and cell-type source; in OIR, MCC950 “normalized” an inverted IL-1β/IL-18 pattern and improved pathology (Sui et al., 2020a; Sui et al., 2020c), whereas in CNV, MCC950 increased lesion volume—implicating competing effects and non-NLRP3 inflammasomes (Dieckmann et al., 2024a).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
- On-target potency/selectivity. MCC950 is among the most potent and selective NLRP3 inhibitors described, blocking canonical and noncanonical activation at nanomolar concentrations and sparing other inflammasomes and upstream pathways; it consistently suppresses IL-1β/IL-18 maturation and caspase-1 activation in primary macrophages and multiple in vivo models (Coll et al., 2015; Akhtar-Schäfer et al., 2018; Perera et al., 2018).  
- RPE-target class activity under AMD-relevant stimuli. Sulfonylurea NLRP3 inhibitors, including MCC950 in compiled datasets, reproducibly suppress IL-1β and LDH in IL-1α–primed ARPE-19 and primary human RPE triggered by LMP, oxidative/photooxidative stress, and P2X7 activation; ex vivo Abca4−/− RPE morphology is preserved by an NLRP3 inhibitor during photooxidative stress (Wang et al., 2019; Korhonen, 2024).  
- Genetic target validation in AMD-relevant deposit biology. Whole‐body Nlrp3 or Casp1 deletion reduces BLamDs and normalizes inflammatory signatures in EFEMP1 R345W mice and even prevents spontaneous deposits in aged WT, indicating the inflammasome pathway contributes causally to deposit formation and retinal inflammation (Ortega et al., 2024a; Ortega et al., 2024b; Ortega et al., 2024c).  
- Demonstrated retinal efficacy in ischemic models and neuroinflammation. MCC950 improves vascular pathology in OIR (reduced neovascularization and leakage with cytokine-pattern reversal) and rescues retinal ganglion cells in glaucoma, supporting retinal target engagement and functional protection in relevant pathologies (Sui et al., 2020a; Sui et al., 2020b; Li, 2020).

Liabilities and gaps  
- Lack of efficacy and potential toxicity in dry-AMD–relevant PD. Intravitreal MCC950 did not improve functional or structural outcomes in PD and at 100 µM worsened ERG amplitudes; Nlrp3 siRNA similarly failed to protect despite gene knockdown; Casp1/11 deletion was robustly protective while Nlrp3 deletion was only partially protective, arguing sensor-specific inhibition may be insufficient in photoreceptor death contexts (Wooff et al., 2020b; Wooff et al., 2020a).  
- Mixed effects in neovascular disease. MCC950 increased CNV lesion volume in LCNV despite lowering IL-1β, and Nlrp3−/− mice displayed larger lesions, raising concern that NLRP3 blockade may remove protective constraints in some inflammatory/angiogenic contexts; non-NLRP3 inflammasomes likely contribute, further complicating pharmacodynamics (Dieckmann et al., 2024a; Dieckmann et al., 2024b; Marneros, 2023a).  
- Uncertain ocular delivery, exposure, and safety margin. Ocular PK for MCC950 is not defined; the PD study underscores unknown intravitreal bioavailability and clearance, and preclinical reviews warn of poor eye penetration and potential liver toxicity with many NLRP3 inhibitors; high intravitreal dose impaired ERG function in PD (Wooff et al., 2020b; Ortega et al., 2024b). While ERGs were unchanged in one CNV study, that does not establish comprehensive ocular safety (Dieckmann et al., 2024a).  
- RPE biology heterogeneity and expression uncertainty. In vivo NLRP3 induction localizes largely to inner retina/microglia rather than RPE in PD; ARPE-19 NLRP3 expression is variable in the literature and some human studies report low/undetectable NLRP3 in RPE, with concerns about antibody specificity, suggesting that direct RPE targeting may not be universally relevant across models or donors (Wooff et al., 2020a; Wooff et al., 2020b; Marneros, 2023b).  
- Unproven restoration of autophagy/lysosomal function by MCC950. Although autophagy restrains inflammasome activation and lysosomal damage is a key trigger in RPE, direct evidence that MCC950 preserves autophagic flux, lysosomal integrity, or POS processing in RPE is absent in the cited studies; current RPE data document IL-1β/LDH suppression and morphology preservation but not autophagy or phagolysosomal kinetics (Celkova et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2019).  
- Pathway redundancy suggests downstream targeting may be superior. Strong protection in Casp1/11−/− mice versus weak/absent benefits from NLRP3 inhibition in PD argue for targeting the shared downstream protease (caspase-1) or gasdermin D rather than individual sensors in retinal degeneration (Wooff et al., 2020a).

Risk-adjusted development path and decision inflection points  
- Go/no-go evidence needed in dry-AMD–relevant RPE assays. Establish whether MCC950 preserves lysosomal integrity and autophagic flux in RPE under LMP/photooxidative/P2X7 triggers and whether it restores POS digestion. Required readouts: LC3-II/I and p62 kinetics with and without bafilomycin, lysosomal pH and cathepsin activity, galectin-3 puncta for LMP, ASC speck formation, caspase-1 activity (FLICA), GSDMD cleavage, IL-1β/IL-18 maturation, and POS ingestion/digestion assays in ARPE-19 and pRPE (Celkova et al., 2015; Ranta-aho et al., 2021).  
- Ocular PK/PD and safety. Quantify intravitreal half-life, retinal/RPE/choroid distribution, and exposure–response for inflammasome suppression with MCC950; determine maximum non-toxic intravitreal dose across species. Include ERG, OCT, histopathology, IBA-1/microglial activation, and cytokine profiling. Investigate sustained intravitreal delivery strategies (e.g., silica-based nanoparticles) that have shown extended ocular release and improved anti-inflammatory efficacy for MCC950 in non-AMD contexts, while generating ocular PK and biodegradation data (Sun et al., 2023).  
- Efficacy in chronic dry-AMD models. Test MCC950 in EFEMP1 R345W mice (BLamD) and aging WT (spontaneous BLamD) with local ocular delivery; endpoints: BLamD size and coverage, RPE/Bruch’s integrity, microglial recruitment (Iba1), and cytokine panels. Given PD negative findings, prioritize deposit/atrophy models where genetic Nlrp3/Casp1 loss is protective (Ortega et al., 2024a; Ortega et al., 2024b; Ortega et al., 2024c; Wooff et al., 2020b).  
- Cell-type–specific targeting and biomarkers. Because inflammasome activation is often myeloid-dominant in vivo, delineate whether MCC950’s ocular benefits, if any, require exposure to subretinal macrophages/microglia versus RPE. Use cell-specific pharmacodynamic markers (ASC specks in Iba1+ cells vs RPE65+ cells; IL-1β/IL-18 localization) to define pharmacologic engagement (Liu et al., 2016; Wooff et al., 2020a).  
- Comparative strategy. In parallel, benchmark a caspase-1 inhibitor (or GSDMD inhibitor) against MCC950 in the same models, given the superior genetic protection with Casp1/11 deletion and the possibility of non-NLRP3 inflammasome contribution (Wooff et al., 2020a; Marneros, 2023a).

Bottom line  
MCC950 has strong mechanistic rationale, exquisite target selectivity, and reproducible suppression of inflammasome outputs in RPE assays across diverse AMD-like triggers. Genetic data incriminating NLRP3–caspase-1 in deposit biology strengthen the target hypothesis in a subset of dry-AMD phenotypes. However, the most AMD-relevant in vivo pharmacology with MCC950 to date is not supportive: no benefit and possible functional toxicity in PD, and mixed signals in neovascular settings (benefit in OIR, harm in LCNV). The translational path is further complicated by variable NLRP3 expression in RPE in vivo, dominance of myeloid inflammasomes in many retinal contexts, uncertain ocular PK/penetration, and the untested premise that MCC950 restores RPE autophagy/lysosomal function and POS clearance.

Given these data, MCC950 should not advance directly to costly IND-enabling studies for dry AMD without targeted de-risking experiments. The highest-value next steps are (i) mechanistic RPE assays that test autophagy/lysosome rescue and POS processing, (ii) rigorous ocular PK/safety to define a therapeutic index and viable delivery modality, and (iii) efficacy in deposit/atrophy models where the genetic target is validated (EFEMP1 R345W, aged WT). If these studies confirm robust ocular exposure, a favorable safety margin, and disease modification, MCC950—or a chemically optimized ocular NLRP3 inhibitor—could warrant advancement for specific dry-AMD endophenotypes. If not, downstream inflammasome targeting (caspase-1/GSDMD) may represent a more effective and generalizable strategy for retinal degeneration.

Mechanism of Action (detailed)  
NLRP3 activation requires priming (e.g., NF-κB upregulation of NLRP3 and pro-IL-1β/IL-18) and a second signal (e.g., K⁺ efflux via P2X7, lysosomal rupture with cathepsin release, mitochondrial ROS) that promotes sensor oligomerization (Coll et al., 2015; Ildefonso et al., 2016). NLRP3’s NACHT domain cycles ATP and orchestrates a conformational change enabling PYD-driven nucleation of ASC, which polymerizes into higher-order filaments/specks that template pro-caspase-1 recruitment and activation; activated caspase-1 cleaves GSDMD to induce pyroptotic pore formation and processes IL-1β/IL-18 for secretion (Coll et al., 2015; Ildefonso et al., 2016). MCC950 binds NLRP3 to suppress its ATPase activity (NACHT), preventing the conformational activation needed for ASC oligomerization and caspase-1 activation; in functional assays MCC950 suppresses both canonical and noncanonical pathways and decreases processed caspase-1 p10/p20 in supernatants without altering upstream TNF-α secretion after TLR priming (Coll et al., 2015; Akhtar-Schäfer et al., 2018). Evidence from retinal disease states aligns with these mechanisms: MCC950 reduces NLRP3 and cleaved caspase-1 in glaucomatous retina (DBA/2J), and in OIR normalizes IL-1β/IL-18 signaling and reduces pathologic neovascularization and leakage (Li, 2020; Sui et al., 2020a).

Inflammasome biology in RPE and AMD is multifactorial. AMD-associated triggers—lipofuscin photooxidation, A2E/oxidative stress, drusen lipid adducts (CEP), Alu RNA accumulation from DICER1 deficiency, and lysosomal destabilization—induce NLRP3 activation in RPE or in recruited subretinal macrophages (Ildefonso et al., 2016; Celkova et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2016). Autophagy limits NLRP3 activation by removing damaged mitochondria and targeting assembled inflammasomes for autophagosomal degradation; autophagy blockade increases IL-1β production and pyroptotic susceptibility (Celkova et al., 2015). Pharmacologic NLRP3 blockade in RPE reduces IL-1β and LDH release under LMP, oxidative, photooxidative, and P2X7 activation, consistent with suppression of caspase-1 activation and pyroptotic lysis (Wang et al., 2019). Whether these biochemical effects in RPE suffice to modify chronic, multicellular retinal degeneration in vivo depends on drug delivery to relevant compartments (RPE, subretinal macrophages/microglia), on the relative contributions of non-NLRP3 inflammasomes, and on downstream cytokine biology (e.g., IL-18’s context-dependent effects).

Therapeutic History (detailed)  
- Preclinical systemic efficacy and selectivity: MCC950 exhibits nanomolar potency for NLRP3 in human/mouse macrophages, sparing AIM2, NLRC4, NLRP1; suppresses caspase-1 p10 and IL-1β secretion; improves disease in EAE and CAPS; PK characterized; off-targets largely excluded (Coll et al., 2015; Perera et al., 2018). In atherosclerosis, MCC950 decreased plaques, macrophage content, and circulating cytokines without overt systemic organ toxicity, though cross-indication reviews caution about liver toxicity risk for the class (Zeng et al., 2021; Ortega et al., 2024b).  
- Ocular ischemic/neovascular settings: In OIR, MCC950 reversed an increased IL-1β/decreased IL-18 pattern, reduced neovascularization and leakage, and modulated endothelial/pericyte signaling; inflammasome activity localized more to macrophages than endothelium (Sui et al., 2020a; Sui et al., 2020b; Sui et al., 2020c). In LCNV, MCC950 lowered IL-1β but increased lesion volume; ERG unchanged; genetic Nlrp3 loss also increased lesion size—collectively indicating a complex, possibly protective role of NLRP3 in CNV or compensation by other inflammasomes (Dieckmann et al., 2024a; Dieckmann et al., 2024b; Marneros, 2023a).  
- Dry-AMD–relevant PD and NLRP3 targeting: Intravitreal MCC950 did not protect PD retina; 100 µM reduced ERG amplitudes. Nlrp3 siRNA reduced transcript but not pathology; Nlrp3−/− protected partially; Casp1/11−/− protected robustly, pointing to caspase-1 as the better target (Wooff et al., 2020b; Wooff et al., 2020a).  
- RPE in vitro/ex vivo: CRID3/IFM inhibitors (sulfonylureas, same target class as MCC950) reduced IL-1β and LDH across LMP, H₂O₂, lipofuscin/photooxidation, and P2X7 triggers in ARPE-19/pRPE and preserved ex vivo Abca4−/− RPE morphology under photooxidation (Wang et al., 2019). Compiled 2024 data list MCC950 alongside IFM analogs as effective in IL-1α–primed RPE independent of trigger (Korhonen, 2024).  
- Genetic validations in chronic deposit models: Nlrp3 or Casp1 deletion reduces BLamD in EFEMP1 R345W mice and in aged WT, with reduced microglial activation and normalization of cytokine profiles (Ortega et al., 2024a; Ortega et al., 2024b; Ortega et al., 2024c). These models offer strong causal support for inflammasome targeting in GA-relevant pathology, albeit via genetic ablation rather than pharmacologic inhibition.

Expected Effect (detailed)  
In ARPE-19/pRPE assays primed with IL-1α, MCC950 should suppress NLRP3 activation triggered by:  
– LMP (Leu-Leu-OMe), where caspase-1 inhibition prevents RPE death and NLRP3 inhibitors reduce IL-1β and LDH release (Ildefonso et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2019).  
– Oxidative and lipofuscin/photooxidative stress (H₂O₂, HNE-POS + blue light), where NLRP3 inhibitors significantly lower IL-1β and LDH and preserve RPE morphology ex vivo (Wang et al., 2019).  
– P2X7 activation (BzATP), a canonical AMD-relevant danger signal leading to K⁺ efflux and inflammasome assembly; selective NLRP3 inhibitors attenuate IL-1β and LDH in Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺-free conditions (Wang et al., 2019).

Because autophagy restrains inflammasome activation and removes inflammasome complexes, a reduction in NLRP3 activity may secondarily stabilize autophagic flux under stress. Yet, this remains hypothetical for MCC950 in RPE; explicit autophagy and lysosomal readouts (LC3 flux, p62, LAMP1/2, cathepsin B/D activities, lysosomal pH, galectin-3 LMP puncta) have not been detailed in the RPE inhibitor studies referenced (Celkova et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2019). Therefore, while reduction of IL-1β/LDH and preservation of morphology are expected and supported, the proposed enhancement of POS clearance should be considered an untested hypothesis requiring targeted experimentation.

In vivo, efficacy expectations diverge by model:  
– Deposit/atrophy (EFEMP1 R345W, aged WT) are rational choices: genetic deletion of Nlrp3/Casp1 is protective, so MCC950 might reduce BLamD burden and retinal inflammation if adequate ocular exposure is achieved (Ortega et al., 2024a; Ortega et al., 2024b; Ortega et al., 2024c).  
– Photoreceptor acute degeneration (PD) may not respond to NLRP3 inhibition and may be sensitive to dose-related toxicity, favoring downstream caspase-1 inhibition or broader inflammasome suppression (Wooff et al., 2020b; Wooff et al., 2020a).  
– Neovascular models diverge: OIR improved with MCC950; CNV worsened. Dry-AMD-focused development should avoid neovascular endpoints initially, or stratify hypotheses based on IL-1β/IL-18 balance and myeloid contribution (Sui et al., 2020a; Dieckmann et al., 2024b; Marneros, 2023a).

Overall Evaluation  
MCC950 is a mechanistically compelling, best-in-class NLRP3 inhibitor with credible RPE-target class activity in vitro and strong genetic support for inflammasome involvement in deposit biology. However, the in vivo ocular pharmacology to date is mixed and generally unsupportive for broad dry-AMD application without further de-risking: failure and potential toxicity in PD, context-dependent effects in neovascular models, uncertain RPE expression/engagement in vivo, lack of ocular PK, and unproven restoration of autophagy/lysosomal function in RPE.

On balance, MCC950 merits continued investigation as a mechanistic probe and potential therapeutic lead for specific dry-AMD endophenotypes linked to deposit pathology and chronic inflammasome signaling—provided that the next tranche of studies addresses ocular PK/safety, cell-type exposure, and autophagy/lysosome outcomes. In parallel, downstream targeting (caspase-1) should be benchmarked given superior genetic protection and potential coverage of multiple inflammasome sensors. If MCC950 cannot meet ocular exposure and safety requirements, alternative delivery (e.g., sustained-release nanoparticles) or next-generation NLRP3 inhibitors with better ocular PK and safety profiles may be required (Sun et al., 2023; Ortega et al., 2024b).

In summary, MCC950 has plausible potential for dry AMD if—and only if— its ocular pharmacology can be tuned to the appropriate disease biology: chronic deposit/atrophy with documented inflammasome involvement, sufficient drug exposure at RPE/subretinal myeloid compartments, and a safety margin that avoids the functional liabilities observed at higher intravitreal doses in PD. Without these, the probability of technical success remains low relative to downstream inflammasome targets.
