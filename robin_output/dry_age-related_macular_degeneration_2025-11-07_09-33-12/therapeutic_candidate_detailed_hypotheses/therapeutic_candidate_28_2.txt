Proposal for 2-Hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (HPβCD; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. C0926)

Below is a comprehensive evaluation of 2‐hydroxypropyl‐β‐cyclodextrin (HPβCD; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. C0926) as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD).

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
HPβCD is a chemically modified cyclic oligosaccharide belonging to the cyclodextrin drug class, which consists of cyclic arrangements of glucopyranose units. In the case of HPβCD, hydroxypropyl groups are substituted on the β-cyclodextrin ring, thereby increasing its water solubility and overall biocompatibility while maintaining the inherent toroidal structure with a hydrophobic inner cavity and hydrophilic exterior. This unique architecture allows HPβCD to encapsulate lipophilic compounds such as cholesterol, forming stable inclusion complexes that enhance the solubilization, mobilization, or extraction of these molecules from biological membranes or intracellular compartments (Braga, 2023; Davidson et al., 2016; El-Darzi et al., 2021; El-Darzi et al., 2023; Mahjoubin-Tehran et al., 2020). Originally discovered and adapted as an excipient to improve the solubility and delivery of hydrophobic drugs, cyclodextrins have more recently gained attention as active pharmaceutical ingredients due to their capacity to modulate lipid metabolism. In particular, HPβCD is being repurposed as a therapeutic candidate because of its established ability to reduce cholesterol accumulation in tissues—an effect that is central to its potential role in addressing dry AMD pathology (El-Darzi et al., 2021; El-Darzi et al., 2023).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, HPβCD has been widely utilized as a pharmaceutical excipient and delivery system with a strong safety record, as evidenced by its FDA-approved use in various formulations. More notably, its cholesterol-binding and mobilizing activity has driven its repurposing for treatment of cholesterol storage disorders, most prominently Niemann–Pick Type C (NPC) disease. In NPC, HPβCD has been administered by various routes—including systemic, intrathecal, and subcutaneous injections—to solubilize and extract unesterified cholesterol from lysosomal compartments, thereby ameliorating the pathological accumulation of lipids in neuronal and peripheral tissues (Davidson et al., 2016; El-Darzi et al., 2023; Kfoury et al., 2025; Rao & Fliesler, 2021). Preclinical studies in NPC models have consistently demonstrated that HPβCD can normalize cholesterol trafficking and extend lifespan in affected animals. Additional biochemical and clinical studies in related fields such as atherosclerosis further underscore the compound’s potency as a cholesterol-mobilizing agent (Braga, 2023; Coisne et al., 2016; Mahjoubin-Tehran et al., 2020). Although HPβCD has not been used explicitly in clinical trials for dry AMD to date, the mechanistic rationale for its application is compelling since retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells in dry AMD exhibit lysosomal cholesterol accumulation and associated dysfunction similar to the cellular defects observed in NPC and atherosclerotic conditions (El-Darzi et al., 2021; El-Darzi et al., 2023). Moreover, retinal studies in mouse models have shown that HPβCD can reduce cholesterol levels in the retina and restore aspects of cellular homeostasis, thereby laying the groundwork for the proposed repurposing in dry AMD (El-Darzi et al., 2021; El-Darzi et al., 2023).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, HPβCD functions primarily by exploiting its unique cyclic structure, which provides a hydrophobic cavity designed to accommodate cholesterol molecules. The hydrophobic interactions between the cyclodextrin cavity and the sterol ring system of cholesterol enable HPβCD to form a reversible inclusion complex with both free and esterified cholesterol. This process shifts the equilibrium of intracellular cholesterol pools and promotes a net efflux of cholesterol from cellular membranes and lysosomal compartments (El-Darzi et al., 2023; Mahjoubin-Tehran et al., 2020).

In cells burdened by cholesterol accumulation—such as RPE cells in the context of drusen formation—the excess cholesterol impairs the fluidity of membranes and disrupts the acidification and enzymatic activity within lysosomes. HPβCD is hypothesized to extract this excess cholesterol from the lysosomal membrane, thereby restoring proper membrane fluidity and normalizing the pH within the lysosome. This in turn is expected to reactivate lysosomal enzymes, such as cathepsins, and restore proper autophagic flux and phagocytic turnover of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) (Coisne et al., 2016; El-Darzi et al., 2021; El-Darzi et al., 2023).

On a biochemical level, studies have shown that HPβCD can upregulate proteins related to cholesterol efflux. For instance, the compound has been observed to modulate the expression of ATP-binding cassette transporters, such as ABCA1 and ABCG1, via liver X receptor (LXR)-dependent pathways. Additionally, HPβCD treatment can alter the cellular distribution of cholesterol by promoting trafficking from the lysosome to other cellular compartments such as the endoplasmic reticulum, where it is enzymatically esterified and eventually excreted (Cougnoux et al., 2023; El-Darzi et al., 2021; Mahjoubin-Tehran et al., 2020).

Another important mechanistic aspect is the compound’s influence on lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy. HPβCD has been shown to increase the abundance of lysosome-associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP1) and to activate the transcription factor EB (TFEB), which is a master regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagic processes. Such activation contributes to maintaining cellular clearance pathways that are essential for RPE cell health, especially in the face of chronic insult from photoreceptor outer segments and accumulated lipofuscin bisretinoids (Braga, 2023; Brown et al., 2016). Through these molecular interactions, HPβCD may counteract the cellular stress induced by disrupted cholesterol homeostasis, thereby mitigating the dual impact of both lipid accumulation and impaired lysosomal degradation that is central to dry AMD pathogenesis. However, it is noteworthy that some studies have reported that prolonged exposure to HPβCD can induce pro-inflammatory responses and even ototoxicity in animal models, underscoring the importance of optimizing dosing regimens (Houben et al., 2021a, 2021b; Yamada et al., 2021).

Expected Effect:  
The hypothesis driving the repurposing of HPβCD for dry AMD is that by extracting the excess cholesterol accumulated in the lysosomes of RPE cells, the compound will restore normal cellular functions. The primary expected effect is multifold. First, the removal of cholesterol is anticipated to restore lysosomal pH to its optimal acidic range. An appropriate pH is crucial for the full activation of lysosomal hydrolases, such as cathepsins, which are necessary for the degradation of photoreceptor outer segments that normally enter the RPE through phagocytosis (Braga, 2023; El-Darzi et al., 2023). Second, by normalizing membrane fluidity, HPβCD would improve the trafficking and fusion of lysosomes, thus enhancing the clearance of cellular waste. This improved clearance capacity may help resolve drusen-like deposits and prevent inflammatory activation of retinal microglia and macrophages—a process that has been implicated in the progression of dry AMD (Brown et al., 2016; El-Darzi et al., 2023; Houben et al., 2021b).

From a molecular perspective, RPE cells express key proteins involved in cholesterol handling, including NPC1, ABCA1, and LXR. The activation of these pathways after HPβCD application could lead to improved reverse cholesterol transport, where the excess cholesterol is removed from RPE cells and delivered to extracellular acceptors for clearance (Davidson et al., 2016; Mahjoubin-Tehran et al., 2020). This facilitated removal is expected to lower intracellular levels of unesterified cholesterol, reduce cholesterol crystal formation, and subsequently attenuate the pro-inflammatory milieu that contributes to RPE degeneration in dry AMD (Braga, 2023; Houben et al., 2021b).

In experimental assays, one might expect to observe a decrease in the levels of cholesterol and cholesterol crystals by biochemical analyses or imaging of RPE cells treated with HPβCD, alongside a normalization of lysosomal pH values measured via pH-sensitive dyes. Enzyme assays should reveal a restoration of cathepsin activity, and phagocytosis assays would ideally demonstrate an increased turnover of POS. Taken together, these effects would not only preserve RPE cell function but also potentially slow the progression of the disease and improve retinal structure and function (Coisne et al., 2016; El-Darzi et al., 2021; El-Darzi et al., 2023).

Overall Evaluation:  
In summary, HPβCD represents a promising repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD based on its well-documented ability to modulate cholesterol homeostasis and improve lysosomal function. The compound’s favorable physicochemical properties—stemming from its cyclic oligosaccharide structure and enhanced solubility due to hydroxypropylation—make it an attractive candidate for drug repurposing. The extensive preclinical evidence in lysosomal cholesterol storage disorders, including NPC disease and atherosclerosis models, provides a solid foundation that supports its potential efficacy in conditions characterized by intracellular cholesterol dysregulation, such as dry AMD (El-Darzi et al., 2021; El-Darzi et al., 2023).

One of the major strengths of HPβCD is its multifaceted mechanism of action. It not only physically extracts cholesterol by forming inclusion complexes but also appears to modulate key cellular pathways that govern cholesterol efflux, lysosomal biogenesis, and autophagic clearance. This combined activity has the potential to address several pathological features of dry AMD simultaneously: restoring lysosomal pH, normalizing the activity of pH-dependent enzymes like cathepsins, and enhancing the degradation of deposited photoreceptor outer segments. Additionally, the evidence that HPβCD can reduce inflammatory activation by preventing cholesterol crystal formation and macrophage recruitment further supports its application in a disease state where chronic inflammation is a well-known driver of progression (Brown et al., 2016; Mahjoubin-Tehran et al., 2020).

However, there are also notable weaknesses and concerns that must be carefully considered. First, while HPβCD’s safety profile is well-established for its role as an excipient and, to some extent, in NPC clinical trials, significant safety issues have been reported in the context of systemic and even local administration. Ototoxicity has emerged as a recurrent concern in multiple preclinical studies and some clinical observations, which could be particularly problematic if similar toxicities are encountered when used in the delicate tissues of the eye (Braga, 2023; Calias, 2018; Yamada et al., 2021). Moreover, some studies have reported dose-dependent pro-inflammatory effects upon chronic exposure to HPβCD, underscoring the necessity for optimizing dosing regimens and careful monitoring of inflammatory markers (El-Darzi et al., 2021; Hoque et al., 2020).

Another potential challenge is the delivery route. Given that the eye is a compartment with unique barriers and an immunoprivileged status, determining the optimal method of administration (e.g., intravitreal injection, topical eye drops, or systemic administration with targeted delivery) will be critical. The compound’s ability to cross the blood-retina barrier has been demonstrated in preclinical models, but achieving sufficient retinal concentration without systemic toxicity may require advanced formulation strategies, such as encapsulation in polymer-based nanoparticles or the development of polyrotaxane-based delivery systems (Coisne et al., 2016; El-Darzi et al., 2021; El-Darzi et al., 2023).

The overall evaluation is therefore cautiously optimistic. On the one hand, the strong mechanistic rationale for using HPβCD to address the underlying cholesterol dysregulation and lysosomal dysfunction in dry AMD is supported by a robust body of literature from related fields (El-Darzi et al., 2021; El-Darzi et al., 2023; Mahjoubin-Tehran et al., 2020). The compound benefits from established use in other cholesterol accumulation disorders and has clearly defined biochemical activities that could be translated into therapeutic benefits in the context of AMD. On the other hand, significant challenges remain in terms of safety—especially related to ototoxicity and potential inflammatory side effects—and in the optimization of delivery methods that can achieve effective retinal concentrations without off-target toxicity (Braga, 2023; Houben et al., 2021a; Yamada et al., 2021).

Given these considerations, it is recommended that the next phases of drug development focus on detailed pharmacokinetic and toxicity studies specific to ocular tissues, along with the exploration of novel delivery formulations that may mitigate systemic and local adverse effects. Further preclinical validation using AMD-relevant models is warranted to assess functional outcomes such as restoration of lysosomal pH, increased cathepsin activity, improved phagocytic efficiency of POS, and ultimately, preservation of retinal structure and function (Coisne et al., 2016; El-Darzi et al., 2021; El-Darzi et al., 2023).

In conclusion, while HPβCD demonstrates considerable potential as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD based on its cholesterol-extracting capabilities and beneficial effects on lysosomal function, the balance between efficacy and safety remains a critical area for further investigation. The strengths—mechanistic depth, prior clinical use in related disorders, and robust preclinical data—are compelling; however, the weaknesses, notably regarding ototoxicity and the need for optimized delivery, must be addressed before moving to clinical trials in AMD patients (Braga, 2023; El-Darzi et al., 2023; Mahjoubin-Tehran et al., 2020; Yamada et al., 2021). Continued research with targeted formulations, dosing studies, and AMD-specific preclinical models will be essential to determine whether HPβCD can be safely and effectively repurposed to improve outcomes in patients with dry age‐related macular degeneration.
