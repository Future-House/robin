Proposal for Eculizumab (Anti-C5 mAb; Alexion Pharmaceuticals)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Eculizumab is a recombinant, humanized monoclonal IgG kappa antibody that specifically binds complement component C5 and prevents its proteolytic activation into C5a and C5b, thereby blocking terminal pathway activity and membrane attack complex (MAC; C5b-9) assembly while leaving upstream C3-dependent functions largely intact (for example, opsonization and immune-complex handling) (Rother et al., 2007; Parker, 2009; Mantegazza & Cavalcante, 2020; West et al., 2024). It was discovered by screening large panels of murine anti-human C5 monoclonals for potent inhibition of C5 activation and terminal complement–mediated hemolysis; the parental murine antibody (5G1.1) was then humanized by CDR grafting into human germline frameworks and engineered to use a hybrid IgG2/IgG4 heavy-chain constant region to minimize Fcγ receptor binding and classical-pathway activation (reducing effector functions that could confound terminal pathway blockade) (Rother et al., 2007; Parker, 2009). Eculizumab binds human C5 with very high affinity (Kd approximately 120 pM) and sterically prevents convertase engagement with the MG7 domain of C5; epitope mapping identifies residues in the MG7 domain (including the KSSKC motif around Arg885) critical for binding, and Arg885His/Cys polymorphisms have been linked to clinical non-responsiveness in some patients, consistent with the structural epitope (Rother et al., 2007; Schatz-Jakobsen et al., 2016; Risitano et al., 2019).

As a drug class, complement inhibitors have been deployed to treat diseases driven by complement dysregulation, with C5 blockade representing a terminal-pathway strategy that ablates C5a signaling and MAC-mediated cytolysis while preserving many proximal complement functions; eculizumab was the first complement inhibitor approved for clinical use and catalyzed broad clinical development of C5, C3, and alternative pathway–targeted agents across hematology, nephrology, neurology, and, more recently, ophthalmology (West et al., 2024; Mantegazza & Cavalcante, 2020; Mannes et al., 2021). Eculizumab is administered intravenously, exhibits a long serum half-life (~272 ± 82 hours), a limited volume of distribution consistent with an intravascularly confined antibody, and shows pharmacodynamic correlation between serum trough concentrations and blockade of hemolytic activity; vaccination against Neisseria meningitidis is required due to increased susceptibility when terminal complement is inhibited (Rother et al., 2007; Smith et al., 2012; Parker, 2009).

Within ophthalmology, complement activation products and MAC deposition have been implicated in AMD pathogenesis, motivating clinical trials of complement inhibitors including C5-directed agents; eculizumab has been formally evaluated in a randomized phase 2 setting for non-exudative AMD (geographic atrophy and drusen cohorts) via systemic administration (Kassa et al., 2019; Yehoshua et al., 2014; ClinicalTrials.gov, 2009; Halawa et al., 2021).

Therapeutic History  
Eculizumab has an extensive clinical record in systemic complement-mediated disorders. It revolutionized therapy for paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH), where it reduces intravascular hemolysis, decreases transfusion requirements, and improves quality of life; it is also approved for atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS) and for neurologic autoimmune indications including anti–acetylcholine receptor–positive generalized myasthenia gravis and aquaporin-4–positive neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (West et al., 2024; Mantegazza & Cavalcante, 2020; Mannes et al., 2021). Early clinical pharmacology in PNH demonstrated that an induction regimen of 600 mg weekly for four weeks followed by 900 mg every other week maintained near-complete terminal pathway blockade and significantly reduced hemolysis and transfusion needs; these dosing principles align with its long half-life and the concentration dependence of hemolytic inhibition (Hillmen et al., 2004; Rother et al., 2007; Smith et al., 2012). Despite robust control of intravascular hemolysis, residual extravascular hemolysis can persist because C3 opsonization is not prevented by C5 inhibition, illustrating an important mechanistic limitation of terminal-pathway agents (Schmidt & Smith, 2023; West et al., 2024).

The drug’s safety profile is dominated by infection risk with encapsulated organisms—particularly meningococcal disease—necessitating vaccination; otherwise, eculizumab has shown acceptable tolerability across long-term use, with clinical programs incorporating monitoring of functional complement activity (CH50) to confirm pharmacodynamic blockade and dose adequacy (Smith et al., 2012; Parker, 2009; Eskandary et al., 2016). Mechanistically relevant sources also report pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic complexities: high-affinity binding at acidic endosomal pH encourages recycling of eculizumab:C5 complexes by FcRn, leading to accumulation of total C5 bound to the drug in circulation; next-generation engineering in ravulizumab introduced pH-dependent release to minimize cargo recycling and extend dosing intervals (Schmidt & Smith, 2023).

In ophthalmology, eculizumab has been investigated as a repurposed systemic therapy for dry AMD. The COMPLETE study (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2009), a single-center, randomized, double-masked phase 2 trial, evaluated intravenous eculizumab in two cohorts: geographic atrophy (GA) and intermediate AMD with high-risk drusen; patients were randomized 2:1 to eculizumab or saline over 24 weeks with follow-up to 52 weeks (Yehoshua et al., 2014; Park et al., 2021). In the GA cohort (n = 30 study eyes), eculizumab did not reduce GA enlargement versus placebo at 26 or 52 weeks (square-root–transformed growth 0.19 ± 0.12 mm vs. 0.18 ± 0.15 mm at 26 weeks, P = 0.96; 0.37 ± 0.22 mm vs. 0.37 ± 0.21 mm at 52 weeks, P = 0.93), and no drug-related adverse events were reported (Yehoshua et al., 2014; Park et al., 2021). In the drusen cohort (n = 30 total participants; 20 active, 10 placebo), eculizumab failed to meet the prespecified primary endpoint of a ≥ 50% reduction in drusen cube-root volume at 26 weeks; none of 18 treated eyes achieved this threshold, while one placebo eye did (10%; P = 0.36), and there was no detectable difference in mean drusen growth between arms through 26–52 weeks (Filho et al., 2014). Trial conduct included meningococcal vaccination and careful multimodal imaging; retention was high with 28/30 completing 52 weeks (Filho et al., 2014; Yehoshua et al., 2014; ClinicalTrials.gov, 2009). Independent evidence syntheses judged the certainty of evidence as low to very low due to small sample size, imprecision, and some risk of bias; overall, systemic eculizumab “may have made little to no difference” in BCVA and GA lesion growth in the available cohort (Tzoumas et al., 2023).

Mechanistically similar C5-directed agents have not shown positive phase 2 outcomes in GA when administered systemically or intravitreally (e.g., LFG316 failed in phase 2), whereas intravitreal C5 inhibition with avacincaptad pegol progressed to later-phase testing and regulatory review, suggesting that drug, route, and pharmacology may be important determinants of outcome; by contrast, intravitreal C3 inhibition (pegcetacoplan) demonstrated efficacy and has been approved for GA, underscoring the translational relevance of target selection and ocular delivery (Kassa et al., 2019; West et al., 2024). Multiple reviews conclude that systemic eculizumab is no longer in development for AMD and highlight delivery and mechanistic concerns as likely contributors to the negative results (Nadeem et al., 2023; Tzoumas et al., 2023).

Mechanism of Action  
At the molecular level, eculizumab targets the MG7 domain of C5 and sterically prevents formation of the C5 convertase–substrate complex, thereby inhibiting the cleavage of C5 into the potent anaphylatoxin C5a and C5b, which nucleates membrane attack complex assembly with C6–C9 (Schatz-Jakobsen et al., 2016; Rother et al., 2007). Structural analysis of an eculizumab Fab bound to C5 resolved at 4.2 Å shows that five CDRs form the principal interface with MG7; mutational scanning of CDR residues identifies a network of contacts necessary for high-affinity binding, and kinetic assays confirm that these interactions prevent complement-mediated hemolysis in vitro (Schatz-Jakobsen et al., 2016). The epitope includes residues near Arg885, and clinical resistance due to Arg885His/Cys substitutions in C5 provides orthogonal validation of the binding site (Schatz-Jakobsen et al., 2016; Risitano et al., 2019).

Pharmacodynamically, eculizumab ablates terminal pathway function: it suppresses CH50 to near zero when trough serum concentrations exceed ~35 μg/mL; however, because proximal C3 activation continues, C3b deposition and downstream opsonins accumulate on surfaces, which can drive extravascular clearance and generate residual pathology even under complete C5 blockade in appropriate disease contexts (Rother et al., 2007; Schmidt & Smith, 2023). Additional mechanisms can undermine complete terminal pathway control: (1) during intense proximal activation, densely opsonized surfaces can support C5 activation by convertases in ways that partially bypass classical C5 convertase assembly or even promote conformational C5 activation on surface C3b clusters; such context-dependent activation can manifest as “pharmacodynamic breakthrough” despite apparently adequate free drug levels, and has been reported in clinical settings as residual terminal-pathway activity and elevated sC5b-9 in some patients (Mannes et al., 2021; Schmidt & Smith, 2023). (2) Eculizumab binds C5 with high affinity at both neutral and mildly acidic pH, allowing FcRn to recycle eculizumab:C5 complexes and potentially alter antigen disposition; ravulizumab’s pH-dependent binding was engineered to release C5 in endosomes and improve pharmacokinetics by avoiding cargo recycling (Schmidt & Smith, 2023). These features are germane to dose selection, monitoring, and potential resistance or escape during strong complement activation.

Rationale for C5 inhibition in AMD rests on abundant mechanistic and histopathological evidence that C5-derived effectors and MAC participate in retinal/choroidal injury. C5a is detected in drusen and acts as a chemotactic and proinflammatory mediator capable of inducing VEGF expression from retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells, while C5b initiates MAC assembly; MAC deposition in Bruch’s membrane and the choriocapillaris increases with aging and is elevated in AMD (Kassa et al., 2019). Sub-lytic and lytic MAC on choroidal endothelium causes cell injury and can trigger surviving endothelial cells to upregulate VEGF and matrix metalloproteinases, linking terminal pathway activation to both atrophic injury and pro-angiogenic remodeling; complement regulatory proteins such as CD59 are natural inhibitors of MAC formation and their dysfunction or insufficiency can predispose to damage (Kassa et al., 2019; Park et al., 2021). Complement dysregulation in AMD further includes genetic risk (e.g., CFH variants) and evidence of alternative pathway activation in ocular tissues, suggesting both proximal and terminal effector involvement in disease biology (Piri & Kaplan, 2023).

Importantly, proximal inhibition at C3 has shown the capacity to restore lysosomal function and reduce sub-RPE drusen-like deposits in human iPSC-derived RPE models carrying the high-risk CFH Y402H variant by reducing C3 turnover and lowering downstream MAC (C5b-9) localization at lysosomes; in this system, a compstatin analogue (Cp40) suppressed C3b and MAC deposition and reversed lysosomal abnormalities, whereas direct C5 inhibition has not been shown in this model to rescue lysosomal function, and clinical C5 blockade has failed in GA (Cerniauskas et al., 2020). These data reinforce that while terminal pathway effectors are present and likely pathogenic in AMD, distal blockade at C5 does not necessarily address upstream complement turnover that drives intracellular lysosomal stress in RPE, and proximal intervention may be required to normalize cellular homeostasis (Cerniauskas et al., 2020).

Expected Effect  
The hypothesis proposes that high-affinity C5 blockade with eculizumab will prevent MAC assembly on RPE, thereby reducing sub-RPE deposit–driven lysosomal stress and indirectly supporting photoreceptor outer segment (POS) phagocytosis in dry AMD. Mechanistically, preventing C5 cleavage would abrogate both C5a signaling and C5b-dependent MAC formation; given the documented presence of C5a in drusen and increased MAC deposition in Bruch’s membrane/choriocapillaris with age and AMD, terminal pathway inhibition is expected to reduce MAC-mediated endothelial and RPE injury, dampen MAC-induced VEGF/MMP signaling, and potentially mitigate inflammation (Kassa et al., 2019). In principle, if MAC-driven lysosomal injury contributes to RPE dysfunction, C5 inhibition could reduce new MAC deposition at the RPE surface and limit subsequent endocytosis of MAC into lysosomes, thereby lowering lysosomal burden (Park et al., 2021).

However, two key bodies of evidence temper this expectation. First, clinical testing of systemic eculizumab in GA and intermediate AMD failed to show benefit on primary anatomical endpoints (GA growth and OCT drusen volume) through 26–52 weeks, despite established systemic pharmacodynamic blockade; these negative outcomes argue that either (a) terminal pathway activity at C5 is not the principal driver of lesion growth/drusen regression during the studied disease stages/time horizon, or (b) pharmacokinetic compartmentalization limited ocular target engagement after intravenous dosing (Yehoshua et al., 2014; Park et al., 2021; Filho et al., 2014). Reviews explicitly highlight the compartment barrier problem—systemic dosing may not achieve therapeutic concentrations in the retina or sub-RPE space due to the blood–retina/Bruch’s membrane barriers and large molecular size—and note that eculizumab exposure shorter than six months is unlikely to show benefit in slowly progressive GA, compounding interpretation (Tzoumas et al., 2023; Wu & Sun, 2019). Second, cellular models directly linking complement dysregulation to lysosomal failure in high-risk AMD RPE show that upstream C3 inhibition, not terminal C5 blockade, restores lysosomal function and reduces deposit formation; this suggests that intralysosomal stress is driven by continuous C3 turnover and opsonization rather than MAC alone, and that distal C5 blockade may be insufficient to normalize these processes (Cerniauskas et al., 2020). Consequently, POS phagocytosis metrics may improve only marginally or not at all if the dominant insult is proximal complement turnover rather than MAC-mediated damage. The clinical failure to slow GA growth or reduce drusen volume with systemic eculizumab supports the inference that target engagement at C5, via the tested route and schedule, did not translate into macroscopic anatomical benefit in human AMD eyes within one year (Yehoshua et al., 2014; Filho et al., 2014).

If eculizumab were to be tested in vitro on human RPE, appropriate readouts would include: surface and intracellular MAC (C5b-9) quantification, lysosomal number/size and membrane integrity (e.g., LAMP2, Galectin-8), Cathepsin D activity, autophagic flux, and POS degradation kinetics; concomitant measurement of upstream C3b/iC3b/C3d deposition would be essential to interpret whether C5 inhibition is affecting the dominant pathogenic pathway in the assay (Cerniauskas et al., 2020). In vivo, ocular pharmacokinetic considerations dominate: intravitreal administration delivers drug to the retina and sub-RPE space but is not reported for eculizumab in the provided context and would carry different safety and dosing constraints; systemic dosing faces blood–retina barrier limitations that likely constrained ocular target engagement in COMPLETE (Tzoumas et al., 2023; Wu & Sun, 2019). Notably, nearly all complement inhibitors that have shown anatomical effects in GA have been delivered intravitreally, underscoring the importance of route of administration for ocular diseases (Tzoumas et al., 2023).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
- Strong mechanistic rationale: C5a and MAC are present in AMD-relevant tissues; MAC deposition increases with age and AMD and can drive endothelial injury, VEGF/MMP upregulation, and potentially RPE dysfunction; blocking C5 should diminish these terminal effector signals (Kassa et al., 2019; Piri & Kaplan, 2023).  
- Potent, well-characterized biologic: Eculizumab binds human C5 with picomolar affinity, has a well-defined structural epitope in the MG7 domain, and reliably ablates terminal pathway function systemically with a large clinical safety database outside ophthalmology (Rother et al., 2007; Schatz-Jakobsen et al., 2016).  
- Clinical feasibility and tolerability: In AMD trials, intravenous eculizumab was well tolerated without drug-related adverse events and with no clear signal for increased neovascular conversion or ocular infection within one year, supporting general safety of systemic C5 blockade in this population under vaccination protocols (Yehoshua et al., 2014; Park et al., 2021; Tzoumas et al., 2023).

Weaknesses and Risks  
- Negative clinical efficacy in AMD: The randomized phase 2 COMPLETE trial showed no reduction in GA growth and no meaningful reduction in drusen volume through 26–52 weeks; certainty is limited by small sample size but confidence intervals and replication across endpoints argue against a large treatment effect (Yehoshua et al., 2014; Filho et al., 2014).  
- Compartmentalization barriers for systemic dosing: Blood–retina and Bruch’s membrane barriers likely limit ocular exposure to intravenously administered eculizumab; multiple reviews cite inadequate ocular penetration as a plausible reason for failure, and systemic eculizumab is no longer being pursued in AMD (Tzoumas et al., 2023; Nadeem et al., 2023; Wu & Sun, 2019).  
- Target selection may be too distal for RPE lysosomal rescue: Human iPSC-RPE data indicate that complement-driven lysosomal pathology and drusen-like deposits are reversed by C3 inhibition, with concomitant reduction of downstream MAC, while C5 blockade alone has not been shown to restore lysosomal metrics and did not slow GA clinically; this suggests that proximal complement turnover is the dominant driver of RPE lysosomal stress (Cerniauskas et al., 2020).  
- Mechanistic escape and incomplete terminal control: Under strong activation, C5 can be activated on dense C3b clusters with features of convertase bypass or conformational priming; stoichiometric C5 inhibitors can form long-lived C5:antibody complexes on surfaces, potentially allowing residual terminal activity, and clinical “pharmacodynamic breakthrough” has been documented (Mannes et al., 2021; Schmidt & Smith, 2023).  
- Genetic and biochemical determinants of response: C5 polymorphisms (e.g., R885H/C) can abolish binding and confer resistance; while prevalence in AMD populations is unknown here, such variants are important risk factors for nonresponse (Schatz-Jakobsen et al., 2016; Risitano et al., 2019).  
- Safety/logistics: Terminal complement blockade mandates meningococcal vaccination and vigilance for invasive infections; while acceptable systemically in selected diseases, risk–benefit in a chronic, slowly progressive ocular disorder is less favorable, and the cost and infusion burden are high (Smith et al., 2012; Parker, 2009).

Contextualization with alternative complement strategies  
- C5 vs C3: Clinical success in GA has emerged at the level of C3 (pegcetacoplan), consistent with cellular data showing rescue of lysosomal function by upstream blockade; by contrast, systemic C5 inhibition with eculizumab and other C5-directed agents failed to meet GA endpoints in early trials, and LFG316 also did not demonstrate efficacy in GA (West et al., 2024; Kassa et al., 2019; Cerniauskas et al., 2020).  
- Route of administration: Most agents demonstrating anatomical effects in GA are delivered intravitreally; eculizumab’s large size and systemic administration likely limited ocular penetration in COMPLETE, and no intravitreal eculizumab data are provided here to support local delivery (Tzoumas et al., 2023).  
- Risk of adverse ocular remodeling: Complement modulation has complex, context-dependent effects; in some models, complement inhibition or modulation can alter macrophage polarization and influence CNV formation, underscoring that indiscriminate or poorly targeted complement blockade could have unintended consequences; while the eculizumab AMD trials did not show a neovascular signal, vigilance is warranted when selecting targets and doses (Tzoumas et al., 2023; Park et al., 2021).

Decision Implications  
- As a repurposed systemic therapy for dry AMD, eculizumab has a low probability of success based on randomized clinical evidence, pharmacokinetic compartment limitations, and mechanistic considerations indicating that distal C5 blockade may not correct the proximal complement turnover driving RPE lysosomal pathology (Yehoshua et al., 2014; Filho et al., 2014; Cerniauskas et al., 2020).  
- If C5 remains a target of interest, local ocular delivery and alternative molecular formats designed for intravitreal pharmacokinetics (e.g., smaller C5-binding entities, RNAi to intraocular C5 production, or intravitreal C5 inhibitors such as avacincaptad pegol) are more plausible routes; indeed, avacincaptad has advanced in GA, while systemic eculizumab has been discontinued in AMD (Kassa et al., 2019; Nadeem et al., 2023; West et al., 2024).  
- For assays aimed at lysosomal stress and POS phagocytosis, prioritizing proximal complement inhibitors (e.g., C3-targeting) is more aligned with human RPE data and recent clinical experience, whereas C5 inhibition alone is unlikely to fully normalize lysosomal function or confer robust structural benefit (Cerniauskas et al., 2020; West et al., 2024).

Conclusion  
Eculizumab provides a clear mechanistic means to extinguish terminal complement effectors implicated in AMD biology, but the totality of evidence—negative randomized clinical data in GA and drusen, route-of-administration barriers to ocular exposure, mechanistic escape during high activation, and cellular evidence favoring proximal over distal inhibition for lysosomal rescue—argues against further development of systemic eculizumab for dry AMD (Yehoshua et al., 2014; Filho et al., 2014; Tzoumas et al., 2023; Cerniauskas et al., 2020). The program should be deprioritized in favor of intraocularly suited agents and proximal complement targets, unless a compelling, testable strategy emerges to achieve effective intraocular C5 blockade with eculizumab (which is not supported by the present context) (Nadeem et al., 2023; Tzoumas et al., 2023).

Mechanistic Appendix: Biochemical and Pathobiologic Evidence Relevant to the Hypothesis  
- Complement activation and terminal effectors in AMD:  
  - C5a is present in drusen and induces VEGF from RPE; MAC deposition increases with age and AMD; MAC induces endothelial lysis and sub-lytic survival signaling that upregulates VEGF and MMPs (Kassa et al., 2019).  
  - Alternative pathway dysregulation is central in AMD; genetic and experimental data implicate factor H and alternative pathway components in AMD and CNV models; inhibition of C3a, C5a, CFB, MAC or supplementation with CD59/CFH blocks CNV in animals (Piri & Kaplan, 2023).  
- Eculizumab structure–function:  
  - Epitope on MG7 with steric convertase blockade; high-affinity binding and resistance polymorphisms at Arg885; CDR function validated by mutational scans (Schatz-Jakobsen et al., 2016).  
  - PK/PD: long half-life, vascular distribution, CH50 suppression threshold, FcRn recycling of C5:eculizumab complexes; ravulizumab engineering to improve recycling and dosing (Rother et al., 2007; Schmidt & Smith, 2023).  
  - Breakthrough mechanisms: convertase bypass and surface-induced conformational activation on dense C3b clusters; residual TP activity despite inhibitors under strong activation (Schmidt & Smith, 2023; Mannes et al., 2021).  
- Clinical AMD trials:  
  - COMPLETE GA cohort: no slowing of GA growth at 26 or 52 weeks; well tolerated (Yehoshua et al., 2014; Park et al., 2021).  
  - COMPLETE drusen cohort: no ≥ 50% reduction in drusen volume in treated eyes at 26 weeks; no significant change in drusen growth vs placebo (Filho et al., 2014).  
  - Critical appraisals: small sample, imprecision, risk of bias; systemic delivery may not achieve retinal levels; eculizumab no longer in AMD development (Tzoumas et al., 2023; Nadeem et al., 2023).  
- RPE lysosomal biology:  
  - In high-risk Y402H iPSC-RPE, excessive C3 turnover increases MAC internalization and overloads lysosomes; C3 inhibition with Cp40 reduces both C3b and MAC, reverses lysosomal defects, and reduces drusen-like deposits; direct C5 inhibition is not shown to restore lysosomal function in this model (Cerniauskas et al., 2020).

Operational Recommendation  
- Do not advance systemic eculizumab for dry AMD. If C5 remains a strategic target, consider intravitreal agents designed for ocular PK and evaluate them head-to-head against proximal inhibitors in RPE lysosomal-function assays that quantify both MAC and C3 fragment deposition, with translational go/no-go predicated on alignment with known clinical signals in GA (Kassa et al., 2019; West et al., 2024; Cerniauskas et al., 2020).
