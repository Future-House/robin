Proposal for Homotaurine (GABA analogue; Tocris Cat. No. 0388)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Homotaurine is a small-molecule GABA analogue that was originally investigated for its potential neuroprotective properties in Alzheimer’s disease due to its ability to interact with inhibitory neurotransmitter receptors. Although originally isolated from marine algae as a natural sulfonated amino acid derivative, the synthetic production of homotaurine has allowed for its repurposing and pharmacological refinement for various clinical indications. As a member of the GABAergic modulator class, it is designed to mimic the activity of the endogenous neurotransmitter γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), thereby affecting neuronal excitability and cellular homeostasis. In particular, homotaurine is postulated to activate GABAA receptors, which are ligand-gated chloride channels that normally contribute to membrane hyperpolarization and dampening of excitatory input. This class of compounds has been widely explored for neuroprotective purposes and has been utilized in clinical trials for Alzheimer’s disease, where an acceptable safety profile was established. The fact that homotaurine has progressed to advanced clinical stages in Alzheimer’s research supports its tolerability and widespread bioactivity, making it an attractive candidate for repurposing in ocular diseases such as dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) (Carullo et al., 2020).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, homotaurine has been primarily evaluated in the context of Alzheimer’s disease, where its anti-aggregation properties and potential to interfere with amyloid-β deposition were of paramount interest. Its prior clinical examination, which concluded with a favorable safety record, provides a solid foundation for its repurposing in other neurodegenerative conditions. Although there is no direct record of homotaurine being used to treat dry AMD, other compounds that modulate GABAergic signaling have been investigated in retinal degenerative models. For example, several studies have documented the expression of GABA receptor subunits, including those of the GABAA family, in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells, suggesting that pharmacological targeting of these receptors may have therapeutic implications in diseases associated with retinal dysfunction (Arellano et al., 2025). Furthermore, inhibitory neurotransmitters such as GABA have been implicated in maintaining proper cellular excitability and synaptic balance in the retina; alterations in their regulation are associated with excitotoxicity and neurodegeneration in retinal conditions including macular degeneration (Pavan & Dalpiaz, 2018; Schur et al., 2018). In a similar vein, preclinical studies have used GABA modulators to rescue or protect RPE and photoreceptor function in models of retinal degeneration and light-induced injury (Schur et al., 2018). Thus, while direct clinical evidence for homotaurine in dry AMD is lacking, its antecedents in the GABAergic drug class and the known vulnerability of RPE cells to excitotoxic calcium overload position it as a promising candidate for therapeutic repurposing.

Mechanism of Action:  
The hypothesized mechanism of action of homotaurine in dry AMD rests on its ability to activate GABAA receptors expressed on RPE cells. Under physiological conditions, GABAA receptors are heteropentameric chloride channels that, upon binding GABA, permit chloride influx and hyperpolarize the cell membrane. This hyperpolarization ordinarily serves to counteract excessive excitation and limit voltage-gated calcium channel opening. In the context of RPE function, maintaining a tight regulation of intracellular calcium is paramount because calcium levels directly influence cytoskeletal reorganization, phagosome trafficking, and lysosomal fusion events necessary for the degradation of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) (Nagymihaly et al., 2021). Several studies have documented that RPE cells not only express GABAA receptor subunits, such as GABAAα1, but that receptor activation in these cells can modify intracellular calcium concentrations, thereby modulating a host of calcium-dependent cellular processes—including phagocytosis (Arellano et al., 2025).

In dry AMD, the RPE’s phagocytic capacity is compromised, leading to the accumulation of lipofuscin and other toxic by-products, which in turn further disrupt cellular function and promote oxidative stress (Si et al., 2023; Inana et al., 2018). Homotaurine’s binding to GABAA receptors is expected to stabilize the membrane potential and reduce the abnormal Ca²⁺ influx that occurs during excitotoxic stress. This reduction in calcium entry is anticipated to alleviate downstream effects such as dysregulated cytoskeletal dynamics and impaired lysosomal trafficking. Cytoskeletal reorganization is essential for phagosome motility; proper F-actin dynamics direct the trafficking of phagosomes toward lysosomal compartments where degradation occurs (Inana et al., 2018; Nagymihaly et al., 2021). Moreover, studies have demonstrated that inflammatory mediators present in AMD, including TNF-α and interleukin-1β, modulate GABAA receptor expression and function, suggesting that modulation of these receptors could be a viable strategy to counteract inflammation-driven excitotoxicity (Arellano et al., 2025). In addition, the excitotoxic environment generated by imbalanced glutamate/GABA signaling in the retina can be mitigated by restoring the inhibitory tone through GABAA receptor activation. The specific EC₅₀ of approximately 10 µM for homotaurine indicates that it has the potential to achieve receptor activation within a therapeutic window that is plausible in an ocular formulation (Carullo et al., 2020; Arellano et al., 2025). Taken together, the combined modulation of calcium influx, along with the stabilization of cytoskeletal and lysosomal pathways, constitutes a comprehensive molecular strategy that homotaurine may employ to rescue impaired POS degradation in dry AMD.

Expected Effect:  
The proposed assay in ARPE-19 cells—a well-established in vitro model for RPE function—aims to evaluate whether homotaurine, via activation of GABAA receptors, can restore impaired photoreceptor outer segment (POS) phagocytosis under conditions of glutamate-induced stress. RPE cell dysfunction in dry AMD is characterized by diminished phagocytosis and lysosomal degradation of POS, leading to the pathological accumulation of lipofuscin, which further impedes cellular function (Si et al., 2023; Inana et al., 2018). By engaging GABAA receptors, homotaurine is expected to “rescue” this vital phagocytic process by normalizing intracellular Ca²⁺ dynamics—thus preventing excitotoxicity—and by ensuring the proper reorganization of actin filaments or other cytoskeletal elements that are essential for efficient phagosome motility (Nagymihaly et al., 2021).

Experimental evidence in related studies supports that compounds with similar mechanisms (i.e., GABA modulators and taurine-related molecules) are capable of influencing intracellular calcium signaling and can enhance the uptake and processing of POS in RPE cells (Arellano et al., 2025; López-Colomé et al., 1994). Under normo-physiological conditions, RPE cells efficiently internalize shed POS through receptor-mediated pathways involving CD36, MerTK, and αVβ5 integrin, initiating a cascade that is dependent on well-regulated intracellular Ca²⁺ fluctuations (Nagymihaly et al., 2021). In a stress state, glutamate excess drives excitotoxic Ca²⁺ entry that may dysregulate these processes. By antagonizing this excitotoxic cascade, homotaurine is anticipated to reduce the aberrant Ca²⁺ load, thereby restoring the dynamic equilibrium that supports both cytoskeletal integrity and lysosomal trafficking. This mechanism is supported by studies showing that dysregulation of Ca²⁺ not only impairs phagosome motility but also has deleterious effects on downstream lysosomal fusion and degradation, ultimately impacting cellular clearance mechanisms (Si et al., 2023; Nowak et al., 2025). Thus, the expected effect is a measurable enhancement in POS uptake as well as improved degradation kinetics in RPE cells subjected to glutamate-induced stress, which would correlate with potential therapeutic efficacy in dry AMD. Furthermore, since the underlying pathology of dry AMD involves chronic oxidative stress and accumulation of metabolic by-products, a pharmacological intervention that can restore intracellular homeostasis has far-reaching implications for halting or even reversing disease progression (Arellano et al., 2025).

Overall Evaluation:  
In evaluating homotaurine for dry age-related macular degeneration, several critical observations emerge. One of the key strengths of homotaurine is its well-documented safety profile from Alzheimer’s clinical trials, which lowers the translational hurdles when repurposing the compound for ocular administration. The fact that homotaurine is a GABA analogue that activates GABAA receptors—a receptor class that is expressed on RPE cells and critically involved in regulating intracellular Ca²⁺—provides a strong mechanistic rationale for its application in dry AMD (Arellano et al., 2025). By potentially mitigating excitotoxic Ca²⁺ influx, homotaurine could restore normal cytoskeletal dynamics and lysosomal trafficking, both of which are indispensable for the effective degradation of photoreceptor outer segments (Nagymihaly et al., 2021).

Among the strengths, the following points are notable:  
1. The candidate’s mechanism directly addresses a central pathology of dry AMD—namely, impaired RPE phagocytosis and lysosomal dysfunction—which leads to toxic lipofuscin accumulation (Si et al., 2023; Inana et al., 2018).  
2. Activation of GABAA receptors by homotaurine could normalize Ca²⁺ handling within RPE cells, thereby alleviating the excitotoxic stress that is linked to altered cytoskeletal assembly and defective phagosome trafficking (Arellano et al., 2025; Inana et al., 2018).  
3. The compound’s history in Alzheimer’s trials suggests a favorable safety margin and known pharmacokinetic properties that may be adapted for ocular use, thus enhancing its potential for rapid clinical translation (Carullo et al., 2020).  
4. Preclinical evidence from other studies involving GABA modulators and related compounds shows that interfering with aberrant neurotransmitter signaling in the retina can elicit neuroprotective and phagocytosis-enhancing effects (Schur et al., 2018).

Conversely, some weaknesses and uncertainties remain that necessitate further investigation:  
1. Although the proposed EC₅₀ of approximately 10 µM for homotaurine is within a plausible therapeutic window for GABAA receptor activation, the actual pharmacodynamic response in RPE cells—especially in the diseased microenvironment of dry AMD—remains to be rigorously quantified (Arellano et al., 2025).  
2. The retinal microenvironment in dry AMD is complex, characterized not only by excitotoxic stress but also by chronic oxidative stress, inflammation, and altered expression of multiple receptor types. In such a context, GABAA receptor activation could be affected by altered ionic gradients, potentially leading to reversed or atypical receptor responses as has been documented in other retinal degenerations (Arellano et al., 2025; Telegina et al., 2022).  
3. While indirect evidence supports the role of GABAergic modulation in restoring phagocytic function, there is a paucity of direct data demonstrating that homotaurine can specifically enhance POS uptake in ARPE-19 cells under injurious conditions. This gap necessitates additional in vitro studies to detail its effects on cytoskeletal reorganization, vesicular trafficking, and lysosomal fusion dynamics (Si et al., 2023; Nagymihaly et al., 2021).  
4. The exact downstream signaling pathways that link GABAA receptor activation to the observed improvements in phagocytic function must be delineated further. For example, the interplay between calcium channel deactivation, actin cytoskeleton stabilization, and the regulation of key phagocytic receptors (e.g., MerTK, αvβ5 integrin) remains incompletely characterized in the context of GABAergic modulation (Nagymihaly et al., 2021).  
5. Finally, the translational leap from in vitro findings in ARPE-19 cells to a robust therapeutic effect in human dry AMD patients is non-trivial. Aging RPE cells in vivo exhibit additional layers of complexity, including altered gene expression profiles, metabolic imbalances, and possible shifts in receptor isoforms, all of which might influence the responsiveness to homotaurine (Nowak et al., 2025; Si et al., 2023).

Overall, homotaurine represents a scientifically sound and mechanistically plausible candidate for repurposing in the treatment of dry age-related macular degeneration. Its potential to activate GABAA receptors on RPE cells, thereby reducing excitotoxic calcium influx and normalizing the intracellular processes that govern cytoskeletal dynamics and lysosomal trafficking, aligns well with the pathophysiological underpinnings of AMD (Arellano et al., 2025). Given the favorable safety data from Alzheimer’s trials and the breadth of preclinical evidence supporting the neuroprotective effects of GABAergic modulation in retinal contexts, homotaurine merits further exploration. Prior to clinical application, however, detailed mechanistic studies using ARPE-19 cells and appropriate animal models of dry AMD must be conducted. These studies should focus on quantifying calcium flux, cytoskeletal dynamics, phagosome motility, and lysosomal fusion efficiency upon homotaurine treatment under pathologically relevant conditions. Additionally, it will be important to investigate potential off-target effects and to determine whether combinatorial therapies that address oxidative stress and inflammation might synergize with homotaurine’s mechanism. In summary, while challenges remain, the repurposing of homotaurine for dry AMD offers a promising therapeutic avenue worthy of investment and further preclinical validation (Arellano et al., 2025; Si et al., 2023; Carullo et al., 2020).
