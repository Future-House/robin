Proposal for Fenofibrate (PPARα agonist; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. F6020)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Fenofibrate is a synthetic fibrate originally developed and approved as a lipid‐lowering agent that acts primarily as a peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα) agonist. It was discovered through efforts to develop compounds that could enhance fatty acid oxidation and thereby reduce triglyceride levels and improve lipid profiles in patients with dyslipidemia. As a member of the fibrate class of drugs, fenofibrate and its active metabolite, fenofibric acid, have been classically used in cardiometabolic conditions such as hyperlipidemia, where they improve lipid profiles by increasing high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels and lowering triglycerides. The compound available as Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. F6020 is representative of these agents and has been extensively studied in preclinical models of metabolic stress. Its chemical design enables it to bind to PPARα, a nuclear receptor that modulates gene expression of enzymes central to fatty acid β-oxidation and lipid homeostasis. In the context of retinal disorders, fenofibrate has been pursued as a repurposing candidate owing to its robust safety profile and its potential to address the dysregulation of lipid metabolism observed in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells. Several studies have suggested that by activating PPARα, fenofibrate may help reduce lipid overload and mitigate the accumulation of toxic by-products such as A2E (a major fluorophore in lipofuscin) that contribute to RPE dysfunction in age-related macular degeneration (Wang et al., 2023; Ren et al., 2022).  

Therapeutic History:  
Fenofibrate has a long clinical history as an antihyperlipidemic agent with a well-established safety profile in patients with cardiovascular risk factors. In addition to its widespread use in metabolic disorders, a significant body of preclinical research has documented the potential benefits of fenofibrate in retinal diseases. Several studies have demonstrated that fenofibrate can ameliorate retinal abnormalities induced by high-fat diets and excessive lipid exposure. For instance, experimental models of fat-induced RPE injury have shown that fenofibrate reduces oxidative stress and inflammatory mediators, thereby protecting RPE integrity through mechanisms such as upregulation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway (Wang et al., 2023). In diabetic retinopathy—a condition that, while distinct from dry AMD, shares underlying processes related to oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and inflammatory cascades—fenofibrate has been explored for its protective effects on the retina, contributing to improvements in vascular permeability and barrier function of the outer blood–retina barrier (Fu et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2023). Although fenofibrate’s potential use in dry AMD has not been as widely investigated as its role in diabetic retinopathy, the documented protective effects on RPE under conditions of lipid overload, as well as its ability to downregulate markers of oxidative stress (e.g., NOX4 and 3‐nitrotyrosine) and inflammatory cytokines (such as TNFα and ICAM1), support its candidacy for repurposing in dry AMD (Ren et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023). It is noteworthy that while clinical data in diabetic populations (e.g., FIELD study outcomes) have driven considerable interest in fenofibrate’s ocular effects, direct applications to dry AMD remain exploratory and are based largely on preclinical findings that show promise for reducing lipotoxic damage in the RPE.

Mechanism of Action:  
Fenofibrate exerts its pharmacological effects primarily through the activation of PPARα, a nuclear receptor that, upon ligand binding, dimerizes with the retinoid X receptor and binds to specific PPAR response elements in promoter regions of target genes. This leads to the transcriptional upregulation of a variety of genes involved in lipid uptake, fatty acid transport, and β-oxidation, ultimately promoting the catabolism of long-chain fatty acids. In RPE cells, robust expression of PPARα is critical for maintaining homeostasis in the face of high lipid loads and oxidative stress. Activation of PPARα by fenofibrate has been shown to enhance mitochondrial and peroxisomal fatty acid oxidation, thereby reducing intracellular lipid accumulation and possibly the generation of toxic lipid peroxidation products such as A2E, a primary component of the lipofuscin granules that accumulate with age in RPE cells (Ren et al., 2022).

Beyond its classical role in lipid metabolism, fenofibrate has also been reported to engage additional cellular signaling pathways that contribute to its cytoprotective effects. In several preclinical studies, fenofibrate and fenofibric acid have been observed to activate the PI3K/AKT pathway in RPE cells, which is well recognized for its roles in cell survival and stress response. The activation of PI3K/AKT signaling by fenofibrate leads to enhanced cell survival signals, mitigates oxidative damage, and downregulates inflammatory mediators in fatty acid–challenged RPE cells (Wang et al., 2023). Moreover, fenofibrate has been implicated in reducing the expression of pro-oxidant enzymes such as NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4) and in decreasing levels of markers of oxidative stress like 3-nitrotyrosine (3-NT) in palmitic acid-treated RPE models (Wang et al., 2023). These actions collectively contribute to mitigation of oxidative injury—a central component of dry AMD pathogenesis.

In addition, some studies have proposed that fenofibrate may exert ancillary effects through mechanisms independent of PPARα activation. For example, evidence suggests that fenofibrate might function as an iron chelator, thereby reducing intracellular free iron levels, which in turn could diminish the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and blunt the aberrant activation of the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway—a pathway implicated in various ocular pathologies including AMD (Mandala et al., 2020). This suppression of iron-induced oxidative stress and its associated downstream signaling cascades may further contribute to the preservation of RPE cell function under conditions of chronic oxidative insult.

Another mechanism of potential interest is fenofibrate’s impact on inflammatory signaling. Classical anti-inflammatory effects have been verified through its ability to inhibit nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) activity, thereby reducing the transcription of a host of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines that might contribute to retinal degeneration (Garcia-Ramírez et al., 2016; Fu et al., 2020). Although direct evidence for improved phagocytosis or lysosomal function in the context of dry AMD is not yet abundant in the literature provided, it has been hypothesized that by reducing lipid overload and oxidative stress, fenofibrate could indirectly improve lysosomal pH stability and optimize the function of cathepsin D—a key lysosomal protease involved in the degradation of photoreceptor outer segment (POS) fragments (as hypothesized in Tang et al. IOVS 2024). Given that proper autophagic flux and phagocytic processing are essential for RPE homeostasis and that their impairment is a hallmark of dry AMD, the restoration of these cellular processes via improved metabolic and intracellular signaling pathways is an attractive proposition (Ren et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023).

Expected Effect:  
The central hypothesis for the repurposing strategy of fenofibrate in dry AMD is that its activation of PPARα in RPE cells—achievable at concentrations with an EC₅₀ of approximately 2 µM—will promote a metabolic shift toward enhanced fatty acid oxidation. This shift is expected to lower the intracellular lipid load and decrease the accumulation of lipofuscin, a complex mixture of lipid-protein aggregates that includes A2E, which is thought to contribute directly to RPE cell dysfunction and subsequent photoreceptor degeneration. In vitro data from models using palmitic acid to induce lipotoxic stress in RPE cells suggest that fenofibrate can restore cellular homeostasis by activating survival pathways such as PI3K/AKT and by suppressing overexpressed stress markers and inflammatory cytokines (Wang et al., 2023).

According to the proposed mechanism, activation of PPARα should lead to the upregulation of genes involved in fatty acid β-oxidation, resulting in improved clearance of lipid-rich photoreceptor outer segments. The improvement in lipid processing not only reduces the overall lipid burden within RPE cells but is also expected to restore lysosomal function—specifically, normalizing lysosomal pH and enhancing cathepsin D activity. These changes should facilitate more efficient phagocytosis and degradation of photoreceptor outer segment debris, thereby mitigating the accumulation of cytotoxic lipofuscin deposits.

Furthermore, by lowering intracellular lipid accumulation and reducing oxidative stress through enhanced PI3K/AKT signaling and potential NF-κB inhibition, fenofibrate is expected to decrease the production of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines that contribute to chronic inflammation in the retina. Since inflammation is a driving force behind the progression of dry AMD, such a decrease would synergize with improved autophagy and phagocytosis to stabilize or even improve RPE function. In preclinical models, the application of fenofibrate in conditions simulating high dietary fat has been shown to restore aspects of RPE cell viability and function, lending indirect support to its potential efficacy in dry AMD (Wang et al., 2023). Although the precise effects on A2E reduction and lipofuscin clearance have yet to be fully elucidated, the cumulative data on fenofibrate’s capacity to improve lipid metabolism and reduce oxidative and inflammatory stress suggest that the compound could address multiple maladaptive processes that underlie dry AMD.

Overall Evaluation:  
When evaluating fenofibrate as a repurposed drug candidate for dry AMD, several strengths and potential weaknesses become apparent. On the strengths side, fenofibrate benefits from an extensive clinical safety record, as it is already in widespread use as a lipid-lowering agent in patients with dyslipidemia. This established safety profile, along with known pharmacokinetics, provides an important advantage when considering rapid repurposing for ocular indications. Moreover, its well-documented efficacy in modulating lipid metabolism through PPARα activation is a significant asset, given that dysregulated lipid handling is a central aspect of dry AMD pathogenesis. Preclinical studies have convincingly demonstrated that fenofibrate can attenuate RPE injury induced by fat overload by reducing oxidative stress markers, enhancing protective signaling pathways (e.g., PI3K/AKT), and suppressing inflammatory cytokines (Wang et al., 2023). Additionally, the possibility that fenofibrate might also act through mechanisms independent of PPARα—such as iron chelation and suppression of Wnt/β-catenin signaling—broadens its therapeutic potential in addressing the multifactorial nature of RPE degeneration (Mandala et al., 2020).

Another critical strength is the hypothesized ability of fenofibrate to improve lysosomal function and promote the phagocytosis of lipid-rich photoreceptor outer segments. Although the direct evidence for improvement in autophagy or direct clearance of lipofuscin and A2E is not yet fully established in the context of dry AMD studies, mechanistic insights from fenofibrate’s action in reducing cellular oxidative stress and lipid droplet accumulation provide a plausible basis for such effects (Ren et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023). The proposed cellular effects—activation of PPARα leading to upregulation of genes related to fatty acid oxidation, restoration of lysosomal pH, and normalization of cathepsin D activity—could collectively improve RPE function and delay the progression of dry AMD. The EC₅₀ value of roughly 2 µM as suggested in the hypothesis is within a physiologically relevant range based on in vitro findings in other RPE models, and may be achievable in ocular tissues with appropriate drug delivery strategies.

However, there are also notable weaknesses and uncertainties that must be addressed. First, while fenofibrate’s benefits have been clearly documented in models of diabetic retinopathy and fat-induced RPE injury, there remains a relative paucity of direct evidence for its efficacy in models that specifically recapitulate the pathophysiological features of dry AMD. Dry AMD is characterized by a complex interplay of factors including drusen formation, chronic inflammation, and impaired phagocytosis, and the literature provided does not offer conclusive data demonstrating that fenofibrate can reduce lipofuscin accumulation or enhance the clearance of photoreceptor outer segments in these contexts (Wang et al., 2023). In other words, while fenofibrate effectively modulates the metabolic state of RPE cells in fat-induced injury models, its impact on the accumulation of A2E and other lipofuscin components remains largely hypothetical at this stage.

Another potential weakness is related to the complexity of AMD pathogenesis. Dry AMD involves not only metabolic and inflammatory dysregulation at the level of the RPE but also changes in Bruch’s membrane, choroidal perfusion, and immune cell infiltration. As such, a therapeutic candidate targeting primarily RPE lipid metabolism and oxidative stress may need to be combined with other interventions or may not be sufficient as a monotherapy to address all pathogenic aspects of the disease. Further, the off-target effects of fenofibrate—such as its potential to act through PPARα-independent pathways—could complicate its clinical profile in the eye, where fine-tuning of multiple overlapping cellular pathways is critical (Mandala et al., 2020; Garcia-Ramírez et al., 2016).

Moreover, although fenofibrate has excellent systemic safety and pharmacokinetics, its ocular penetration, bioavailability in the retina, and long-term effects specifically on the RPE have not been comprehensively evaluated in the context of dry AMD. Optimization of drug delivery to the posterior segment of the eye is a well-known challenge, and while fenofibrate’s lipophilic nature could be advantageous for retinal penetration, formulation and dosing strategies would require careful investigation.

In summary, fenofibrate represents a compelling candidate for repurposing in dry AMD based on its ability to activate PPARα and improve lipid metabolism, as well as its demonstrated capacity to reduce oxidative stress and inflammation in RPE cells under conditions of lipid overload. Its long-standing clinical use and excellent safety record provide a strong foundation for further preclinical and eventually clinical studies in the context of ocular degenerative diseases. Nonetheless, a critical gap in the current literature is the direct demonstration that fenofibrate can lower A2E levels and enhance the phagocytosis of lipid-rich photoreceptor outer segments—a key mechanism underlying its hypothesized benefit in dry AMD. Addressing this gap through targeted in vitro and in vivo studies using dry AMD models will be essential. Should these studies confirm that fenofibrate improves lysosomal function, normalizes RPE phagocytosis, and reduces lipofuscin accumulation, it would represent a major advance in the treatment options for dry AMD.

Overall, fenofibrate’s potential lies in its multifaceted mechanisms of action. By combining improved fatty acid oxidation, antioxidant defenses, anti-inflammatory effects, and possibly even modulation of cellular iron levels and aberrant signaling pathways, fenofibrate could serve as a powerful therapeutic to modify the progressive degeneration of the RPE seen in dry AMD. However, additional mechanistic studies and preclinical validations are necessary before this candidate can be confidently advanced in clinical trials for dry AMD. Future research should aim to delineate the concentrations required for ocular efficacy, assess drug delivery methods that maximize retinal bioavailability, and determine whether the cellular effects observed in diabetic retinopathy and fat-induced injury models translate to the unique pathophysiological environment of dry AMD (Fu et al., 2020; Garcia-Ramírez et al., 2016; Mandala et al., 2020; Ren et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023).

In conclusion, while fenofibrate is a promising candidate for repurposing in dry AMD therapy, its ultimate success will depend on the ability to generate robust, direct evidence that supports its hypothesized effects on RPE lipid metabolism, lysosomal function, and phagocytic clearance of toxic lipofuscin components. The existing data on fenofibrate’s systemic and retinal protective effects provide an encouraging starting point; however, the transition from preclinical promise to clinical efficacy in the context of dry AMD remains contingent on focused investigations into these key areas. The strengths of fenofibrate—its established safety profile, multiple beneficial metabolic actions, and evidence from related retinal models—are balanced by the need for more specific, AMD-targeted research to fully validate its repurposing potential (Fu et al., 2020; Garcia-Ramírez et al., 2016; Mandala et al., 2020; Ren et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023).
