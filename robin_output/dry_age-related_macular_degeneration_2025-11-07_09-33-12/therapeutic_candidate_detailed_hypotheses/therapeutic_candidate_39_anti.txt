Proposal for Anti-Tyro3 agonist antibody (Presumed research grade)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
The candidate under evaluation is an anti‐Tyro3 agonist monoclonal antibody (mAb), a protein‐based therapeutic engineered to specifically bind and activate the Tyro3 receptor. Tyro3 is one of the three members of the TAM receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) family—comprising Tyro3, Axl, and MerTK—that modulate key cellular functions in different tissues. In the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), both MerTK and Tyro3 are expressed; however, MerTK is traditionally recognized as the primary driver of phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments (POS), while Tyro3 has emerged as a compensatory receptor with a crucial anti‐inflammatory signal in MerTK‐deficient settings (Lieffrig et al., 2023; Palczewska et al., 2016). This candidate antibody is presumed to be of research grade, synthesized using recombinant antibody technology. It is generated by isolating B cells that produce an antibody directed against the extracellular domain of Tyro3; through hybridoma technology or phage display methods, a specific mAb is cloned and further optimized for high binding affinity and agonistic activity with an effective concentration (EC₅₀) on the order of approximately 20 nM. Monoclonal antibodies, by virtue of their proteinaceous nature and high specificity, have long served as therapeutics in a wide range of indications including oncology, autoimmunity, and inflammatory diseases. In this particular case, the therapeutic candidate belongs to the class of receptor agonists rather than the more common inhibitory antibodies. Receptor agonist antibodies are designed to cross‐link target receptors, thereby inducing conformational changes that stimulate receptor dimerization and activation of downstream signaling cascades. Although the majority of previous antibody therapeutics have been developed as receptor antagonists to block signaling (as is often the case with oncogenic targets), the approach of receptor activation via agonist antibodies is emerging as a promising strategy in diseases where deficiency in receptor function contributes to pathology (Palczewska et al., 2016).  

Therapeutic History:  
The therapeutic use of monoclonal antibodies targeting the TAM RTK family has primarily been explored in oncology. In cancer, for example, anti‐TYRO3 antibodies have been predominantly investigated as inhibitors intended to block tumorigenic signaling pathways (Hsu et al., 2019). However, the narrative differs considerably when one considers ocular diseases such as dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD). Dry AMD is characterized by RPE dysfunction, accumulation of photoreceptor debris, and a resultant chronic inflammatory milieu that drives retinal degeneration. Although MerTK is well documented as being critically involved in the phagocytosis of POS—with deficiencies in its function directly linked to retinal degeneration in models such as the Royal College of Surgeons (RCS) rat—recent genetic and biochemical studies have indicated that Tyro3, despite historically receiving less attention than MerTK, can functionally compensate in aspects of anti‐inflammatory signaling when MerTK is absent (Lieffrig et al., 2023; Akalu et al., 2017). In MerTK‐deficient RPE cells, the compensatory actions of Tyro3 have been demonstrated to preserve anti‐inflammatory gene expression profiles and maintain retinal viability, suggesting the therapeutic potential of harnessing Tyro3 agonism to ameliorate inflammation‐mediated retinal degeneration in dry AMD (Lieffrig et al., 2023; Palczewska et al., 2016). Despite this promising genetic evidence, there is little precedent in the literature for the clinical or preclinical use of an anti‐Tyro3 agonist antibody explicitly for dry AMD or similar retinal diseases. The majority of TAM receptor–targeted therapeutic approaches developed to date have focused on receptor inhibition for cancer therapy (Hsu et al., 2019). As such, the repurposing of an agonist antibody against Tyro3 to enhance RPE phagocytosis represents a novel direction that capitalizes on emerging insights into compensatory TAM signaling in the retina (Lieffrig et al., 2023). While there are no reports of prior successful application of anti‐Tyro3 agonist antibodies in either clinical or veterinary practice specifically for dry AMD, the genetic suppression of photoreceptor degeneration in MerTK‐deficient models by Tyro3 signals a strong preclinical rationale worthy of further investigation (Akalu et al., 2017; Palczewska et al., 2016).  

Mechanism of Action:  
The proposed mechanism of action for the anti‐Tyro3 agonist antibody revolves around its ability to engage and activate Tyro3 in RPE cells where MerTK function is compromised. Under normal conditions, MerTK activation in RPE cells triggers a cascade of intracellular events that lead to phagocytosis of shed photoreceptor outer segments. This process is mediated by the recruitment of signaling molecules that activate regulators of the actin cytoskeleton, chiefly through pathways involving the small GTPase Rac1. Specifically, upon ligand binding, MerTK undergoes dimerization and trans‐autophosphorylation, generating docking sites for signal transduction proteins such as Vav and the p130Cas/CrkII/Dock180 complex; these effectors then catalyze Rac1 activation, which in turn promotes the actin polymerization necessary for the formation of phagocytic cups around POS (Kwon & Freeman, 2020). In the context of MerTK deficiency, however, RPE cells exhibit a marked disruption in phagocytosis, even though compensatory pathways involving Tyro3 are partially active. Experimental observations indicate that in mertk−/− RPE cells that retain Tyro3 expression, while anti‐inflammatory gene expression is preserved, the phagocytic uptake of POS remains severely impaired, suggesting that Tyro3’s natural activation level is suboptimal for driving effective phagocytosis under these conditions (Lieffrig et al., 2023).  

The agonist mAb candidate is designed to overcome this limitation by binding specifically to the extracellular domain of Tyro3 and triggering receptor activation through induced dimerization. This antibody binding is expected to mimic the natural ligand-induced conformational changes and stimulate the intracellular kinase activity of Tyro3. Upon receptor activation, Tyro3 should phosphorylate its own intracellular tyrosine residues, creating docking sites for various adaptor proteins that propagate downstream signaling cascades. Although the precise molecular details of Tyro3 signaling in RPE cells are not as well characterized as those for MerTK, it is anticipated that downstream effectors such as the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) and Rac1 will become activated. PI3K activation leads to the generation of phosphatidylinositol (3,4,5)-trisphosphate (PIP₃), which in turn recruits Rac guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs) to the plasma membrane, thereby catalyzing the conversion of Rac1 to its active GTP-bound form. Active Rac1 is critical for orchestrating rearrangements of the actin cytoskeleton, a key step in phagocytic cup formation and subsequent internalization of POS (Palczewska et al., 2016; Lieffrig et al., 2023).  

Furthermore, evidence suggests that Tyro3 signaling may be linked to the regulation of αvβ5 integrin, another receptor complex highly expressed in RPE cells that plays an important role during POS phagocytosis. In wild-type RPE cells, αvβ5 integrin engages with bridging molecules such as MFG-E8 to facilitate the physical attachment of POS to the RPE cell surface. Although MerTK is instrumental in transducing signals required for the downstream internalization process, it is the coordinated activity between MerTK and αvβ5 integrin that ensures a successful phagocytic event (Kwon & Freeman, 2020; Palczewska et al., 2016). The hypothesis underlying this therapeutic candidate is that, in the absence or dysfunction of MerTK, an agonist mAb stimulating Tyro3 can activate a compensatory signaling cascade that establishes convergence with αvβ5 integrin-mediated pathways. This cross-talk is believed to enhance Rac1 activation and promote cytoskeletal reorganization necessary for POS internalization even under compromised conditions (Lieffrig et al., 2023; Palczewska et al., 2016). In summary, the antibody-driven activation of Tyro3 is anticipated to increase intracellular PI3K and Rac1 signaling, consequently supporting not only anti-inflammatory responses but also promoting the assembly of phagocytic structures in MerTK-impaired RPE cells.  

Expected Effect:  
In the in vitro and in vivo assays proposed for dry AMD, the administration of the anti‐Tyro3 agonist mAb is expected to yield several beneficial effects on RPE cell function. First, by binding to and activating Tyro3, the antibody is anticipated to trigger a cascade of intracellular signals that primarily enhance the downstream activity of PI3K and Rac1, which are critical mediators in remodeling the actin cytoskeleton for phagocytosis (Palczewska et al., 2016; Lieffrig et al., 2023). Given that MerTK-deficient RPE cells have been shown to retain anti-inflammatory signaling via Tyro3 yet suffer from diminished phagocytic uptake of POS, the agonist is postulated to at least partially restore the phagocytic capacity. The expected increase in active Rac1 will promote pseudopod formation and stabilization of the phagocytic cup around bound photoreceptor debris, thereby facilitating the internalization process.  

Moreover, αvβ5 integrin complexes, which are essential for the initial binding of POS to the RPE surface, are expected to be functionally engaged in the presence of enhanced Tyro3 signaling. The hypothesized cross-regulation between Tyro3 and these integrin complexes should result in improved phagocytic efficiency. Recent experimental evidence suggests that wild-type RPE cells, which naturally co-express MerTK and Tyro3, maintain a robust anti-inflammatory gene expression profile and efficient POS clearance; conversely, in mertk−/− models, the intact expression of Tyro3 is sufficient to sustain retinal viability despite markedly impaired phagocytosis (Lieffrig et al., 2023). Thus, an anti‐Tyro3 agonist mAb could potentially tip the balance toward enhanced phagocytic performance in diseased states by elevating Tyro3 signaling from its basal compensatory level to one that more effectively drives phagocytosis.  

The proposed expected effect in the assay includes an increase in the phagocytic index of RPE cells, as measured by the uptake of fluorescently labeled POS, and a reduction in markers of retinal inflammation. In mechanistic experiments, activation of Tyro3 should correlate with increased phosphorylation levels of pivotal signaling intermediates such as PI3K and Akt, and a subsequent rise in GTP-bound Rac1 levels, which can be detected via pull-down assays. In addition, the impact on αvβ5 integrin function may be assessed by examining the recruitment of cytoskeletal adaptor proteins that have established roles in integrin-mediated phagocytosis. Furthermore, given that Tyro3’s activation may also support anti-inflammatory gene expression, treatment with the antibody might reduce levels of inflammatory cytokines in RPE cultures under oxidative or inflammatory stress conditions—conditions that mimic dry AMD pathogenesis (Lieffrig et al., 2023; Palczewska et al., 2016).  

It is important to note that although Tyro3 agonism is projected to enhance intracellular signaling events associated with phagocytosis, previous studies have indicated that in MerTK-deficient RPE cells the restoration of phagocytic function is only partially achievable through Tyro3 activation alone (Lieffrig et al., 2023). Therefore, while an increase in Rac1 and PI3K signaling levels is anticipated with the anti‐Tyro3 agonist, it might not fully recapitulate the robust phagocytic response observed in fully functional RPE cells. Nonetheless, the profound anti-inflammatory effects observed in Tyro3-expressing RPE cells could contribute to preserving retinal structure and function over time, thereby delaying the progression of dry AMD. In addition, gene expression studies have documented that Tyro3 is naturally expressed in the RPE, albeit at levels comparable to those in the rodent and human retina (Palczewska et al., 2016), thereby providing a cellular substrate for the agonist mAb to exert its effects.  

Overall Evaluation:  
In assessing the potential of an anti‐Tyro3 agonist antibody as a therapeutic candidate for dry age‐related macular degeneration, several points of strength and limitation emerge from the available literature.  

Strengths:  
One of the foremost strengths of this therapeutic candidate lies in its receptor specificity. Tyro3 is highly expressed in RPE cells and is demonstrated to participate in anti‐inflammatory signaling; importantly, genetic studies have revealed that Tyro3 acts as a suppressor of photoreceptor degeneration in MerTK-deficient models (Akalu et al., 2017; Palczewska et al., 2016). This genetic evidence suggests that enhancement of Tyro3 signaling could counteract some of the detrimental consequences associated with MerTK deficiency that are known to contribute to dry AMD pathology (Lieffrig et al., 2023). The approach leverages the inherent compensatory potential of Tyro3 and could offer an innovative strategy to bypass the limitations imposed by non-functional MerTK. Additionally, the design of an agonist mAb allows for targeted activation of the receptor, theoretically minimizing off-target effects that might occur with less specific small-molecule agonists. Given that monoclonal antibodies can be engineered to display high binding affinity (targeting an EC₅₀ in the vicinity of 20 nM) and long half-lives in situ, the intrinsic pharmacokinetic properties of an antibody therapeutic could be advantageous in the ocular environment, where sustained receptor activation may be necessary (Palczewska et al., 2016).  

Furthermore, the mechanism by which Tyro3 activation is proposed to enhance phagocytosis involves well-characterized intracellular cascades—including the PI3K and Rac1 signaling pathways—that are known to be essential for actin cytoskeleton reorganization and phagocytic cup formation (Palczewska et al., 2016; Lieffrig et al., 2023). The interplay between Tyro3 and αvβ5 integrin complexes represents an attractive target because αvβ5 integrin is already implicated in POS binding and is a critical component of the RPE’s phagocytic machinery (Kwon & Freeman, 2020). Thus, the strategy of boosting Tyro3 activity may not only revitalize phagocytic signaling in MerTK-impaired cells but could also reinforce the integrin-mediated aspects of the phagocytic process. These mechanistic attributes confer a rational basis for pursuing an agonist-based approach in dry AMD, a field where current treatment options remain largely palliative (Lieffrig et al., 2023).  

Weaknesses:  
On the other hand, there are several challenges and uncertainties that temper enthusiasm for the candidate. Notably, while Tyro3 activation has been demonstrated to preserve anti-inflammatory signaling in RPE cells, the literature consistently reports that phagocytosis remains significantly impaired in MerTK-deficient models even when Tyro3 is intact (Lieffrig et al., 2023). This suggests that although agonizing Tyro3 may mitigate inflammation—which is instrumental in curtailing further retinal damage—it does not necessarily normalize the essential phagocytic clearance of POS. Consequently, the anticipated improvement in phagocytosis through Tyro3 agonism might be only partial in nature, thereby limiting the therapeutic impact on a key aspect of dry AMD pathogenesis.  

Another major limitation stems from the fact that the detailed molecular mechanisms underlying Tyro3-specific signaling in the RPE remain less well characterized relative to MerTK. With much of the established research focusing on MerTK’s role in phagocytosis (Kwon & Freeman, 2020; Si et al., 2023), there exists a significant knowledge gap regarding the full spectrum of Tyro3’s downstream signaling cascades, potential cross-talk with αvβ5 integrin, and the modulatory control of Rac1/PI3K activity. This incomplete mechanistic understanding introduces uncertainties into how robustly Tyro3 agonism can be translated into effective POS internalization. In addition, the structural homology among the TAM receptors poses a potential challenge in developing antibodies that can selectively activate Tyro3 without unintended cross-reactivity with MerTK or Axl—a challenge that has been encountered in the development of TAM receptor inhibitors in oncology (Hsu et al., 2019; Myers et al., 2019).  

Furthermore, prior therapeutic approaches targeting TAM receptors in other disease areas have predominantly involved receptor inhibition rather than activation. In cancer, for example, the focus on developing inhibitors for TYRO3 has yielded several small molecule compounds and antibody-based antagonists (Hsu et al., 2019). The shift from inhibition to activation for therapeutic gain in dry AMD is conceptually innovative but also uncharted territory with limited preclinical validation. The delivery of antibody therapeutics to the retina presents additional hurdles, including issues related to ocular penetration, stability, and dosing regimen—factors that must be rigorously optimized in subsequent preclinical studies. Finally, while expression analyses confirm that Tyro3 is present in the retina and RPE, quantitative differences in receptor density between in vitro cell cultures and in vivo retinal tissue may influence the efficacy and dosing of the candidate mAb (Palczewska et al., 2016).  

Overall, the anti‐Tyro3 agonist antibody candidate presents a compelling and innovative therapeutic strategy for dry AMD by aiming to leverage Tyro3’s compensatory signaling capacity in the RPE to overcome deficits in MerTK. Its strengths lie in the high specificity of an agonist mAb approach, the solid genetic rationale provided by models showing retinal protection via Tyro3, and the potential to modulate key intracellular pathways such as PI3K and Rac1 that are essential for phagocytosis and anti-inflammatory responses (Lieffrig et al., 2023; Akalu et al., 2017). However, significant weaknesses remain, including the incomplete capacity of Tyro3 signaling to fully restore phagocytic activity in MerTK-deficient settings, the comparatively limited mechanistic insight into Tyro3’s actions in RPE cells, and the challenges associated with achieving high specificity among structurally similar TAM receptors. Moreover, whereas robust preclinical data exist supporting the concept of Tyro3 acting as a genetic suppressor of photoreceptor degeneration, the translation of these findings into a receptor-specific agonist antibody therapy for dry AMD is largely uncharted and will require extensive validation in both in vitro models and animal studies (Lieffrig et al., 2023; Palczewska et al., 2016).  

From an overall perspective, the candidate shows promise by addressing an unmet need in dry AMD, particularly through its potential to modulate inflammatory and phagocytic pathways compromised in the disease. The strategy of using receptor-specific agonism to bypass defects in MerTK signaling is innovative and supported by genetic evidence, yet its success hinges on the ability to induce sufficient downstream activation of PI3K/Rac1 and to engage integrin-mediated mechanisms to boost phagocytosis. Given that prior studies indicate that Tyro3 alone does not fully complement the loss of MerTK in terms of POS clearance—leading primarily to anti-inflammatory maintenance rather than complete phagocytic restoration—further studies are needed to determine whether antibody-mediated activation can overcome this limitation or whether combination therapies might be warranted (Lieffrig et al., 2023).  

In conclusion, while the anti‐Tyro3 agonist antibody candidate is built upon a robust scientific hypothesis with promising preclinical genetic support, its therapeutic viability for dry AMD will depend on resolving several key issues: confirming that elevated Tyro3 signaling can indeed significantly enhance POS phagocytosis, ensuring that the designed antibody is both highly selective and potent in the complex ocular environment, and establishing favorable pharmacokinetic and safety profiles in relevant preclinical models. The current body of evidence indicates that Tyro3 can partially compensate for MerTK deficiency in terms of anti-inflammatory signaling and retinal preservation, which bodes well for its potential application in dry AMD (Lieffrig et al., 2023; Akalu et al., 2017). However, the incomplete restoration of phagocytosis observed in MerTK-deficient models even when Tyro3 is active suggests that this approach, while promising, may need to be part of a broader strategy to fully rescue RPE function. Thus, the candidate merits further investigation, with a recommendation to pursue detailed preclinical studies to refine the antibody design, evaluate downstream signaling and biological effects in RPE cells, and ultimately determine whether this approach can translate into a clinically meaningful therapy for dry AMD.
