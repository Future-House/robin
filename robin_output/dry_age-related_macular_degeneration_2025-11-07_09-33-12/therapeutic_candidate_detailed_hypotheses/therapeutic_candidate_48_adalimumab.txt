Proposal for Adalimumab (Anti-TNFα mAb; Humira)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Adalimumab (marketed as Humira) is a fully humanized recombinant IgG1 monoclonal antibody developed using recombinant DNA technology that targets tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNFα). As a member of the anti‐TNFα class of biologics, adalimumab is designed to bind both soluble and membrane‐bound TNFα with high affinity, thereby preventing its interaction with the TNF receptors (TNFR1 and TNFR2) on target cells. This antibody was originally discovered and engineered to address systemic inflammatory diseases, and its fully human composition is intended to lower immunogenicity compared to earlier chimeric molecules such as infliximab. In its development, molecular engineering efforts focused on isolating the relevant antibody variable regions that exhibit specificity for TNFα, followed by the construction of a recombinant molecule with human constant regions to improve tolerability and clinical efficacy. Anti‐TNFα antibodies belong to a broader class of therapeutic compounds that have dramatically changed the treatment landscape for autoimmune and inflammatory diseases. They have been widely applied in conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, Crohn’s disease, and ulcerative colitis, and over the past two decades, their use has been extended into ocular inflammatory diseases such as non‐infectious uveitis. Such applications underscore the versatility and translational potential of TNFα inhibition in modulating inflammatory cascades (Theodossiadis et al., 2007; Levy-Clarke et al., 2014).

Therapeutic History:  
Adalimumab’s clinical journey began with its approval for systemic autoimmune diseases where TNFα plays a fundamental role in mediating inflammation and tissue damage. Its robust performance in rheumatoid arthritis and related conditions has been well documented in numerous large-scale randomized controlled trials, which demonstrated significant improvement in clinical outcomes and inflammatory markers. These successes provided the foundation for exploring its application in other diseases characterized by inflammatory dysregulation. In recent years, growing evidence from both biochemical studies and clinical observations has highlighted the role of chronic inflammation in the pathogenesis of several retinal degenerative disorders. In particular, studies have shown that TNFα is upregulated in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells and may contribute to the degeneration seen in dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD). For example, preclinical work has demonstrated that inflammation mediated by TNFα can impair the integrity of RPE cells, reduce the expression of proteins critical for RPE function (including visual cycle proteins and complement regulators) and disrupt phagocytic clearance mechanisms; these findings form the basis for hypothesizing that TNFα blockade may preserve RPE function in dry AMD (Dörschmann et al., 2023; García-Layana et al., 2021).

While the majority of clinical uses of adalimumab have been in the realm of systemic and ocular inflammatory conditions such as non‐infectious uveitis, there has been emerging interest in repurposing anti‐TNFα therapies for retinal degenerations. In the context of ocular disease, several investigators have explored both systemic and local (intravitreal or subconjunctival) routes of administration to modulate intraocular inflammation. Pilot clinical trials and case reports have assessed intravitreal adalimumab in patients with uveitis and even in those with neovascular (“wet”) AMD refractory to anti‐VEGF therapies; these studies reported varying degrees of efficacy and underscored an acceptable local safety profile, though in some instances, higher dosages have been associated with ocular toxicity. In animal studies, intravitreal or subconjunctival administration of adalimumab has been shown to reduce inflammation and offer neuroprotection in models of retinal degeneration, such as in the rd10 mouse model for retinitis pigmentosa, establishing a precedent for local administration to achieve high ocular levels with minimal systemic exposure. Despite these advancements, use of adalimumab specifically for dry AMD has not been established in clinical practice. The proposed approach builds on the mechanistic insights from studies demonstrating that elevated TNFα levels can lead to downregulation of phagocytic receptors and lysosomal enzymes in RPE cells—processes central to dry AMD pathology—and posits that reversing these effects via TNFα neutralization may preserve RPE function (Nashine et al., 2022; Hospital Universitari Vall d’Hebron Research Institute, 2010; Olivares-González et al., 2020).

Mechanism of Action:  
The molecular mechanism of adalimumab centers on its targeted neutralization of TNFα. TNFα is a proinflammatory cytokine produced by a variety of cell types that include activated T lymphocytes, macrophages, and, importantly, resident cells in the eye such as RPE and Müller glia. When secreted into the extracellular milieu, TNFα binds to its receptors (TNFR1 and TNFR2), triggering intracellular signaling cascades that lead to the activation of nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) and other pathways that promote the expression of additional inflammatory cytokines, adhesion molecules, and matrix metalloproteinases. These processes collectively contribute to a sustained inflammatory response that can result in cellular dysfunction and death. Adalimumab, by virtue of its high affinity binding, sequesters TNFα and prevents receptor engagement. In doing so, it effectively interrupts these downstream proinflammatory signals and halts the amplification of the inflammatory cascade (Wang et al., 2011; Levy-Clarke et al., 2014).

In the context of retinal pigment epithelium cells, TNFα-mediated signaling has been implicated in the suppression of genes critical to the maintenance of RPE functions. Specifically, experimental studies have suggested that exposure to TNFα leads to a reduction in the expression of MERTK—a receptor tyrosine kinase essential for the phagocytic clearance of photoreceptor outer segments (POS)—as well as cathepsin D, a lysosomal enzyme crucial for the degradation of engulfed material. The decrease in MERTK expression compromises the ability of RPE cells to efficiently phagocytose POS, thereby potentially contributing to the accumulation of cellular debris, drusen formation, and further RPE stress that are characteristic of dry AMD. In parallel, a reduction in cathepsin D would impair lysosomal degradation, exacerbating the intracellular buildup of waste products and further undermining cellular homeostasis in the RPE. By neutralizing TNFα, adalimumab is hypothesized to prevent these detrimental changes, preserving the expression levels of MERTK and cathepsin D and thereby maintaining robust phagocytic and degradative capacity in RPE cells (Dörschmann et al., 2023; Levy-Clarke et al., 2014; García-Layana et al., 2021).

At the molecular level, the binding of adalimumab to TNFα is mediated via its antigen-binding (Fab) region, which recognizes specific epitopes on the cytokine. This interaction is characterized by high affinity and specificity, ensuring that even low concentrations of the antibody can effectively neutralize TNFα activity. The blockade prevents TNFα from initiating receptor-mediated signaling that would otherwise promote inflammation and cellular stress. Moreover, because TNFα is known to regulate not only inflammatory cytokine production but also apoptotic processes, its inhibition may confer additional neuroprotective effects in the retina by reducing signals that lead to programmed cell death. The comprehensive inhibition of TNFα by adalimumab therefore stands as a multifaceted intervention, addressing both the inflammatory insult and its secondary effects on critical cellular functions in the RPE (Wang et al., 2011; Levy-Clarke et al., 2014; Nashine et al., 2022).

Expected Effect:  
The therapeutic hypothesis for leveraging adalimumab in dry AMD rests on its potential to neutralize elevated TNFα levels in the subretinal microenvironment. Elevated TNFα has been implicated in the pathological downregulation of key proteins involved in RPE function, notably MERTK and cathepsin D. MERTK is a receptor that plays an essential role in the phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments, a process that is critical for the renewal of photoreceptors and the overall maintenance of retinal health. Cathepsin D, on the other hand, is involved in the lysosomal degradation of the ingested material; together, these proteins ensure that the RPE can effectively clear and recycle photoreceptor debris. In dry AMD, this phagocytic process is compromised, leading to the accumulation of cellular waste products and drusen formation, which further fuels inflammation and RPE degeneration (Dörschmann et al., 2023; García-Layana et al., 2021).

Administration of adalimumab is expected to interrupt this cascade by neutralizing TNFα, thereby preventing its signaling that leads to the downregulation of MERTK and cathepsin D. With TNFα effectively neutralized, RPE cells should experience a more favorable molecular milieu in which the expression of these critical proteins is maintained at functional levels. This preservation would, in turn, support the RPE’s capacity to phagocytose and degrade photoreceptor outer segments, thereby averting the buildup of toxic waste products. Furthermore, since TNFα signaling also contributes to other aspects of cellular stress and inflammation—such as the upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and the induction of apoptotic pathways—the neutralization of TNFα by adalimumab may also reduce overall cellular stress and promote RPE cell survival and function (Dörschmann et al., 2023; García-Layana et al., 2021; Nashine et al., 2022).

In vitro assays using cultured human RPE cells exposed to TNFα could be employed to test this hypothesis. In these experiments, treatment with adalimumab should result in the normalization or preservation of MERTK and cathepsin D expression levels when compared to TNFα-treated controls. In addition, functional assays measuring the uptake and degradation of photoreceptor outer segments (or surrogate fluorescent particles) would be expected to show improved phagocytic efficiency in RPE cells treated with adalimumab. Such findings would provide robust mechanistic support for the proposed therapeutic strategy. Moreover, preliminary in vivo studies in appropriate animal models of dry AMD or models of induced RPE dysfunction could further validate that TNFα blockade leads to preservation of RPE integrity, improved photoreceptor survival, and retardation of retinal degeneration (Dörschmann et al., 2023; García-Layana et al., 2021; Olivares-González et al., 2020).

Considering that the expression of MERTK is well documented in RPE cells and that its function is critical for POS phagocytosis—a process that declines with aging and in the presence of inflammatory mediators—the ability of adalimumab to maintain or restore MERTK expression would be highly relevant in the context of dry AMD. In the proposed strategy, adalimumab may also help to stabilize other RPE functions, such as the regulation of the complement cascade and the visual cycle, both of which have been shown to be adversely affected by TNFα exposure. Thus, the anticipated effect of adalimumab is not limited solely to the preservation of phagocytic machinery; it may also contribute to a broader stabilization of RPE cell health in the face of chronic low-grade inflammation that characterizes dry AMD (Dörschmann et al., 2023; Levy-Clarke et al., 2014; García-Layana et al., 2021).

Overall Evaluation:  
The overall evaluation of adalimumab as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD reveals a compelling yet cautiously optimistic perspective. One of the principal strengths of adalimumab lies in its well-characterized mechanism of action; by neutralizing TNFα, it directly addresses a central mediator of inflammation that has been implicated in the dysregulation of RPE functions. The experimental evidence indicating that TNFα exposure can lead to the downregulation of essential proteins such as MERTK and cathepsin D provides a strong rationale for the hypothesis that TNFα inhibition might preserve the RPE’s ability to maintain photoreceptor homeostasis. This mechanistic insight is particularly attractive because it targets one of the fundamental dysfunctions in dry AMD—namely, the failure of the RPE to clear photoreceptor outer segments effectively, which leads to the accumulation of drusen and progressive retinal degeneration (Dörschmann et al., 2023; García-Layana et al., 2021).

Furthermore, the extensive clinical experience with adalimumab in systemic and ocular inflammatory diseases supports its safety profile. The fact that adalimumab is already approved by regulatory agencies, coupled with several clinical studies demonstrating its localized safety when administered intravitreally or via related routes in ocular conditions such as uveitis, adds to its attractiveness as a candidate for repurposing (Hospital Universitari Vall d’Hebron Research Institute, 2010; Leal et al., 2018; Theodossiadis et al., 2007).

Despite these strengths, several weaknesses and uncertainties must be acknowledged. To date, most studies involving adalimumab in the ocular context have focused on inflammatory conditions such as uveitis and neovascular AMD rather than dry AMD specifically. As a result, direct clinical evidence supporting its efficacy in preserving RPE phagocytic function in dry AMD is currently limited. The hypothesis that adalimumab can prevent the TNFα-induced downregulation of MERTK and cathepsin D is primarily based on preclinical observations, and further mechanistic studies, both in vitro and in relevant animal models, are necessary to validate this pathway in the context of dry AMD (Dörschmann et al., 2023; Nashine et al., 2022; García-Layana et al., 2021).

Another potential challenge with using adalimumab for dry AMD is related to drug delivery and dosing. While intravitreal administration has shown promise in limited studies, determining the optimal dose that achieves effective TNFα blockade without incurring retinal toxicity remains an unresolved issue. Some animal studies have indicated that higher doses of intravitreal adalimumab can lead to retinal inflammation, reduced electroretinogram responses, or histopathological evidence of toxicity. Given that dry AMD is a chronic condition and would likely require repeated dosing or sustained drug delivery systems, the long-term safety of local TNFα blockade needs to be rigorously evaluated (Manzano et al., 2008; Pascual-Camps et al., 2014).

Moreover, because TNFα plays roles in normal immune surveillance and tissue repair, its complete inhibition—especially in an aged population that may have impaired immune responses—could theoretically predispose patients to infections or other adverse effects. Although the systemic safety profile of adalimumab is well established, the ocular microenvironment has distinct immunological characteristics, and the long-term effects of TNFα inhibition on retinal homeostasis, particularly regarding complement activation and maintenance of the blood–retinal barrier, require further study. There is also the possibility that compensatory inflammatory pathways may become activated in response to TNFα blockade, potentially diminishing the efficacy of the intervention over time (García-Layana et al., 2021; Theodossiadis et al., 2007; Sharma et al., 2009).

Additionally, the magnitude of the contribution of TNFα to the pathogenesis of dry AMD relative to other factors—such as oxidative stress, genetic predispositions (including polymorphisms in complement factors), and lipid metabolism abnormalities—remains to be fully delineated. It is possible that TNFα is one of several drivers of RPE dysfunction, and that its inhibition may need to be combined with other therapeutic strategies (for example, antioxidant supplementation or complement inhibitors) to achieve a meaningful clinical benefit. Therefore, while the rationale for adalimumab is compelling, a monotherapy approach may not be sufficient to halt the multifactorial progression of dry AMD (Dörschmann et al., 2023; García-Layana et al., 2021; Nussenblatt et al., 2010).

In summary, adalimumab presents a promising repurposed candidate for the treatment of dry AMD based on its potent and specific inhibition of TNFα—a cytokine implicated in the downregulation of crucial RPE proteins such as MERTK and cathepsin D. The drug’s extensive use in other inflammatory settings, along with preliminary evidence supporting its ocular safety, lend credibility to the hypothesis that TNFα blockade can preserve the phagocytic machinery of RPE cells and potentially slow the degenerative process characteristic of dry AMD. Nevertheless, significant challenges remain, including the need for robust preclinical validation in dry AMD models, optimization of ocular dosing regimens, and long-term safety assessments in an elderly population. A carefully designed series of in vitro experiments using human RPE cells—followed by well-controlled animal studies—should be undertaken to establish proof of concept regarding the preservation of MERTK and cathepsin D expression and function. If these studies yield positive results, subsequent early-phase clinical trials could be justified to evaluate the efficacy of intravitreal adalimumab in patients with dry AMD (Dörschmann et al., 2023; Levy-Clarke et al., 2014; García-Layana et al., 2021; Olivares-González et al., 2020; Hospital Universitari Vall d’Hebron Research Institute, 2010).

In light of these considerations, our recommendation is to advance adalimumab into a rigorous preclinical testing pipeline aimed specifically at dry AMD models. This should include studies that measure the expression levels and activity of MERTK and cathepsin D in RPE cells under conditions of TNFα stimulation, both in the presence and absence of adalimumab treatment. Pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiling in ocular tissues, along with assessments of retinal structure and function via in vivo imaging and electrophysiological testing, will be critical to determining an appropriate dosing strategy. Finally, integration with complementary therapeutic approaches may be considered to address other facets of dry AMD pathogenesis. While the path to clinical application is fraught with challenges, the potential benefit of preserving RPE phagocytic function—and by extension, photoreceptor survival—in a disease with very limited current treatment options provides a strong impetus for further development of this candidate (Dörschmann et al., 2023; Levy-Clarke et al., 2014; García-Layana et al., 2021; Nashine et al., 2022).

In conclusion, adalimumab’s established molecular profile as a potent TNFα inhibitor, its extensive clinical track record in systemic and ocular inflammatory diseases, and the emerging data linking TNFα to RPE dysfunction collectively support the rationale for its repurposing in dry AMD. Nevertheless, given the complexity of AMD pathogenesis and the potential risks associated with chronic intraocular immunomodulation, a thorough and methodical approach to preclinical investigation is warranted before embarking on large-scale clinical trials. The ultimate goal is to validate that neutralization of TNFα can indeed preserve the phagocytic capabilities of the RPE, thereby maintaining retinal health and preventing the progression of dry AMD (Dörschmann et al., 2023; García-Layana et al., 2021; Levy-Clarke et al., 2014; Theodossiadis et al., 2007).

Strengths of this candidate include its potent and specific inhibition of TNFα, which is a key mediator in inflammatory cascades, its favorable immunogenicity profile stemming from its fully human composition, and its prior demonstration of safety in ocular applications such as uveitis. In addition, the biological rationale is supported by evidence that TNFα negatively impacts the expression of proteins critical for RPE phagocytosis and lysosomal degradation—a process that, when impaired, underlies the pathology of dry AMD (Dörschmann et al., 2023; García-Layana et al., 2021).

On the other hand, weaknesses center on the limited direct evidence for efficacy in dry AMD, potential challenges in achieving precise intraocular dosing without inducing retinal toxicity, and uncertain long-term consequences of sustained TNFα inhibition in the delicate immune environment of the eye. Moreover, the multifactorial nature of dry AMD suggests that a singular focus on TNFα may not fully address the disease’s complex pathophysiology, raising the possibility that combination therapies might eventually be necessary for optimal outcomes (Manzano et al., 2008; Sharma et al., 2009; Nussenblatt et al., 2010).

Given these observations, our evaluation concludes that adalimumab represents a promising and scientifically sound candidate for repurposing to treat dry AMD. Its mechanistic profile aligns well with the goal of preserving RPE phagocytic function by preventing TNFα-mediated downregulation of key proteins like MERTK and cathepsin D. However, to move forward with this candidate, research efforts must focus on resolving the outstanding questions through rigorous preclinical studies, optimization of the drug delivery method, and detailed long-term safety assessments. If successful, these steps could pave the way for early-phase clinical trials designed to test the efficacy of intravitreal adalimumab in stabilizing or improving RPE function in patients with dry AMD (Dörschmann et al., 2023; Levy-Clarke et al., 2014; García-Layana et al., 2021; Nashine et al., 2022; Hospital Universitari Vall d’Hebron Research Institute, 2010).

In summary, while the current body of literature on adalimumab has primarily focused on its role in systemic inflammatory diseases and ocular inflammatory conditions such as uveitis and neovascular AMD, the underlying mechanistic data provide a persuasive rationale for extending its application to dry AMD. The hypothesis that adalimumab can preserve RPE phagocytic function by neutralizing TNFα—thereby preventing the deleterious downregulation of MERTK and cathepsin D—is scientifically plausible and merits further systematic investigation. The potential benefits of repurposing a well-characterized agent with an established safety profile are substantial, especially in a disease area where effective treatments remain elusive (Dörschmann et al., 2023; García-Layana et al., 2021; Levy-Clarke et al., 2014; Theodossiadis et al., 2007).

Overall, our recommendation for the drug development team is to prioritize a series of detailed preclinical studies that examine the effects of adalimumab on RPE cells under TNFα-induced stress conditions, with particular emphasis on the regulation of MERTK and cathepsin D expression and subsequent phagocytic activity. These studies should involve both conventional cell culture models and in vivo models that mimic the chronic inflammatory environment observed in dry AMD. Should these studies yield positive results, a phase I/II clinical trial focusing on safety and early efficacy in patients with dry AMD would be justified. This stepwise approach will ensure that the promising mechanistic rationale is thoroughly vetted before large-scale clinical application (Dörschmann et al., 2023; García-Layana et al., 2021; Olivares-González et al., 2020).

In conclusion, the convergence of molecular, biochemical, and clinical evidence supports the further exploration of adalimumab as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD. Its ability to mitigate TNFα-mediated inflammatory cascades could play a critical role in preserving RPE function, maintaining photoreceptor health, and ultimately slowing the progression of a disease that currently lacks effective treatments. Nonetheless, rigorous preclinical validation and careful consideration of dosing and long-term safety will be essential to realize its potential in this new indication (Dörschmann et al., 2023; Levy-Clarke et al., 2014; Theodossiadis et al., 2007; Olivares-González et al., 2020).
