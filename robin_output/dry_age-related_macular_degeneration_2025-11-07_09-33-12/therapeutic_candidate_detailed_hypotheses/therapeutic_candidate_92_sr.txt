Proposal for SR-3029 (CK1δ/ε inhibitor; Tocris Cat. No. 6401)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
SR-3029 is a synthetic small molecule designed to specifically inhibit casein kinase 1 delta (CK1δ) and epsilon (CK1ε). Originally developed in the context of oncology, this compound (catalog number Tocris Cat. No. 6401) belongs to the class of CK1 inhibitors, which have been widely investigated for their role in modulating circadian rhythms and cell cycle regulation. Chemically, SR-3029 has been optimized for high potency with an IC₅₀ in the low nanomolar range (approximately 10 nM) in various cell systems. The compound’s molecular design focuses on targeting the catalytic domain of CK1 isoforms, thereby interfering with their ability to phosphorylate downstream substrates. Historically, CK1 inhibitors have been used to interrogate the biology of circadian processes and have demonstrated promising anti-proliferative effects in certain cancer cell lines, particularly in melanoma models (Battaglin et al., 2021, pp. 9–11). The underlying rationale for its repurposing in dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) hinges on the well-documented role of CK1 in regulating the stability of circadian clock proteins, specifically PERIOD 2 (PER2), which is a crucial mediator of circadian regulation. Although SR-3029 was initially synthesized with oncological applications in mind, the chemical properties that confer potent inhibition of CK1δ/ε have now been hypothesized to be beneficial in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells, where robust circadian timing is essential for maintaining phagocytic rhythms necessary for retinal homeostasis (Battaglin et al., 2021, pp. 9–11).

Therapeutic History:  
Over the past decade, the research community has leveraged CK1 inhibitors to explore diverse cellular functions through their impact on circadian and cell cycle-related pathways. SR-3029, along with related analogs such as SR-2890, has been extensively characterized in preclinical cancer models where inhibition of CK1δ/ε resulted in anti-proliferative activity. For instance, studies conducted in melanoma cell lines, including A375, have consistently demonstrated that SR-3029 exhibits robust activity in suppressing tumor cell proliferation by modulating circadian proteins that also play roles in cell cycle control (Battaglin et al., 2021, pp. 9–11). Despite these promising anti-cancer properties, the candidate has not been previously investigated for ocular indications. A comprehensive search of biomedical and clinical literature reveals that neither the CK1 isoforms nor inhibitors such as SR-3029 have been robustly associated with the treatment of dry AMD or similar retinal degenerative conditions. This lack of direct evidence is underscored by data from the Open Targets Platform, which indicates no significant target–disease associations for CSNK1D or CSNK1E in age-related macular degeneration (Buniello et al., 2025). In effect, while the historical therapeutic applications of SR-3029 have been concentrated in oncology, there remains a significant knowledge gap and an unmet opportunity in exploring its potential to normalize circadian regulation in the RPE of dry AMD patients.

Mechanism of Action:  
The fundamental mechanism of action of SR-3029 stems from its ability to inhibit CK1δ/ε activity. CK1δ is a pivotal regulator of biological rhythms, in part by phosphorylating key sites on the circadian regulator PER2. Specific phosphorylation events, particularly at residues such as T347, have been demonstrated to dictate the stability and subsequent degradation of PER2, thereby influencing the periodicity of the circadian clock (Eng et al., 2017, pp. 2–4). Under normal conditions, CK1-mediated phosphorylation tags PER2 for ubiquitin-driven proteasomal degradation, ensuring a timely turnover that is essential for maintaining a robust circadian rhythm. Pharmacological inhibition of CK1δ/ε provided by agents like SR-3029 is expected to reduce such phosphorylation events, thereby stabilizing PER2 and allowing it to accumulate within the cell. This stabilization is theorized to provide a more consistent and regulated circadian signal, which is particularly relevant in tissues reliant on synchronized timing mechanisms.

In retinal pigment epithelium cells, the circadian clock plays an integral role in regulating the phagocytic clearing of photoreceptor outer segments (POS), a process intrinsically linked to the diurnal cycle. The proper timing of phagocytosis in the RPE is essential to nutritive and metabolic homeostasis in the retina, as the RPE is responsible for the daily clearance of shed POS. Experimental evidence from non-ocular systems underscores that CK1 inhibition can lead to enhanced PER2 stability, a critical factor in rhythmic cellular activities (Eng et al., 2017, pp. 2–4, 16–17). Additionally, studies have reported that CK1δ and CK1ε not only affect PER2 stability but also interact in a broader regulatory network that includes proline-directed kinases, such as cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), as well as light-responsive signaling pathways that further entrench the circadian control of cellular functions (Eng et al., 2017, pp. 12–14).

Given these mechanistic insights, SR-3029’s inhibition of CK1δ/ε is expected to directly impact the circadian machinery within the RPE. By stabilizing PER2, the compound may restore circadian phase coherence and improve the timing of daily photoreceptor debris clearance. While the biochemical evidence for SR-3029’s action is robust in non-ocular tissue, the permissiveness of these pathways within the RPE microenvironment remains to be fully documented. Nonetheless, the extrapolation from studies in circadian regulation suggests that the proposed intervention in the context of dry AMD is biochemically plausible (Eng et al., 2017, pp. 2–4, 12–14, 16–17).

Expected Effect:  
The working hypothesis for repurposing SR-3029 in the treatment of dry AMD is predicated on its ability to modulate circadian rhythms in retinal pigment epithelium cells. Specifically, the expectation is that SR-3029 will inhibit the catalytic activity of CK1δ/ε in the RPE at an IC₅₀ of roughly 10 nM, resulting in the stabilization of PER2. The enhancement of PER2 stability is anticipated to restore the circadian timing of phagocytic bursts—a process critical to the timely ingestion of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) by RPE cells. In dry AMD, impaired or misaligned phagocytic rhythms can lead to the accumulation of cellular debris and subsequent retinal degeneration. By reestablishing the diurnal cycle of phagocytosis, SR-3029 holds the potential to improve the clearance of POS debris and sustain retinal homeostasis over time (Eng et al., 2017, pp. 16–17).

In vitro studies with the ARPE-19 cell line have suggested that disruption of circadian cues can be at least partially rectified by targeting CK1 activity, thereby restoring the correct peak timing and magnitude of phagocytic activity. Although these observations derive from limited datasets, they serve as a conceptual proof-of-principle that the circadian machinery—when appropriately modulated—can influence phagocytic outcomes. With SR-3029, the expected sequence of events is as follows: (1) Inhibition of CK1δ/ε leads to a reduction in PER2 phosphorylation; (2) PER2 becomes stabilized and accumulates in a controlled, rhythmic fashion; (3) Enhanced PER2 levels reset or reinforce the intrinsic circadian clock of the RPE; (4) A reestablished diurnal rhythm drives the timely initiation of phagocytosis, thereby ensuring that photoreceptor outer segments are ingested during the optimal time window. The overall outcome of this mechanism should be an improvement in the clearance of POS debris, which is a critical factor in mitigating the progression of retinal degeneration in dry AMD (Eng et al., 2017, pp. 2–4, 12–14, 16–17).

On a molecular level, several markers are anticipated in an effective experimental model: a measurable increase in PER2 protein levels, a synchronized restoration of downstream circadian gene expression, and an improvement in the amplitude and timing of phagocytic bursts as quantified in vitro. Furthermore, if SR-3029 can exert its pharmacological effects specifically within the RPE without significant off-target activities, it may offer a dual benefit by not only modulating circadian rhythms but also potentially ameliorating any concurrent cellular stress responses linked to dysregulated CK1 activity. However, it is important to note that while the proposed mechanism is supported by mechanistic studies in non-ocular tissues, direct in vivo evidence in retinal models of dry AMD remains sparse. The data from the Open Targets Platform reveal an absence of direct associations between CK1 isoforms and AMD, suggesting that while the biochemical rationale is strong, further targeted studies are needed to corroborate these expected physiological outcomes in the eye (Buniello et al., 2025).

Overall Evaluation:  
In summary, SR-3029 presents as a promising therapeutic candidate by virtue of its potent inhibition of CK1δ/ε and its well-characterized biochemical mechanism that involves the stabilization of PER2 protein. The strengths of this candidate are manifold. First, its high potency at low nanomolar concentrations translates to a favorable pharmacodynamic profile, which has been confirmed in oncology models (Battaglin et al., 2021, pp. 9–11). The ability of SR-3029 to modulate the circadian clock through stabilization of PER2 positions it as an attractive candidate for diseases where dysregulated circadian rhythms play a pivotal role, such as in the daily phagocytic activity of the RPE. The theoretical framework underpinning its repurposing for dry AMD is built upon robust circadian biology, wherein proper clock function is essential for mediating temporal processes like phagocytosis (Eng et al., 2017, pp. 2–4, 16–17). This mechanistic link is particularly relevant given that the maintenance of retinal homeostasis depends critically on the precise timing of photoreceptor debris clearance—a process that is compromised in dry AMD.

Despite the promising mechanistic rationale, there are significant challenges and weaknesses that must be addressed before SR-3029 can be considered a validated therapeutic for dry AMD. A key limitation is the absence of direct evidence linking CK1δ/ε inhibition to improved retinal outcomes in preclinical models of dry AMD. Data from the Open Targets Platform clearly show an absence of target–disease associations between CSNK1D/CSNK1E and AMD, suggesting that the role of CK1 isoforms in retinal degeneration remains underexplored (Buniello et al., 2025). This gap in the literature highlights the necessity for dedicated in vitro and in vivo studies to determine whether SR-3029 can elicit the expected beneficial effects on RPE phagocytosis and overall retinal function.

Another potential concern is the translatability of findings from cancer cell systems to non-transformed, terminally differentiated cells such as those found in the RPE. The anti-proliferative activity of SR-3029, while advantageous in the context of oncology, raises questions regarding its safety profile when applied to ocular tissues. Off-target effects, particularly those that might interfere with the normal physiological functions of non-dividing retinal cells, must be rigorously evaluated. Additionally, the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties established in oncological studies may not directly mirror the behavior of the drug in the ocular microenvironment. Issues such as drug delivery to the RPE, potential retinal toxicity, and the compound’s ability to traverse the blood–retinal barrier will need thorough investigation in preclinical models.

From a mechanistic perspective, while the hypothesis that SR-3029 will stabilize PER2 and thereby enhance circadian regulation in the RPE is compelling, it remains a largely theoretical proposition at this point. The published literature underscores the importance of CK1-mediated phosphorylation in determining PER2 stability, but direct evidence for such mechanisms in ocular tissues—particularly in the context of phagocytic function—is limited (Eng et al., 2017, pp. 2–4, 12–14, 16–17). It is critical to recognize that even if PER2 stabilization is achieved, the downstream impact on phagocytic burst timing may be influenced by a complex interplay of additional regulatory factors present in the RPE. Therefore, the expected effect of SR-3029 on diurnal POS ingestion, although plausible, remains to be empirically validated.

A further point of evaluation concerns the broader pharmacological profile of SR-3029. Studies in oncology have demonstrated that SR-3029, along with its analogs, possesses favorable pharmacokinetic properties, such as stability and interactions with cytochrome P450 enzymes, which are indicative of good metabolic profiles (Battaglin et al., 2021, pp. 9–11). These characteristics are beneficial from a drug development standpoint, as they suggest that the compound could be administered with a predictable safety profile and efficacious dosing regimen. Nonetheless, the ocular application of such compounds often requires re-optimization of dosing strategies and formulation development to ensure adequate drug levels reach the target tissue without eliciting off-target toxicities.

In weighing the overall evidence, SR-3029 stands out as a candidate with a strong biochemical rationale, particularly when viewed through the lens of circadian biology. The compound’s ability to modulate kinase activity that governs the stability of the circadian regulator PER2 ties directly into the hypothesis that reestablishing a proper circadian rhythm in RPE cells will lead to improved phagocytosis. Given that impaired phagocytic clearance is a significant pathogenic contributor in dry AMD, this approach represents an innovative therapeutic strategy that moves beyond conventional anti-inflammatory or vascular-targeted therapies. The repurposing of a compound originally developed for cancer therapy to address retinal degeneration is a novel and potentially transformative idea that leverages the deep mechanistic insights gained from circadian biology research (Battaglin et al., 2021, pp. 9–11; Buniello et al., 2025; Eng et al., 2017, pp. 2–4, 12–14, 16–17).

Conversely, the most notable weakness of the proposal lies in the current disconnect between the known pharmacodynamic actions of CK1 inhibitors and their demonstrated efficacy in retinal models of dry AMD. The lack of pre-existing data specifically linking CK1δ/ε inhibition with improvements in RPE phagocytosis, coupled with the Open Targets Platform’s indication of no known target–disease link between these kinases and AMD, raises concerns about the translational relevance of this approach (Buniello et al., 2025). Moreover, even though the anti-proliferative effects of SR-3029 are well documented in melanoma and related cancer models (Battaglin et al., 2021, pp. 9–11), these effects may not necessarily translate into clinical benefits in the context of retinal diseases, where the cellular landscape and treatment paradigms differ substantially.

In addition, the intricate balance of kinase signaling in the RPE must be considered. CK1 isoforms are involved in a myriad of cellular processes beyond circadian regulation, including cell survival, metabolism, and stress responses. Therefore, global inhibition of CK1δ/ε could theoretically disrupt other homeostatic processes, potentially leading to unintended consequences. Such off-target or pleiotropic effects, while possibly tolerable in cancer treatment—where the therapeutic window is often broader—could be more problematic in the delicate environment of the retina, where precise regulation is paramount.

It is also important to consider the broader landscape of AMD research. Many current therapeutic efforts in AMD focus on anti-angiogenic agents and complement pathway inhibitors; targeting the circadian machinery represents a relatively unexplored avenue. This novelty, while offering the potential for breakthroughs, also entails increased risk, as the underlying biology connecting circadian dysfunction to AMD pathology is not yet fully understood. For SR-3029 to be validated within this context, it will require rigorous preclinical investigations that definitively establish a causative link between CK1δ/ε activity, PER2 dynamics, and phagocytic efficiency in the RPE. Only then can the compound’s therapeutic efficacy in modifying disease progression in dry AMD be confidently assessed.

In conclusion, the current evaluation of SR-3029 reveals a compound with considerable promise based on its high potency as a CK1δ/ε inhibitor and its well-founded mechanistic basis in circadian regulation. The potential for SR-3029 to stabilize PER2 and thereby restore the circadian control of phagocytosis in the RPE is an intriguing therapeutic proposition for dry AMD. However, the absence of direct experimental evidence in retinal contexts, as highlighted by the Open Targets Platform data (Buniello et al., 2025), represents a significant gap in the literature. The repurposing strategy calls for a systematic evaluation in appropriate cell-based assays and animal models of dry AMD to determine if the pharmacological modulation of CK1 activity can translate into meaningful clinical benefits. Until such data are acquired, the application of SR-3029 in dry AMD remains speculative yet innovative. The strengths of the candidate lie in its potent kinase inhibition and the robust background of circadian biology, while its weaknesses are rooted in the lack of established target–disease connectivity and the potential for off-target effects in the RPE. Ultimately, SR-3029 embodies both promise and uncertainty, warranting further investigation to validate its efficacy and safety within the unique microenvironment of the retina (Battaglin et al., 2021, pp. 9–11; Buniello et al., 2025; Eng et al., 2017, pp. 2–4, 12–14, 16–17).
