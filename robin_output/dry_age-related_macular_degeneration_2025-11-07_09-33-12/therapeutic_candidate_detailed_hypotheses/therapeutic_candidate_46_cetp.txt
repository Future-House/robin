Proposal for CETP inhibitor anacetrapib (Evacetrapib analog; Merck research compound)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Anacetrapib is a synthetic small‐molecule inhibitor of cholesteryl ester transfer protein (CETP) originally developed by Merck as part of a class of compounds aimed at modulating lipid metabolism. This compound was identified and refined through extensive medicinal chemistry programs designed to target CETP’s lipid transfer function. As a member of the CETP inhibitor class, anacetrapib has been developed primarily for cardiovascular risk reduction by increasing high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) and lowering low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), and its chemical structure and binding properties have been designed to disrupt the lipid‐transfer tunnel of the CETP enzyme (Gurfinkel & Joy, 2011; Tall & Rader, 2018). The proposed repurposing for dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) is based on the rationale that by inhibiting CETP, the agent could raise local HDL levels in Bruch’s membrane (BrM) and improve reverse cholesterol transport, thereby reducing drusen‐like lipid accumulation and indirectly supporting retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) phagocytic capacity—a hypothesis derived from observed associations between CETP activity, local retinal lipid metabolism, and AMD pathogenesis (Betzler et al., 2020; Shughoury et al., 2022).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, CETP inhibitors have been evaluated extensively for their potential to address dyslipidemia and reduce atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. Anacetrapib, in particular, has undergone large clinical trials such as the DEFINE and REVEAL studies, where its efficacy in raising HDL‐C by approximately 138% and reducing LDL‐C by 37–40% compared to placebo was demonstrated (Cheung et al., 2017; Tall & Rader, 2018). The class of CETP inhibitors also includes compounds such as torcetrapib, dalcetrapib, and evacetrapib—with torcetrapib’s development being terminated due to off‐target effects including blood pressure elevations, while anacetrapib and evacetrapib showed more favorable profiles in subsequent trials (Gurfinkel & Joy, 2011; Sirtori, 2011). Although these compounds were developed to address cardiovascular pathologies, genetic and mechanistic studies have implicated CETP and HDL metabolism in the ocular environment as well. Several genome‐wide association studies and Mendelian randomization studies have demonstrated a relationship between CETP gene variants, HDL levels, and AMD risk (Burgess & Davey Smith, 2017; Schmidt et al., 2020). Despite this, no CETP inhibitor has previously been used in a clinical setting for the treatment of dry AMD or similar ocular diseases, and the repurposing of anacetrapib for dry AMD represents a novel therapeutic direction inspired by preclinical studies in murine models that suggest potential benefits in the retinal context (Cheung et al., 2017).

Mechanism of Action:  
Anacetrapib functions by binding to CETP, thereby preventing the transfer of cholesteryl esters from HDL to apolipoprotein B‐containing lipoproteins (i.e., LDL, very‐low‐density lipoprotein [VLDL]) and the subsequent exchange of triglycerides in the opposite direction. At the molecular level, CETP normally operates via a tunnel‐mechanism or shuttle mechanism to facilitate the bidirectional lipid transfer between lipoproteins. Inhibition of CETP by anacetrapib results in the accumulation of cholesterol-rich, enlarged HDL particles and a concomitant reduction in the levels of atherogenic LDL particles (Gurfinkel & Joy, 2011; Sirtori, 2011). In the context of the retina, emerging evidence suggests that CETP is expressed not only systemically but also locally—in the retinal pigment epithelium, photoreceptor outer segments, and interphotoreceptor matrix (Shughoury et al., 2022; Cheung et al., 2017). The rationale is that CETP activity in Bruch’s membrane may normally lower HDL levels, thereby impairing the capacity for reverse cholesterol transport in this specialized extracellular matrix, which can lead to the accumulation of lipids and the formation of drusen. By inhibiting CETP, anacetrapib may restore or increase local HDL levels, improving the efflux of accumulated cholesterol and other lipids from the RPE and neighboring structures (Fan et al., 2017; Lin et al., 2022). Moreover, the resulting HDL particles may gain enhanced cholesterol efflux capacity, as evidenced by preclinical studies that show increased cholesterol removal from macrophages via ABCA1- and ABCG1-mediated pathways (Sirtori, 2011; Page et al., 2016). In addition, proper HDL particle composition under CETP inhibition could influence key inflammatory regulatory mechanisms through the carriage of complement factors, which are linked to AMD pathogenesis (Tricorache et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2015).

Expected Effect:  
In the proposed assay, treatment with anacetrapib is expected to increase HDL levels locally in Bruch’s membrane, thereby enhancing reverse cholesterol transport across the retinal layers. The improved cholesterol efflux from the RPE is postulated to mitigate the accumulation of drusen-like lipid deposits—a hallmark of dry AMD—and potentially restore critical cellular functions such as integrin localization and associated phagocytic receptor activity (Cheung et al., 2017; Schmidt et al., 2020). This restoration of lipid homeostasis is anticipated to reduce the build-up of oxidized lipids and other debris beneath the RPE, ultimately alleviating RPE dysfunction—a central component in the pathogenesis of dry AMD (Burgess & Davey Smith, 2017; Cheung et al., 2017). The mechanism relies on the observation that HDL not only serves as a cholesterol acceptor but also carries important complement regulatory proteins and inflammatory mediators; thus, its normalization in Bruch’s membrane could indirectly support the phagocytic activity of RPE cells and improve tissue homeostasis (Lin et al., 2022; Cheung et al., 2017). Gene expression studies have identified CETP expression in ocular tissues, reinforcing the notion that local CETP inhibition may directly influence lipid handling in the retina (Shughoury et al., 2022; Cheung et al., 2017). Ultimately, the expected outcomes in preclinical models—and, by extension, in clinical trials—are a measurable reduction in drusen burden, improvement in RPE functionality, and a potential delay or attenuation in the progression of dry AMD (Schmidt et al., 2020; Cheung et al., 2017).

Overall Evaluation:  
The repurposing of anacetrapib for the treatment of dry AMD presents a compelling opportunity, supported by a solid mechanistic hypothesis and a favorable human safety profile established in cardiovascular trials. Among the clear strengths is the extensive biochemical and clinical data on CETP inhibitors in the context of dyslipidemia; anacetrapib has demonstrated robust HDL‐C raising and LDL‐C lowering effects, with a tolerability profile superior to earlier CETP inhibitors such as torcetrapib (Gurfinkel & Joy, 2011; Tall & Rader, 2018). Furthermore, emerging evidence of CETP expression in ocular tissues and its potential role in modulating local lipid metabolism—combined with findings that suggest dysfunctional HDL composition may contribute to drusen formation—lends strong support to the therapeutic rationale (Shughoury et al., 2022; Cheung et al., 2017).

However, there are important weaknesses and challenges that must be addressed. First, the genetic and epidemiological data linking HDL cholesterol and AMD have been paradoxical; several Mendelian randomization studies have implicated HDL‐raising alleles, particularly those related to CETP modulation, with an increased risk for AMD (Betzler et al., 2020; Fan et al., 2017). This counterintuitive observation raises concerns that simply increasing HDL levels may not guarantee improved RPE function unless the quality and functionality of HDL particles are also enhanced (Schmidt et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2022). Second, while the cardiovascular applications of anacetrapib provide a strong safety signal, the ocular-specific pharmacodynamics and tissue distribution of the drug remain largely unexplored. It is conceivable that the systemic modulation of HDL might not translate into the desired local changes in Bruch’s membrane, particularly given the unique lipid microenvironment of the retina (Cheung et al., 2017; Cheung et al., 2017). Third, preclinical data demonstrating that anacetrapib can reduce BrM lipid burden and restore integrin localization in murine models, although promising, have yet to be validated extensively, and species differences in CETP expression and lipid metabolism could limit translatability to human patients (Cheung et al., 2017; Cheung et al., 2017). Lastly, the long-term consequences of altering CETP activity in the retina are unknown, and there exists a potential risk that chronic CETP inhibition could inadvertently disrupt local complement regulation or other aspects of retinal homeostasis, given the tight interplay between lipid metabolism and inflammation in AMD pathogenesis (Tricorache et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2015).

Overall, while the conceptual framework underlying the repurposing of anacetrapib for dry AMD is scientifically intriguing and supported by a convergence of genetic, biochemical, and preclinical mechanistic data, further investigation is needed to reconcile the paradoxical relationships observed in human genetic studies, to precisely characterize the local retinal pharmacokinetics of the drug, and to validate its effects on reverse cholesterol transport in the specific context of the retina. In summary, anacetrapib holds potential as a novel therapeutic candidate for dry AMD, but its ultimate success will depend on carefully designed preclinical studies and early-phase clinical trials that specifically address its ocular efficacy and safety profile (Burgess & Davey Smith, 2017; Schmidt et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2022).
