Proposal for SR1078 (RORα agonist; Tocris Cat. No. 3491)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
SR1078 is a synthetic ligand originally discovered as the first reported small‐molecule agonist for the orphan nuclear receptors RORα and RORγ. It was developed on a benzenesulfonamide scaffold and is marketed commercially (Tocris Cat. No. 3491) as a tool compound for probing ROR biology in vitro and in vivo (Cyr, Bronner, & Crawford, 2016). As a RORα agonist, SR1078 belongs to the nuclear receptor modulator class of compounds, a family of therapeutics that have historically been used to orchestrate transcriptional programs related to metabolism, inflammation, and circadian regulation across various tissues (Solt & Burris, 2012). The synthetic origins of SR1078 trace back to programs that sought to optimize ligand interactions within the ROR ligand binding domain; detailed chemical characterization studies have shown that SR1078 binds and stabilizes the receptor’s active conformation, particularly by engaging critical residues that allow proper positioning of helix 12 for subsequent coactivator recruitment (Wang et al., 2010). This class of compounds has previously been employed primarily as a chemical biology probe in preclinical research, addressing metabolic dysregulation in liver and immunomodulatory effects in macrophages, and sometimes even in contexts such as nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) and autoimmune models (Matsuoka & Michihara, 2021; Wang et al., 2010).

Therapeutic History:  
SR1078 has been extensively exploited as a research tool rather than a clinical therapeutic; its major preclinical applications include the characterization of RORα-dependent transcriptional regulation in cell-based systems as well as demonstrating in vivo efficacy by modulating metabolic target genes such as FGF21 and G6Pase in liver tissue (Wang et al., 2010). In multiple studies, a single 10 mg/kg intraperitoneal (i.p.) dose of SR1078 in mice produced measurable plasma exposures in the low micromolar range—specifically approximately 3.6 μM at 1 hour post-dose—indicating that the compound has appreciable systemic bioavailability when administered in vivo (Cyr, Bronner, & Crawford, 2016; Wang et al., 2010). Despite these promising pharmacokinetic data in non-ocular models, there are no registered clinical trials or preclinical studies in dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) or similar retinal degenerative diseases on public repositories such as ClinicalTrials.gov (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.); indeed, the literature does not document any previous therapeutic use of SR1078 for treating dry AMD or analogous retinal conditions (Wang et al., 2010). Moreover, while several studies have harnessed SR1078 to explore ROR-dependent transcriptional mechanisms in liver and immune cells, the compound’s utility in ocular pathologies has not been established, which means that its repurposing for AMD remains a novel and uncharted application at this time (Solt & Burris, 2012; Cyr, Bronner, & Crawford, 2016).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, SR1078 exerts its pharmacological effects by binding directly to the ligand binding domains (LBDs) of the retinoic acid receptor–related orphan receptors, with particular emphasis on RORα. Binding of SR1078 induces a conformational change in the receptor that leads to stabilization of helix 12 (H12), a structural event that is critical for the recruitment of LXXLL-containing coactivator proteins (Wang et al., 2010). This ligand-dependent stabilization promotes enhanced transcription of canonical ROR target genes including metabolic regulators such as fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) and glucose-6-phosphatase (G6Pase), as demonstrated in liver-based assays in HepG2 cells and in vivo in murine models (Wang et al., 2010). Additionally, in immunological contexts, RORα activation by SR1078 has been associated with shifts in macrophage polarization toward an M2 phenotype via upregulation of the Krüppel-like factor 4 (KLF4) gene, showcasing the versatility of RORα signaling in modulating inflammatory states (Matsuoka & Michihara, 2021). Despite these well-characterized mechanisms, it must be noted that the current literature does not provide any direct evidence that SR1078 modulates the transcription of phagocytic receptors—particularly S1PR5—in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) or that it enhances the phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) (Cyr, Bronner, & Crawford, 2016; Solt & Burris, 2012). The hypothesis under consideration extends the established mechanism of SR1078’s action into a novel pathway linked to RPE physiology by proposing that SR1078 activation of RORα will lead to upregulation of S1PR5, a receptor that is implicated in phagosome maturation and may offer MERTK-independent enhancement of POS uptake and trafficking (Buniello et al., 2025). However, while SR1078 is well characterized for its ROR-dependent transcriptional effects in other cell types, detailed biochemical data connecting its activity to the expression of S1PR5 in RPE remain absent in the current literature corpus (Cyr, Bronner, & Crawford, 2016; Solt & Burris, 2012). Furthermore, studies investigating RORα in retinal contexts have primarily focused on inflammatory and angiogenic processes, with limited discussion of phagocytic receptor dynamics and POS handling (Buniello et al., 2025; Lew, Mazzoni, & Finnemann, 2020).

Expected Effect:  
The central hypothesis for repurposing SR1078 for dry AMD is that activation of RORα in RPE cells by SR1078 will drive transcriptional upregulation of S1PR5, thereby enhancing the availability or function of phagocytic receptors required for the efficient uptake, trafficking, and eventual degradation of photoreceptor outer segments (POS). This is especially relevant for MERTK-mutant iPSC-RPE models, where defective MERTK-dependent phagocytosis has been well-documented as a key pathological mechanism leading to accumulations of POS debris and subsequent retinal degeneration (Ramsden et al., 2017; Lukovic et al., 2015). In these models, MERTK deficiency abolishes the normal internalization of POS, leading to functional deficits in the RPE (Ramsden et al., 2017). The expected therapeutic effect of SR1078, as posited, would be the restoration of phagocytic capacity through a bypass mechanism; by activating RORα, it is anticipated that SR1078 may upregulate S1PR5 expression, which in turn is hypothesized to support phagosome maturation and a pathway of POS uptake that does not solely rely on MERTK signaling (Buniello et al., 2025). This effect is bolstered by mechanistic reasoning suggesting that S1P receptor signaling plays a role in phagosome dynamics, as demonstrated in studies highlighting sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) mediated regulatory pathways in the RPE (Lee et al., 2025); however, it is important to emphasize that these studies have primarily implicated S1PR3 rather than S1PR5, and the evidence for S1PR5’s involvement in RPE phagocytosis is comparatively limited (Buniello et al., 2025). Additionally, a follow-up reference (Unknown Reference) has been mentioned in internal discussions to indicate that SR1078 induces S1PR5 transcription in RPE, although this publication is not directly available in the current context. Therefore, if the hypothesis holds true, treatment with SR1078 would be expected to mitigate the phagocytic deficits observed in MERTK-mutant iPSC-RPE by increasing the transcriptional output of S1PR5 and possibly other RORα-dependent genes linked to membrane trafficking and lipid handling, thereby normalizing POS processing and ultimately slowing the progression of dry AMD (Buniello et al., 2025). However, any such expected effect remains largely hypothetical at this stage, given the absence of direct experimental data linking SR1078-mediated RORα activation to enhanced POS uptake in RPE cells (Cyr, Bronner, & Crawford, 2016; Solt & Burris, 2012).

Overall Evaluation:  
In evaluating SR1078 as a therapeutic candidate for the treatment of dry age-related macular degeneration, several strengths and weaknesses need to be considered. One of the major strengths is that SR1078 is a well-characterized synthetic agonist with robust biochemical evidence supporting its ability to modulate RORα activity. Its pharmacological profile includes demonstrable in vivo exposure, with measurable plasma concentrations following i.p. dosing, and it has been shown to activate canonical ROR target genes in hepatic and immune cell contexts (Cyr, Bronner, & Crawford, 2016; Wang et al., 2010). Its established use as a chemical biology tool provides a solid foundation regarding its mechanism of action, including the ligand-induced stabilization of helix 12 within the receptor’s ligand binding domain, a critical determinant for coactivator recruitment and transcriptional activation (Wang et al., 2010). Moreover, SR1078’s potential brain penetrance is cited in internal discussions, which could be advantageous for targeting tissues behind biological barriers such as the blood–retina barrier, although detailed ocular pharmacokinetic data are not available in the current literature (Wang et al., 2010). This systemic exposure and preclinical PK profile may allow for eventual ocular delivery if its safety and efficacy in retinal cells can be established.

Despite these strengths, there are notable weaknesses and gaps in the evidence supporting the repurposing of SR1078 for dry AMD. First, while SR1078 is a proven RORα agonist in other tissue systems, there is no direct experimental data demonstrating that RORα activation by SR1078 in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) leads to upregulation of S1PR5 or enhancement of photoreceptor outer segment (POS) uptake. The proposed mechanism—that RORα activation will upregulate S1PR5, which in turn supports phagosome maturation and MERTK-independent POS uptake—is conceptually plausible, but it remains speculative in the absence of specific studies connecting these dots (Cyr, Bronner, & Crawford, 2016; Solt & Burris, 2012). In fact, available open-target data suggest that while RORA (and by extension, its modulators) has some animal-model support in retinal diseases, both RORA and S1PR5 display minimal recorded evidence linking them directly to age-related macular degeneration (Buniello et al., 2025).

Another concern is the potential for adverse effects. One excerpt has indicated that activation of RORα by SR1078 in certain cellular contexts can result in elevated p53 levels and apoptosis (Solt & Burris, 2012). Such pro-apoptotic effects in RPE cells would be counterproductive in any therapy aimed at rescuing or preserving photoreceptor function, since the health of the RPE is critical for maintaining retinal integrity (Solt & Burris, 2012).

Furthermore, while several preclinical studies document the efficacy of SR1078 in modulating metabolic and immune pathways in liver and macrophage models (Wang et al., 2010; Matsuoka & Michihara, 2021), none of these studies have extended their investigations into RPE cell biology or directly measured outcomes such as POS phagocytosis. Models of MERTK-mutant iPSC-RPE have established that defects in phagocytosis lead to the accumulation of POS debris, an established pathological hallmark in retinal degeneration (Lukovic et al., 2015). Given that alternative strategies—such as translational readthrough inducing drugs (TRIDs) and AAV-mediated gene therapies—are already in development to address MERTK deficiency (Ramsden et al., 2017; Lukovic et al., 2015), the rationale for pursuing a strategy based on RORα agonism hinges critically on demonstrating that SR1078 can effect a meaningful rescue of phagocytic function in these specialized cellular models. At present, the literature does not provide such evidence; thus, the anticipated beneficial effect in the RPE remains hypothetical.

Moreover, the clinical and translational track record of drugs targeting nuclear receptors is mixed. While some compounds in this class have advanced to clinical use in metabolic and inflammatory disorders, many have faced issues related to off-target effects or an unfavorable safety profile due to the broad physiological roles of nuclear receptors (Solt & Burris, 2012). In the specific case of SR1078, the absence of any clinical trial data for retinal indications—as evidenced by a negative search on ClinicalTrials.gov—further underscores the need for caution as we consider its potential repurposing for dry AMD (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.).

On the other hand, the conceptual framework behind the hypothesis—that modulating alternative phagocytic pathways might bypass MERTK-dependent defects—is compelling. MERTK mutations are well documented in models of retinitis pigmentosa and have been linked to defective POS internalization in RPE cells (Ramsden et al., 2017; Lukovic et al., 2015). If SR1078 can indeed stimulate an alternative route for POS clearance via upregulation of S1PR5, this could represent a novel therapeutic angle for a subset of dry AMD patients whose disease is driven by phagocytic insufficiency. S1PR5 has been implicated in phagosome maturation, and although its expression levels in RPE are reported to be low (Buniello et al., 2025), even a modest upregulation might yield functional benefits in the context of otherwise compromised clearance mechanisms. Nevertheless, the primary gap remains the absence of direct evidence; key experiments—such as validating S1PR5 upregulation upon SR1078 treatment in iPSC-derived RPE, demonstrating enhancements in POS uptake in MERTK-mutant models, and confirming the lack of deleterious pro-apoptotic effects in retinal cells—are urgently needed before this approach can be considered promising for clinical translation (Buniello et al., 2025; Cyr, Bronner, & Crawford, 2016).

In summary, SR1078 emerges as an intriguing candidate for repurposing in dry AMD due to its well-established mechanism as a RORα agonist, its favorable pharmacokinetic exposure in preclinical models, and its use as a robust tool compound in nuclear receptor biology. Its potential to influence cellular metabolism and inflammatory responses might theoretically be harnessed to modulate alternative pathways of phagocytosis in RPE, particularly in cases where MERTK mutations compromise the canonical clearance of photoreceptor outer segments. However, significant caveats abound. The direct relationship between RORα activation by SR1078 and the upregulation of S1PR5 in RPE is not substantiated by existing literature, and the evidentiary link connecting this pathway to enhanced photoreceptor outer segment processing remains highly speculative (Buniello et al., 2025; Cyr, Bronner, & Crawford, 2016). Furthermore, safety concerns—in particular, reports of increased p53 levels and apoptosis in certain cellular contexts following SR1078 treatment—must be thoroughly evaluated in retinal models to ensure that any modulation of RORα does not inadvertently exacerbate retinal degeneration (Solt & Burris, 2012).

Overall, while the repurposing of SR1078 as a potential therapeutic for dry AMD represents an innovative approach by attempting to bypass MERTK defects through alternate phagocytic mechanisms, the current preclinical evidence is insufficient to definitively endorse its future development in this indication. Before proceeding further, targeted experiments must be designed to:  
1. Validate that RORα is expressed and functionally active in RPE cells at levels that would permit SR1078-mediated modulation,  
2. Confirm that treatment with SR1078 leads to a significant upregulation of S1PR5 transcription and/or protein expression in RPE,  
3. Demonstrate that such upregulation translates into improved photoreceptor outer segment uptake and processing in both standard and MERTK-mutant iPSC-RPE models, and  
4. Conduct detailed safety and ocular pharmacokinetic studies to rule out adverse pro-apoptotic or off-target effects in the retina.

Until such data are available, the overall utility of SR1078 for dry AMD remains an interesting hypothesis worthy of further investigation but, at present, cannot be considered a validated therapeutic candidate for clinical development. The strengths of SR1078 lie in its well-defined nuclear receptor agonism and its established use as a mechanistic probe in other tissues; its weaknesses include the lack of direct evidence for a role in RPE phagocytosis, potential safety liabilities related to apoptosis, and a sparse clinical history in ocular applications (Cyr, Bronner, & Crawford, 2016; Solt & Burris, 2012). Future research should prioritize generating in vitro and in vivo data in relevant retinal models to establish a clearer connection between RORα activation and improved POS phagocytic function. Only with such a foundational body of evidence can the repurposing of SR1078 for dry AMD be confidently pursued as a novel therapeutic strategy.
