Proposal for Pyrithione Zinc (Zinc ionophore; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. P1030)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Pyrithione Zinc (ZnPT; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. P1030) is a synthetic complex formed between zinc ions (Zn²⁺) and pyrithione, a sulfur- and oxygen-containing heterocycle that acts as a chelating and transporting agent. This compound belongs to the class of zinc ionophores, small molecules that facilitate the transport of Zn²⁺ across biological membranes and have been extensively studied for their capacity to modulate intracellular zinc homeostasis (Kavanagh et al., 2021; Lamore, 2012). Historically, pyrithione zinc was originally discovered and synthesized to exploit its broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties; it is most notably employed in over-the-counter formulations for dermatological conditions such as dandruff and psoriasis owing to its microbicidal effects (Lamore, 2012; Kavanagh et al., 2021). Its ability to form a stable 1:1 complex in aqueous solutions confers upon it the capacity to bind and transport Zn²⁺ in a controlled manner, which is critically exploited in various therapeutic contexts. The prevailing interest in zinc ionophores, including pyrithione zinc, stems from their reported ability to influence cellular processes by correcting aberrant zinc homeostasis, a property that is increasingly being evaluated in non-dermatological diseases. In the context of ophthalmology, particularly in dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD), the potential to reacidify lysosomes via the effective delivery of Zn²⁺ positions pyrithione zinc as a promising repurposed therapeutic candidate (Choi et al., 2024).

The synthetic origins of pyrithione zinc involve standard coordination chemistry techniques where pyrithione is combined with a divalent zinc salt to yield a salt that displays both lipophilicity and membrane permeability. This molecular design allows the compound not only to bind Zn²⁺ but also to act as a shuttle, delivering zinc to compartments such as lysosomes in cells. This reacidification of lysosomes is critical for rescuing enzymatic activities that are impaired due to aging or stress-induced damage, a central pathological hallmark in dry AMD. In previous studies evaluating zinc ionophores such as clioquinol or 1H10, similar mechanisms of lysosomal reacidification and restoration of autophagic processes have been documented—findings that support the conjecture that pyrithione zinc could act via comparable mechanisms (Choi et al., 2024). Moreover, the chemical flexibility of pyrithione zinc allows for precise formulation adjustments to fine-tune its therapeutic window, a key attribute in repurposing efforts where dose-dependent efficacy versus toxicity is of paramount concern (Kavanagh et al., 2021).

Therapeutic History:  
Pyrithione zinc has a well-established therapeutic history in dermatology, where its antimicrobial action underpins its use in shampoos and creams for treating dandruff and psoriasis. Its safety profile in topical applications has been documented in numerous clinical and preclinical studies. Despite its longstanding use in dermatological applications, the repurposing of pyrithione zinc for conditions such as dry AMD represents a novel approach, one that leverages its zinc ionophore properties rather than its antimicrobial activity (Lamore, 2012; Kavanagh et al., 2021). While direct clinical trials in retinal diseases have not been reported for pyrithione zinc, the concept of utilizing zinc ionophores to restore lysosomal function is supported indirectly by a body of work documenting similar outcomes in ARPE-19 cell models treated with other zinc ionophores like clioquinol and 1H10 (Choi et al., 2024).

In preclinical studies addressing dry AMD pathology, zinc supplementation has been shown to rescue lysosomal acidification in ARPE-19 cells challenged with A2E, a bisretinoid known to impair lysosomal function and promote cell death. In these models, compounds like clioquinol and analogous zinc ionophores facilitated the restoration of an acidic lysosomal pH, enhanced autophagic flux, and promoted the degradation of toxic lipid-protein aggregates (Choi et al., 2024). Although these investigations did not employ pyrithione zinc specifically, they help establish a mechanistic precedent under which ionophore-mediated zinc delivery can alleviate degenerative stress associated with dry AMD. Conversely, certain studies using pyrithione derivatives have raised concerns about cytotoxicity when the compound is combined with excess zinc, with observations of reduced cell viability at relatively low concentrations in non-retinal cell models (Kavanagh et al., 2021). Such findings underscore the importance of a rigorous evaluation of dosing and formulation in the context of retinal therapies to avoid off-target toxicity while harnessing the beneficial effects of lysosomal reacidification.

Mechanism of Action:  
The principal mechanism by which pyrithione zinc is hypothesized to exert its therapeutic effects in dry AMD involves the targeted delivery of Zn²⁺ into lysosomal compartments within retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells. At a molecular level, pyrithione acts as an ionophore by forming a coordination complex with Zn²⁺; this complex transiently masks the ionic charges of zinc, thereby facilitating its diffusion across the hydrophobic lipid bilayer of the plasma membrane and organellar membranes (Kavanagh et al., 2021; Lamore, 2012). Once the Zn²⁺-pyrithione complex enters the intracellular environment, the local pH, as well as other competing ligands, promote the dissociation of the complex, releasing free Zn²⁺ where it is needed most.

In the context of A2E-laden ARPE-19 cells, the accumulation of A2E has been well documented to induce lysosomal alkalinization, thereby impairing the optimal activity of lysosomal hydrolases such as cathepsin D and L (Choi et al., 2024). These proteolytic enzymes require an acidic environment to maintain their conformational integrity and catalytic efficiency. By delivering Zn²⁺ into lysosomes, pyrithione zinc is expected to assist in the restoration of lysosomal acidity. This reacidification is pivotal because the acidic pH not only reactivates cathepsin enzymes but also supports the overall lysosomal degradative capacity, including the breakdown of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) and A2E itself (Choi et al., 2024).

The biochemical interplay involves zinc acting as a cofactor in several enzymatic and regulatory processes that influence lysosomal function. For example, there is evidence suggesting that intracellular zinc modulates the activity of proton pumps such as the vacuolar-type H⁺-ATPase, which are responsible for maintaining lysosomal acidity by actively pumping protons into the lysosome (Choi et al., 2024). Restoration of acidic pH results in the activation of cathepsin D and L, key proteases that digest accumulated debris generated from mechanisms such as photoreceptor outer segment phagocytosis. Moreover, proper lysosomal function facilitates autophagic flux, an essential cellular process for clearing damaged organelles and aggregated proteins that contribute to AMD pathology (Choi et al., 2024).

At the same time, it is important to acknowledge that in addition to its beneficial zinc-transport properties, pyrithione zinc has also been demonstrated to activate various stress-signaling cascades. For instance, some studies have reported that the perturbation of zinc homeostasis by pyrithione-based compounds can lead to the induction of apoptotic signaling and cellular stress responses mediated by pathways such as NF-kappaB inhibition (Lamore, 2012). While the activation of such pathways may offer a potential mechanism for eliminating severely damaged cells, it also raises the specter of cytotoxicity if the ionophore is not carefully dosed (Kavanagh et al., 2021). Thus, the molecular actions of pyrithione zinc will necessitate a delicate balance—ensuring that sufficient Zn²⁺ is delivered to restore lysosomal acidification and enzyme activity without triggering deleterious cell stress responses. This dual action underscores the importance of mechanistic studies to delineate the precise intracellular dynamics of zinc delivery and to characterize the downstream effects on signaling pathways in RPE cells.

Furthermore, the release of Zn²⁺ in the lysosomal lumen is hypothesized to enhance not only cathepsin-mediated proteolysis but also to promote a cascade of biochemical events that result in improved phagocytosis of POS. Lysosomal reacidification directly impacts the activity of other lysosomal constituents such as lipid hydrolases and enables the proper degradation of lipofuscin components, a hallmark of dry AMD pathology (Choi et al., 2024). Experimental observations have shown that correcting the pH imbalance in lysosomes leads to a reduction in A2E accumulation, which in turn improves cellular viability and function in ARPE-19 cells—a model that recapitulates many of the features of dry AMD (Choi et al., 2024). 

Expected Effect:  
In the proposed assay, the application of pyrithione zinc at a concentration of 5 µM is expected to deliver Zn²⁺ efficiently into the lysosomal compartments of A2E-laden ARPE-19 cells, thereby reacidifying them and restoring the activity of critical proteolytic enzymes such as cathepsin D and L. These enzymes have a well-established role in the degradation of photoreceptor outer segments, and their activation is integral to maintaining cellular homeostasis in RPE cells (Choi et al., 2024; Sharif, 2015). By normalizing lysosomal pH, it is anticipated that pyrithione zinc will enhance the degradative capacity of these lysosomes, thereby reducing the accumulation of toxic aggregates such as A2E that contribute to cell death and the progression of dry AMD (Choi et al., 2024).

The rationale behind this expected effect is grounded in previous studies where zinc ionophores, such as clioquinol and 1H10, were shown to mitigate A2E-induced lysosomal alkalinization and improve autophagic flux in ARPE-19 cells. In these studies, restoration of lysosomal acidity was directly correlated with improved cathepsin D/L activities and enhanced clearance of photoreceptor outer segment debris (Choi et al., 2024). Extrapolating from these findings, the delivery of Zn²⁺ via pyrithione zinc is hypothesized to replicate a similar biological response wherein the reactivated lysosomal enzymes restore the degradative processes essential for the maintenance of retinal health.

It is important to note that the concentration of 5 µM is selected based on the hypothesis that it will be sufficient to deliver therapeutic levels of Zn²⁺ into the lysosomes without exceeding the threshold at which pyrithione zinc’s cytotoxic effects become pronounced. Prior reports have highlighted that pyrithione, particularly when combined with additional zinc, can induce significant cytotoxicity at relatively low concentrations (Kavanagh et al., 2021). However, the working hypothesis posits that at 5 µM, a careful balance can be achieved wherein lysosomal acidification is restored while maintaining cellular viability. Should the lysosomal pH shift toward the optimal acidic range—as observed through increased fluorescence intensity with pH-sensitive dyes such as pHrodo™ Red AM—the activation of cathepsin D and L and the consequent enhancement in POS degradation will serve as robust indicators of therapeutic efficacy (Choi et al., 2024).

Moreover, this restoration of lysosomal function is expected to have downstream benefits beyond the simple clearance of A2E. Enhanced lysosomal acidification can re-establish the efficient interplay between phagocytosis and autophagy; in ARPE-19 cells, this dual process is critical for the continual renewal of cellular components and the prevention of cellular stress associated with the accumulation of photoreceptor debris (Choi et al., 2024). Given that A2E accumulation is known to impair these cellular clearance mechanisms and contribute to the pathogenesis of dry AMD, the expected effect of pyrithione zinc in reactivating these pathways could translate into improved RPE cell survival and functionality. Thus, the mechanistic action of lysosomal reacidification not only addresses the biochemical defect inherent in A2E-induced toxicity but also re-establishes the physiological processes necessary for retinal homeostasis.

Overall Evaluation:  
The overall evaluation of pyrithione zinc as a potential therapeutic candidate for dry AMD is complex, involving a balance between its promising mechanistic advantages and the potential liabilities related to cytotoxicity. Among its strengths is the underlying rationale based on its established function as a zinc ionophore capable of delivering Zn²⁺ to lysosomal compartments—a strategy that has been shown in similar studies using alternative zinc ionophores (Choi et al., 2024). The preclinical evidence from ARPE-19 cell models illustrates that zinc-mediated lysosomal reacidification can rescue cathepsin activity, reduce A2E accumulation, and enhance autophagic flux—all of which are critical in mitigating the deleterious effects observed in dry AMD (Choi et al., 2024). Additionally, the fact that pyrithione zinc is already in use for dermatological applications suggests that a wealth of formulation, stability, and safety data is available from non-ocular contexts, which may expedite some aspects of drug development if repurposing proves feasible (Lamore, 2012; Kavanagh et al., 2021).

From a mechanistic perspective, the targeted delivery of Zn²⁺ to lysosomes addresses a central pathology in dry AMD—lysosomal alkalinization resulting from A2E accumulation. By reacidifying lysosomes, pyrithione zinc is expected to restore the activity of critical lysosomal enzymes such as cathepsin D and L, thereby enhancing the degradative clearance of photoreceptor debris (Choi et al., 2024; Sharif, 2015). This direct targeting of the lysosomal pH defect represents a highly rational strategy for addressing dry AMD at its biochemical root. Furthermore, the intricate coordination of zinc ion binding and subsequent release in the intracellular environment has the potential to synergize with other cellular processes, including the induction of autophagy and the restoration of phagocytic functions, that are disrupted in AMD (Choi et al., 2024).

Despite these compelling strengths, significant challenges remain. Chief among these is the reported cytotoxicity associated with pyrithione zinc when used at concentrations that may exceed safe thresholds in certain cellular contexts (Lamore, 2012). Although the proposed concentration of 5 µM is based on the hypothesis that beneficial zinc delivery can be achieved without inducing deleterious cell stress, prior evidence indicates that pyrithione zinc can elicit cellular stress signaling and apoptosis under suboptimal dosing conditions (Kavanagh et al., 2021). This necessitates thorough dose-response studies and careful titration of the compound in RPE cell assays to ensure that therapeutic benefits are not negated by adverse side effects. Moreover, the potential for off-target effects—such as the inadvertent modulation of NF-kappaB or other signaling pathways that could lead to unanticipated outcomes—must be rigorously investigated (Lamore, 2012).

A further weakness in the candidate’s current profile is the paucity of direct evidence linking pyrithione zinc to improved outcomes in retinal models. Although the bulk of the supporting literature centers on other zinc ionophores like clioquinol and 1H10, the specific cellular responses and safety profile of pyrithione zinc in ARPE-19 or primary RPE cells remain less well characterized (Choi et al., 2024). Consequently, additional preclinical studies—including both in vitro and in vivo evaluations—are necessary to validate the utility of pyrithione zinc in the context of dry AMD.

Opportunities for further research include a detailed evaluation of the compound’s pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics in retinal tissue models, as well as the establishment of a robust therapeutic window that delineates efficacy from toxicity. Advanced imaging and pH-sensitive assays should be utilized to confirm the extent of lysosomal reacidification in ARPE-19 cells following treatment, and the restoration of cathepsin D/L activity should be quantified with precision. Moreover, potential strategies to mitigate the cytotoxic effects—such as modifications to the compound’s molecular structure or the use of combination therapies with antioxidants—could further enhance the safety profile of pyrithione zinc (Kavanagh et al., 2021).

Overall, while pyrithione zinc presents as a promising candidate owing to its theoretical ability to deliver Zn²⁺ intracellularly and restore lysosomal function, its successful repurposing for the treatment of dry AMD hinges on addressing several critical gaps in knowledge. These include a more nuanced characterization of its cytotoxicity profile in RPE models, confirmation of its efficacy in reacidifying lysosomes within the relevant cellular context, and a demonstration that its beneficial effects outweigh potential off-target actions. The current body of evidence, drawn largely from studies focusing on alternative zinc ionophores, suggests that targeting lysosomal pH is a viable strategy for alleviating AMD pathology (Choi et al., 2024). However, given the mixed reports on pyrithione zinc’s cytotoxicity in non-retinal cell lines (Kavanagh et al., 2021), a judicious and methodical approach to preclinical evaluation is warranted.

In summary, the strengths of pyrithione zinc as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD lie in its well-understood chemistry as a zinc ionophore, its capacity to reacidify lysosomes and thereby restore crucial degradative enzyme activities, and its established use in other clinical contexts that might facilitate repurposing. Its mechanism of action, which involves precisely modulating intracellular Zn²⁺ levels to correct lysosomal dysfunction, addresses the central biochemical defect observed in RPE cells burdened by A2E accumulation (Choi et al., 2024). Nevertheless, the weaknesses—primarily concerning the potential for cytotoxicity and the limited direct evidence in retinal disease models—underscore the need for extensive further investigation. Identifying the optimal dosing regimen, verifying the specificity of Zn²⁺ delivery to lysosomes, and elucidating any secondary effects on signaling pathways will be pivotal in determining whether pyrithione zinc can ultimately be repurposed as an effective therapeutic for dry AMD.
