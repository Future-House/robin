Proposal for Anti-HtrA1 monoclonal antibody (mAb; Novartis investigational)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
The anti‐HtrA1 monoclonal antibody under evaluation is a biologic therapeutic candidate developed through advanced recombinant antibody engineering, and its design is predicated on targeting the extracellular serine protease HtrA1, which has been implicated in the pathology of age‐related macular degeneration (AMD). This candidate originates from rational immunization and humanization processes whereby immunized mice (or phage‐display derived libraries) were used to identify and optimize clones that inhibit HtrA1 with high affinity (Kd < 10 nM), thereby ensuring selective binding to its proteolytic domain. The antibody belongs to the class of therapeutic monoclonal antibodies, a group of compounds that have been widely deployed in various inflammatory, oncologic, and degenerative disease states owing to their specificity and ability to modulate cellular microenvironments by neutralizing key mediators; indeed, similar agents have been successfully used to modulate extracellular protease activities in other disorders (Tom et al., 2020). The candidate’s design specifically aims to neutralize excess extracellular HtrA1 activity, thereby preserving the integrity of cell surface receptors such as integrin and CD36 on retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells. Given that the degradation of these receptors impairs the recognition, binding, and subsequent receptor-mediated uptake of photoreceptor outer segments (POS), the antibody is intended to maintain normal phagocytic function in RPE through its targeted blockade of pathological HtrA1 activity (Oura et al., 2018; Tom et al., 2020; Dere et al., 2021). The investigational product, reportedly in clinical evaluation by Novartis in the context of dry AMD and exemplified by its progression into Phase II trial design under the GALLEGO study, exemplifies the translational potential of antibody-based inhibition strategies in ocular degenerative conditions (Asahi et al., 2021).

Therapeutic History:  
Anti‐HtrA1 monoclonal antibodies represent a novel therapeutic modality with a unique mechanism relative to classic anti–vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) agents. Preclinical investigations have provided compelling evidence that aberrant HtrA1 expression contributes to the pathogenesis of AMD through multiple interrelated processes including extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, induction of photoreceptor apoptosis, and interference with receptor function on RPE cells (Oura et al., 2018). Notably, animal models – including zebrafish systems – have demonstrated that HtrA1 overexpression in rod photoreceptors induces retinal morphological changes that recapitulate early AMD features, such as lipofuscin accumulation and photoreceptor structural aberrations; pharmacological inhibition of HtrA1 in these models led to partial rescue of the apoptotic phenotype, thereby validating HtrA1 as a therapeutic target (Oura et al., 2018). In parallel, other preclinical studies have shown that anti‐HtrA1 antibodies, such as the antigen-binding fragment FHTR2163, effectively inhibit HtrA1’s protease activity in in vitro cleavage assays with substrates such as Dickkopf-related protein 3 (DKK3); this observation was further corroborated through in vivo studies in cynomolgus monkeys and rabbits, where intravitreal administration resulted in dose-dependent inhibition of DKK3 cleavage, a phenomenon that served as a robust pharmacodynamic marker (Tom et al., 2020; Khanani et al., 2021). Clinical evaluation of anti-HtrA1 therapy for geographic atrophy (GA), the advanced stage of dry AMD, began with Phase I studies that demonstrated acceptable ocular safety, manageable adverse event profiles, and evidence of target engagement using aqueous humor biomarker assessments (Khanani et al., 2021). However, it is also important to note that emerging literature has raised potential concerns; for example, some studies have reported that in donors with homozygous AMD risk alleles at the chromosome 10q26 locus, HTRA1 mRNA and protein levels in RPE tissue are reduced relative to non-risk donors, suggesting that—at least in some patient subgroups—HtrA1 may be protective rather than purely pathogenic (Williams et al., 2021). Consequently, the therapeutic history of anti-HtrA1 agents for AMD is marked by both promising preclinical and early-phase clinical safety data as well as unresolved controversies regarding patient stratification and the precise role of HtrA1 in disease progression (Tom et al., 2020; Williams et al., 2021).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, HtrA1 is a multifunctional serine protease that is secreted into the extracellular space and possesses the capability to degrade multiple ECM proteins and regulatory molecules that are critical for maintaining retinal homeostasis. Its proteolytic activity has been implicated in cleaving latent transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) binding proteins, such as latent TGF-β binding protein-1 (LTBP-1), which in turn facilitates dysregulated TGF-β signaling and promotes pro-apoptotic pathways through downstream effectors such as AKT and FOXO (Oura et al., 2018). Additionally, HtrA1’s substrates include proteins involved in the structural integrity of Bruch’s membrane, such as fibronectin, elastin, and other ECM components, and emerging data also suggest that it may cleave cell surface receptors like CD36, thereby directly impairing RPE function in the uptake of lipid-rich photoreceptor outer segments (He et al., 2022; Oura et al., 2018).  
The therapeutic candidate under discussion has been engineered to bind with high affinity to the catalytic domain of HtrA1, forming a “cage-like” inhibition complex that sterically hinders substrate access and completely abrogates its protease activity (Tom et al., 2020). This binding not only prevents the deleterious cleavage of ECM components but also protects important cell surface receptors, including integrin and CD36, from enzymatic degradation. Integrin receptors have been implicated in maintaining the proper adhesion and signaling functions of the RPE, while CD36 plays a crucial role in the recognition and phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments; preserving these receptors is therefore essential to sustaining the normal turnover of POS and preventing photoreceptor degeneration (Oura et al., 2018; Tom et al., 2020). In vitro studies have confirmed that this antibody can effectively inhibit the proteolytic cleavage of DKK3—a known substrate of HtrA1—thus providing a direct measure of target engagement and serving as an important pharmacodynamic biomarker in early clinical assessments (Tom et al., 2020). Moreover, biochemical analyses indicate that the antibody’s binding affinity (with Kd values well below 10 nM) is sufficient to compete with endogenous substrates, ensuring that even low levels of HtrA1 activity are neutralized (Tom et al., 2020). This detailed molecular mode of action underpins the rationale for expecting that inhibition of HtrA1 will restore receptor integrity on RPE cells, thereby normalizing receptor-mediated POS binding and uptake (Oura et al., 2018; Tom et al., 2020).

Expected Effect:  
Based on its mechanism of action, it is anticipated that administration of the anti-HtrA1 monoclonal antibody will result in a cascade of protective effects within the retinal microenvironment that are critical for slowing the progression of dry AMD. The foremost expected outcome is a robust inhibition of HtrA1’s protease activity, as evidenced by a reduction in the proteolytic fragments of key substrates such as DKK3 in the aqueous humor. This reduced substrate cleavage is expected to correlate with enhanced preservation of the structural integrity of extracellular matrix components in Bruch’s membrane and with the maintenance of the cell surface integrity of RPE receptors such as integrin and CD36 (Tom et al., 2020). In preclinical models, inhibition of HtrA1 has been shown to rescue photoreceptor cells from apoptosis and to ameliorate morphological features reminiscent of early AMD, such as lipofuscin accumulation and disorganized photoreceptor outer segments (Oura et al., 2018). The hypothesis underlying the proposed mechanism is that by preventing the degradation of these crucial receptors, the RPE will maintain its natural phagocytic efficiency—specifically, its ability to bind and internalize photoreceptor outer segments, a process essential for the renewal and proper function of the photoreceptor layer (Oura et al., 2018; He et al., 2022).  
In addition, it is expected that the antibody will indirectly modulate pro-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic signaling cascades. HtrA1-mediated ECM breakdown and receptor degradation have been implicated in the activation of inflammatory pathways, which can lead to aberrant VEGF expression and neovascular complications in AMD (Lu et al., 2019). Thus, by neutralizing HtrA1, the therapeutic candidate may reduce local inflammation and the secondary upregulation of angiogenic factors, contributing to an overall stabilization of retinal morphology and function. The preservation of integrin and CD36 receptor availability is particularly significant in this context because it ensures the RPE’s ability to perform its routine phagocytic clearance of POS debris, a process that, when compromised, is known to lead to the accumulation of toxic byproducts that exacerbate retinal degeneration (Oura et al., 2018; Tom et al., 2020). Finally, the candidate’s high binding affinity and favorable pharmacokinetic profile—highlighted in early-phase ocular safety studies where sustained inhibition of target protease activity was observed for several weeks post-intravitreal injection—support an anticipated clinical regimen that may allow for clinically acceptable dosing intervals while maintaining therapeutic efficacy (Khanani et al., 2021).

Overall Evaluation:  
In summary, the anti-HtrA1 monoclonal antibody candidate for dry AMD represents a promising therapeutic strategy, with its strengths rooted in a clear genetic, biochemical, and preclinical rationale. The candidate is supported by compelling evidence that HtrA1 overexpression contributes to RPE dysfunction and photoreceptor cell death via ECM remodeling, receptor cleavage, and dysregulated TGF-β signaling, all of which are critical hallmarks of AMD pathogenesis (Oura et al., 2018; Tom et al., 2020). Its high binding affinity (Kd below 10 nM) ensures potent and specific blockade of HtrA1’s proteolytic activity, and preclinical studies have repeatedly demonstrated that inhibition of HtrA1 can lead to measurable improvements in retinal structure and function, including the preservation of phagocytic receptor integrity on RPE cells (Tom et al., 2020). Early clinical studies have provided encouraging safety and pharmacodynamic data, including the demonstration of dose-dependent reductions in DKK3 cleavage in the ocular compartment, which serves as a reproducible biomarker of target engagement (Khanani et al., 2021).

However, the candidate also faces notable challenges and uncertainties that must be carefully considered as part of the overall development strategy. One significant concern arises from a body of literature which documents that in certain genetically predisposed populations—specifically, those carrying risk alleles at the chromosome 10q26 locus—the expression of HtrA1 in RPE tissues is paradoxically reduced, suggesting that HtrA1 may have a protective or homeostatic role in these contexts (Williams et al., 2021). This dichotomy raises the possibility that a “one-size-fits-all” inhibition strategy may not be appropriate for all patient subgroups, and that detailed molecular phenotyping or patient stratification might be necessary to identify those individuals in whom HtrA1 activity is pathologically elevated rather than diminished. In other words, while anti-HtrA1 inhibition appears beneficial in models characterized by HtrA1 overexpression, it could potentially be detrimental in patients with inherently low HtrA1 levels where further suppression might compromise baseline ECM maintenance and RPE health (Williams et al., 2021; Filliat, 2017).

Furthermore, while the mechanistic rationale involving receptor preservation (integrin and CD36) and the normalization of POS binding is sound and supported by preclinical observations, direct evidence demonstrating improved receptor-mediated phagocytosis in clinical settings remains limited. Although early-phase studies have shown effective neutralization of HtrA1 and corresponding reductions in DKK3 cleavage, it is yet to be definitively established whether these biochemical endpoints translate into meaningful functional improvements in RPE phagocytic capacity and overall retinal health. In addition, the broader roles of HtrA1 in processes such as inflammation and angiogenesis, while supportive of the therapeutic rationale, may also lead to off-target effects or unintended consequences that would need to be monitored in longer-term studies (Lu et al., 2019).

There is also the practical consideration of dosing and delivery. Intravitreal administration of an antibody fragment necessitates careful control over ocular pharmacokinetics, and although initial studies suggest a favorable profile with sustained target inhibition for several weeks after injection (Khanani et al., 2021), the potential for immunogenicity, ocular inflammation, or other local adverse effects remains an area that requires ongoing investigation. The preliminary Phase I data are reassuring regarding safety and tolerability; however, efficacy endpoints from larger, longer-term Phase II or Phase III trials will ultimately be needed to validate the clinical benefit of this approach. Moreover, while the current hypothesis specifically targets the preservation of integrin and CD36 receptor integrity, further studies are required to conclusively delineate the interplay between HtrA1 inhibition, receptor restoration, and the normalization of POS binding and uptake (Oura et al., 2018; Tom et al., 2020).

In conclusion, the anti-HtrA1 monoclonal antibody candidate represents one of the most promising repurposed therapeutic strategies for dry AMD currently under investigation. Its design is underpinned by robust scientific rationale and early-phase data that support its potential to mitigate key pathogenic processes—including ECM degradation, receptor cleavage, and inflammation—that are central to AMD progression (Tom et al., 2020; Khanani et al., 2021). The high binding affinity and demonstrated pharmacodynamic effects set a solid foundation for further development. Nonetheless, the conflicting evidence regarding HtrA1 expression in different patient subgroups, along with uncertainties about the direct impact on RPE function and clinical outcomes, highlight the need for rigorous patient selection and further mechanistic studies. Overall, while the candidate shows substantial promise, its ultimate success will depend on confirming that HtrA1 inhibition results in functional benefits for RPE phagocytosis and retinal integrity without adversely affecting the protective aspects of HtrA1 in certain populations (Williams et al., 2021; Tom et al., 2020). Future clinical trials with well-defined inclusion criteria and comprehensive biomarker assessments will be essential to resolve these controversies and to realize the full therapeutic potential of anti-HtrA1 monoclonal antibodies in the treatment of dry AMD.
