Proposal for Pyrrolidine dithiocarbamate (PDTC; NF-κB inhibitor; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. 239,804)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Pyrrolidine dithiocarbamate (PDTC) is a synthetic compound that belongs to the dithiocarbamate class, a group of molecules originally derived from the reaction of secondary or tertiary amines with carbon disulfide under alkaline conditions. PDTC is commercially available (for example, from Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. 239,804) and has been widely used in experimental research due to its multifunctional properties. Initially, dithiocarbamate compounds were recognized for their potent antioxidant capacities and metal chelation abilities; however, subsequent research identified PDTC as one of the most potent and specific inhibitors of nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), with its inhibitory action shown to be independent of its antioxidant effects (Morais et al., 2009; Shin et al., 2023). The compound’s structure, featuring both a pyrrolidine ring and dithiocarbamate moiety, imbues it with the ability to bind metal ions such as Zn²⁺ and modulate redox signaling pathways. In terms of therapeutic classification, PDTC is considered a research tool and a candidate for repurposing in inflammatory and neoplastic diseases, and its class has been investigated extensively over the past decades for applications in cancer therapy, inflammatory disorders, and experimental models of ocular disease (Kwak et al., 2008; Morais et al., 2009).

Therapeutic History:  
The dithiocarbamate class, and PDTC in particular, has a rich history in biomedical research. PDTC has been employed primarily as an experimental NF-κB inhibitor in various paradigms, ranging from cancer models to inflammatory diseases. In the realm of oncology, PDTC has been shown to induce cell death in multiple cancer cell lines including breast, colon, gastric, and prostate cancers by modulating pathways related to cell survival and apoptosis (Morais et al., 2009). Its ability to chelate metal ions and act as a radical scavenger has also been exploited in studies examining cytoprotective effects in liver injury and neurodegenerative conditions (Shin et al., 2023). Notably, PDTC has been studied in ocular models; for example, its use in experimental endotoxin-induced uveitis in Lewis rats provided evidence of its anti-inflammatory activity and ocular safety profile (Kanai et al., 2010). While there is no record of PDTC having been directly used in clinical trials for dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD), its application in related ocular inflammatory conditions, such as autoimmune anterior uveitis and retinal neovascularization, offers a promising translational bridge to dry AMD (Morais et al., 2009). In several studies of retinal diseases, PDTC has been shown to modulate angiogenic factors and inflammatory cytokine profiles, indicating its potential utility in conditions where inflammation plays a key pathogenic role (Morais et al., 2009; Shin et al., 2023). Its established use in experimental models, combined with evidence of a favorable safety profile in ocular applications, makes PDTC an attractive candidate for repurposing in dry AMD, especially given the emerging understanding of the role of chronic inflammation and NF-κB activation in this degenerative disease (Shin et al., 2023).

Mechanism of Action:  
PDTC’s therapeutic potential largely derives from its robust inhibition of NF-κB activation. Under typical conditions, NF-κB is sequestered in the cytoplasm by inhibitory proteins known as IκBs. Upon stimulation by pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α or exposure to oxidative stress, the IκB proteins are phosphorylated, ubiquitinated, and degraded, releasing NF-κB to translocate to the nucleus where it drives the expression of numerous genes involved in inflammatory responses, cell survival, and immune regulation (Morais et al., 2009; Shin et al., 2023). PDTC interferes with this pathway by preventing the phosphorylation and consequent degradation of IκB, thereby keeping NF-κB in its inactive, cytoplasmic state (Kwak et al., 2008). In addition to this canonical inhibition, PDTC is also known to function as a metal ion chelator and a free radical scavenger, and studies have demonstrated that its chelating capability is crucial to its mechanism (Shin et al., 2023). The compound’s ability to chelate zinc ions, which are essential cofactors for the DNA-binding activity of NF-κB, may further contribute to its inhibitory effects on NF-κB-mediated transcription. This property is particularly relevant in the context of retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) physiology; in the hypothesis being considered, PDTC is proposed to chelate Zn²⁺ in RPE cells, thereby impairing NF-κB activation. As NF-κB is known to downregulate genes such as MERTK—a receptor tyrosine kinase critical for the phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments (POS)—and lysosomal enzymes like cathepsin D, inhibition of NF-κB by PDTC would be expected to restore their expression (Morais et al., 2009). Moreover, beyond simple inhibition of NF-κB nuclear translocation, PDTC has been observed to induce the expression of endogenous antioxidants such as superoxide dismutase and heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) through NF-κB–independent pathways, suggesting that the compound can modulate multiple signaling cascades concurrently (Shin et al., 2023). Collectively, these molecular actions position PDTC as a multifaceted inhibitor of inflammatory signaling, where both the blockade of NF-κB translocation and the chelation of essential metal ions converge to mitigate inflammation-induced cellular dysfunction (Shin et al., 2023; Sui et al., 2020).

Expected Effect:  
In the proposed application for dry AMD, the hypothesis is that PDTC will chelate Zn²⁺ and thereby inhibit NF-κB activation in RPE cells. In dry AMD, chronic inflammation is believed to play a crucial role in the downregulation of key proteins involved in phagocytosis and lysosomal function, notably MERTK and cathepsin D. TNF-α—a pro-inflammatory cytokine that robustly activates the NF-κB pathway—is known to downregulate the expression of MERTK and lysosomal genes in ARPE-19 cells, a commonly used model of RPE function (Morais et al., 2009; Shin et al., 2023). By inhibiting NF-κB activation, PDTC is expected to reverse the repressive effects of TNF-α, thereby restoring the normal expression levels of MERTK and cathepsin D. This restoration is anticipated to improve the ingestion and processing of POS, a critical function of RPE cells that is compromised in dry AMD. Improved POS clearance would reduce the accumulation of cellular debris and lipofuscin, which are hallmarks of RPE dysfunction and contribute to disease progression (Morais et al., 2009; Shin et al., 2023). Additionally, PDTC’s capacity to inhibit NF-κB not only suppresses the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, IL-8, and GM-CSF, but may also promote a shift in the balance of macrophage polarization in the retinal microenvironment. For instance, a reduction in NF-κB–mediated inflammatory signaling has been linked to a decreased abundance of M1-type (pro-inflammatory) macrophages and a corresponding increase in M2-type (anti-inflammatory or tissue repair) macrophages, which would further support retinal homeostasis and repair mechanisms (Sui et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2005). Expression analyses in various studies have confirmed that RPE cells exhibit active NF-κB signaling under inflammatory stress and that inhibition of NF-κB can lead to upregulation of phagocytic receptors and lysosomal enzymes, which are essential for POS turnover (Morais et al., 2009; Shin et al., 2023). Thus, in the described assay using TNF-α–treated ARPE-19 cells, PDTC is expected to counteract the deleterious inflammatory signaling, normalizing the expression of MERTK and cathepsin D, and ultimately improving the functional capacity of the RPE to clear photoreceptor debris (Morais et al., 2009).

Overall Evaluation:  
The repurposing of PDTC for dry AMD represents a promising and innovative approach rooted in well-established molecular pharmacology. One of the major strengths of PDTC is its dual mode of action: it is a potent inhibitor of NF-κB, preventing the transcriptional activation of a wide array of pro-inflammatory and cell stress genes, and it also possesses metal chelation properties, which may further enhance its ability to disrupt the pathological signaling cascades in RPE cells (Morais et al., 2009; Shin et al., 2023). The experimental evidence from animal models of ocular inflammation—such as endotoxin-induced uveitis—demonstrates that PDTC can be administered in ocular contexts with a tolerable safety profile, thereby supporting the feasibility of translating these findings to the treatment of dry AMD (Kanai et al., 2010). From a biochemical perspective, the strategy of chelating Zn²⁺ to inhibit NF-κB activation is compelling, particularly in view of the fact that zinc ions are embedded in the structure of NF-κB that is essential for its binding to DNA. In RPE cells, which are subject to continuous oxidative and inflammatory stress in dry AMD, the ability of PDTC to sequester Zn²⁺ may provide a specific molecular intervention point. This intervention could reverse NF-κB–mediated downregulation of MERTK and cathepsin D, thereby improving POS uptake and degradation—a critical process that is disrupted in AMD and contributes to the accumulation of toxic debris (Morais et al., 2009; Shin et al., 2023).

However, there are weaknesses and potential challenges associated with this therapeutic strategy. Although preclinical models of uveitis and retinal neovascularization have shown PDTC’s efficacy in reducing inflammatory markers and preserving tissue structure, there is still a significant gap in direct evidence for its therapeutic benefit in dry AMD. The proposed mechanism—involving restoration of phagocytic receptor and lysosomal gene expression by NF-κB inhibition—has been observed in vitro in ARPE-19 cells, yet extensive in vivo validation in models that recapitulate the complex pathology of dry AMD is lacking (Morais et al., 2009; Shin et al., 2023). Moreover, the specificity of PDTC’s metal chelation in the retinal environment remains uncertain. While Zn²⁺ chelation is a plausible mechanism, other divalent metal ions are present in the retina, and off-target chelation could potentially disrupt essential metalloprotein functions. This necessitates further biochemical studies to dissect the selectivity of PDTC’s chelating action in the RPE (Shin et al., 2023; Morais et al., 2009).

Another potential concern is related to dosing and toxicity. Several studies have indicated that PDTC can exert cytotoxic effects in endothelial cells and other cell types when administered at high concentrations or prolonged exposure periods, even though its anti-inflammatory effects are observed at lower doses (Morais et al., 2009). While ocular models such as those in experimental uveitis have shown acceptable tolerability, the long-term implications of PDTC administration in the context of a chronic condition like dry AMD require careful evaluation. It will be essential to optimize dosing regimens that confer sufficient NF-κB inhibition without incurring cytotoxicity, particularly given that RPE cells are critical for retinal integrity and visual function (Kanai et al., 2010; Morais et al., 2009).

Furthermore, the multifactorial etiology of dry AMD—spanning oxidative stress, inflammation, and impaired lipid metabolism—raises the possibility that a single agent such as PDTC may not be sufficient as a standalone therapy. Combination approaches that integrate PDTC with agents targeting complementary pathways (for example, those modulating complement activation or enhancing endogenous antioxidant defenses) might be necessary to achieve a clinically meaningful impact (Morais et al., 2009; Shin et al., 2023). In addition, while PDTC has been robustly characterized as an NF-κB inhibitor, the literature suggests that its effects on other signaling pathways (such as those involving AP-1 or STAT family members) are less well defined. Unintended modulation of these additional pathways could potentially lead to undesirable effects that need to be accounted for in a comprehensive safety evaluation (Shin et al., 2023; Sui et al., 2020).

In conclusion, PDTC exhibits many attributes that support its consideration as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD. Its well-documented ability to inhibit NF-κB activation—combined with its metal chelation and antioxidant properties—addresses key pathological mechanisms underlying chronic inflammation and impaired phagocytic function in the RPE. Preclinical data from ocular inflammation models lend further credence to its potential translational application. Nevertheless, significant challenges remain, including the need for rigorous in vivo studies to verify restoration of MERTK and lysosomal gene expression, careful titration of dosing to avoid cytotoxicity, and detailed characterization of its chelation selectivity within the intricate retinal milieu. Future investigations should aim to clarify these mechanistic details and evaluate the long-term safety and efficacy of PDTC in relevant animal models of dry AMD before moving toward clinical trials. Overall, PDTC represents a promising candidate with a strong scientific rationale and a history of multifaceted use in experimental settings; however, a cautious and methodical progression through preclinical validation is warranted to fully assess its suitability for addressing the unmet therapeutic needs in dry AMD (Kwak et al., 2008; Kanai et al., 2010; Morais et al., 2009; Shin et al., 2023; Sui et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2005).
