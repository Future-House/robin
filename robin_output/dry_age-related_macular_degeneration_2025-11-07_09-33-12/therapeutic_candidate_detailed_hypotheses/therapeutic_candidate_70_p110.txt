Proposal for P110 (Drp1/Fis1 interaction inhibitor peptide; AnaSpec Cat. No. 77584)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
P110 is a synthetic, cell‐permeable inhibitory peptide specifically engineered to disrupt the pathological interaction between dynamin‐related protein 1 (Drp1) and its mitochondrial outer membrane receptor fission protein 1 (Fis1). It is synthesized via solid‐phase peptide synthesis methods and rational design strategies that mimic the native binding interface of these two proteins. As such, P110 belongs to the class of peptide-based mitochondrial fission inhibitors, a subclass of compounds that have been previously investigated in a variety of neurodegenerative and metabolic disorders, where aberrant mitochondrial fission is implicated in disease pathology. These types of compounds have historically been used experimentally to restore normal mitochondrial morphological dynamics and attenuate excessive fission triggered by oxidative stress. The concept builds upon evidence that an overactive fission process, largely mediated by elevated activity of Drp1 in response to cellular stressors, results in mitochondrial fragmentation, cytochrome c release, and subsequent cell death. In retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells, which bear extremely high energy demands for functions such as photoreceptor outer segment (POS) phagocytosis, proper mitochondrial network integrity is essential. The peptide’s cell-permeability and selective inhibition of the Drp1/Fis1 binding suggest that P110 can efficiently penetrate ocular tissues and modulate mitochondrial dynamics locally, offering a promising approach to preserve mitochondrial function in the diseased retina (Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Fisher et al., 2022).

Therapeutic History:  
Numerous preclinical studies have investigated the modulation of mitochondrial dynamics in models of oxidative stress and neurodegeneration, often employing broad-spectrum inhibitors such as mitochondrial division inhibitor 1 (Mdivi-1) or genetic silencing approaches using Drp1-specific siRNA. In several in vitro studies using ARPE-19 cells—a human RPE cell line—excessive mitochondrial fission triggered by oxidative stress (for example, via hydrogen peroxide exposure) was effectively ameliorated by Drp1 inhibition. This intervention preserved mitochondrial structure, maintained ATP production, and increased cell viability, thereby preventing the morphological transition from tubular to fragmented mitochondria (Yako et al., 2021). Although these investigations have provided robust proof-of-concept evidence that targeting Drp1-mediated fission can be beneficial in preventing RPE dysfunction, most of the studies have not directly tested P110 peptide for age-related macular degeneration (AMD). Instead, the literature has explored alternative approaches using compounds like Mdivi-1 and genetic knockdown modalities to decrease Drp1 activity, which indirectly supports the mechanistic rationale for using a targeted inhibitor like P110. The literature also shows that increased expression of mitochondrial fission factor (Mff) and elevated Drp1 levels in AMD donor tissues correlate with impaired mitochondrial homeostasis and defective phagocytosis in the RPE (Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Fisher et al., 2022). There remains, however, a gap in direct evidence for P110’s use in AMD despite its clear conceptual alignment with the therapeutic strategy of reducing pathological mitochondrial fission. Clinical trials have emerged that investigate mitochondrial fission inhibitors in ocular conditions, yet the available database queries indicate that while there is clinical interest in Drp1-targeting approaches, specific details on the P110 peptide remain sparse (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.). Thus, while the broader therapeutic paradigm—targeting aberrant mitochondrial fission—is well supported, P110 itself represents an innovative candidate that requires further preclinical validation in AMD-relevant models.

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, the pathological process underpinning excessive mitochondrial fission centers on the overactivation of Drp1, a GTPase that orchestrates mitochondrial scission. Under normal physiological conditions, Drp1 exists in the cytosol and is transiently recruited to the mitochondrial outer membrane by receptors such as Fis1, mitochondrial fission factor (Mff), and MiD49/MiD51. In response to oxidative and inflammatory stress—conditions known to be prevalent in the aging RPE and AMD—the interaction between Drp1 and Fis1 is upregulated, leading to excessive fission, mitochondrial fragmentation, and subsequent mitochondrial dysfunction. P110 is designed to selectively inhibit the Drp1/Fis1 binding interface, thereby attenuating the aberrant recruitment of Drp1 to the mitochondria. In doing so, it preserves the elongated and interconnected mitochondrial network, which is essential for optimal mitochondrial bioenergetics and the production of adenosine triphosphate (ATP). Biochemically, inhibition of this interaction prevents the oligomerization of Drp1 on the mitochondrial surface, a process that is otherwise necessary for the formation of constriction rings that facilitate organelle division (Zhao et al., 2025). By blocking Drp1 recruitment, P110 indirectly maintains mitochondrial membrane potential and reduces the likelihood of cytochrome c release—a key event in the apoptosis cascade that is frequently observed in stressed RPE cells (Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Yako et al., 2021). Although many of the studies in the context of AMD have focused on Mdivi-1 as a Drp1 inhibitor, the specificity of P110 for the Drp1/Fis1 interaction might offer a distinct pharmacological profile, reducing off-target effects and preserving the physiological fission events necessary for maintaining overall mitochondrial quality control (Fisher et al., 2022; Yako et al., 2021). Furthermore, the fact that mitochondrial fission and fusion processes are tightly integrated with mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy pathways supports the prospect that modulating this specific interaction may lead to a net improvement in mitochondrial dynamics without completely abolishing essential fission events (Yako et al., 2021; Buonfiglio et al., 2024).

Expected Effect:  
Given the underlying hypothesis, the expected effect of P110 in AMD RPE cells would be to restore a balance in mitochondrial dynamics that has been disrupted by chronic oxidative stress. The assay proposed involves exposing cultured RPE cells, such as ARPE-19, to oxidative insults (e.g., H₂O₂) that would otherwise induce excessive Drp1-mediated mitochondrial fission. P110’s mode of action is anticipated to specifically block the pathological protein-protein interaction between Drp1 and Fis1, thereby reducing the formation of excessively fragmented mitochondria and promoting the preservation or re-establishment of a tubular mitochondrial network. This intact network is critical for efficient ATP production, which in turn supports high-energy processes such as the phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments (POS). POS phagocytosis is a metabolically demanding function that is crucial for photoreceptor renewal and overall retinal health. By maintaining ATP supply and preventing the release of pro-apoptotic factors (such as cytochrome c), P110 is expected to protect RPE cells from oxidative damage and improve cellular viability under stress conditions (Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Yako et al., 2021). Additionally, studies have demonstrated that mitochondrial fission inhibitors can lead to improved mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), reduced reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and enhanced cell survival under conditions of oxidative insult (Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Yako et al., 2021). In RPE cells, these improvements translate into a better capacity for POS uptake and metabolism, which are essential for sustaining the metabolic demands of the outer retina. Given that expression levels of Drp1 and its functional activity are elevated in AMD RPE cells, as evidenced by electron microscopy and proteomic analyses showing reduced mitochondrial numbers, diminished cristae, and defective fusion/fission ratios, the application of P110 is expected to counteract these detrimental changes by stabilizing the mitochondrial network (Fisher et al., 2022; Yako et al., 2021).

Overall Evaluation:  
In summarizing the overall evaluation of P110 as a therapeutic candidate for dry age-related macular degeneration, several key points emerge from the literature. First, the mechanistic rationale underpinning P110’s mode of action is robust and well-aligned with current understanding of AMD pathophysiology. Excessive Drp1-mediated mitochondrial fission has been implicated as a crucial driver of mitochondrial dysfunction in RPE cells—a hallmark of dry AMD. Several studies using alternative approaches such as pharmacological inhibition with Mdivi-1 or genetic knockdown via siRNA have consistently demonstrated that mitigating aberrant mitochondrial fission leads to improved mitochondrial integrity, preserved bioenergetic function, and enhanced cell viability in models of oxidative stress (Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Yako et al., 2021). This information directly supports the hypothesis that targeting the Drp1/Fis1 interaction, rather than globally inhibiting all Drp1 activity, could offer a more refined therapeutic approach with potentially fewer side effects. Such specificity is particularly appealing in the context of the retina where finely tuned mitochondrial dynamics are essential for high energy-demand processes like POS phagocytosis (Fisher et al., 2022).

One of the primary strengths of P110 lies in its cell-permeable peptide nature, which facilitates targeted intracellular delivery—a critical characteristic for therapies intended for ocular application. Its design, which specifically disrupts the interaction between Drp1 and Fis1, is predicated on the notion that pathological fission can be selectively blocked without interfering with the physiological fission necessary for regular mitochondrial turnover and repair. This specificity could, in theory, translate to a better safety profile compared to less selective inhibitors such as Mdivi-1 that may have off-target effects, including undesirable impacts on other aspects of mitochondrial function (Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Fisher et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2025).

Despite the promising mechanistic rationale and potential for ocular development, there are notable weaknesses and challenges to be considered. The current literature does not yet offer direct preclinical or clinical evidence that P110 peptide itself has been used to treat dry AMD. Most existing studies have extrapolated the benefits of Drp1 inhibition from experiments involving Mdivi-1 or Drp1 knockdown in cellular models of RPE oxidative stress (Yako et al., 2021; Buonfiglio et al., 2024). Therefore, while the concept of targeting Drp1-mediated mitochondrial fission in AMD is well supported, the direct application of P110 remains largely theoretical at this point. This gap in the literature introduces uncertainty regarding the peptide’s pharmacokinetic properties, its stability in ocular tissues, and its potential immunogenic responses upon local administration in the eye.

Another potential limitation is the complex role of mitochondrial dynamics in cellular homeostasis. Although excessive fission in the context of oxidative stress is detrimental, mitochondrial fission is also a necessary process for mitophagy and the removal of damaged organelles. Some studies have indicated that a certain degree of fission may even be beneficial for cellular quality control, as it facilitates the segregation and subsequent removal of malfunctioning mitochondria (Skeie et al., 2021; Yako et al., 2021). The nuanced interplay between reactive oxygen species (ROS) signaling and fission means that complete inhibition of fission—even if intended to be only pathological—could potentially have unintended consequences. It will be critical to determine whether P110’s selective inhibition of Drp1/Fis1 interaction can maintain the necessary balance between inhibiting excessive fission while still allowing physiological fission to contribute to normal mitophagy and mitochondrial turnover.

Furthermore, while the peptide’s cell-permeability is an advantage, peptide therapeutics in ocular applications face challenges related to delivery, degradation by ocular proteases, and potential issues with achieving sustained concentrations in the target tissues. Addressing these challenges may require the development of specialized drug delivery systems, such as intravitreal injections with sustained-release formulations or conjugation to nanoparticles, to ensure that adequate therapeutic levels of P110 are maintained at the retinal level over the necessary duration (Yako et al., 2021; Buonfiglio et al., 2024).

Additional practical concerns include the need for rigorous in vivo studies in relevant animal models of dry AMD to assess not only the efficacy in preserving mitochondrial function and POS phagocytosis in RPE cells but also the overall impact on retinal structure and visual function. Currently, many studies have been limited to in vitro models using ARPE-19 cells under oxidative stress conditions. Although these models provide valuable mechanistic insights, they do not fully recapitulate the complex interplay of cellular and systemic factors that contribute to AMD in vivo. The therapeutic potential of P110 will need to be evaluated in animal models that mimic the pathophysiological conditions of dry AMD, where factors such as chronic low-grade inflammation, complement activation, and cumulative oxidative damage play significant roles (Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Fisher et al., 2022; Yako et al., 2021).

In addition to efficacy and safety concerns, the regulatory landscape for peptide therapeutics in ocular indications should be considered. The development of P110 for dry AMD will require detailed pharmacodynamic and toxicological evaluations to satisfy regulatory requirements. While the peptide’s mechanism—targeting a well-characterized protein-protein interaction—provides a clear scientific rationale, the path toward clinical translation is contingent on demonstrating not only mechanistic efficacy but also an acceptable safety profile in both preclinical toxicology studies and early-phase clinical trials (Fisher et al., 2022; ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.).

In conclusion, the comprehensive literature review supports the notion that targeting mitochondrial dysfunction, specifically through the inhibition of Drp1-mediated mitochondrial fission, is a highly promising therapeutic avenue for dry AMD. P110, as a selective inhibitor of the Drp1/Fis1 interaction, is well poised within this framework. The candidate is grounded in a strong mechanistic rationale that links excessive mitochondrial fission to reduced ATP production, impaired POS phagocytosis, and ultimately, RPE cell dysfunction—a cascade of events that underlies the pathogenesis of dry AMD (Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Fisher et al., 2022).

The inherent advantages of a peptide-based therapeutic, including its specificity and cell-permeability, are particularly attractive for ocular drug development, where targeted delivery and minimal off-target effects are crucial. Nonetheless, notable weaknesses remain that must be addressed before P110 can be considered a viable therapeutic candidate for dry AMD. The absence of direct experimental data with P110 in AMD models, the complex nature of mitochondrial dynamics where inhibition of fission must be carefully balanced to avoid impairing critical mitophagy processes, and potential challenges associated with peptide stability and delivery in the ocular environment represent significant hurdles. These challenges underscore the necessity for further preclinical investigations, including in vivo efficacy and safety studies in animal models that recapitulate the chronic oxidative stress and metabolic derangements characteristic of AMD (Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Yako et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2025).

Ultimately, while the theoretical basis for P110’s use in dry AMD is compelling and supported by a considerable body of literature showing beneficial effects of mitochondrial fission inhibition in RPE cells, advancing this candidate will require a coordinated effort to fill the current gaps in experimental evidence. Future studies should focus on validating the efficacy of P110 in terms of restoring mitochondrial dynamics, preserving ATP production, and ultimately improving POS phagocytosis in both cell culture and animal models of AMD. If these studies yield positive results, P110 has the potential to become an important component of a new generation of therapies aimed at addressing the mitochondrial dysfunction that lies at the heart of dry AMD pathophysiology (Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Fisher et al., 2022; Yako et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2025).

Overall, the research direction for P110 is promising given the established relationship between mitochondrial dysfunction and AMD. With its targeted mechanism of action and a strong conceptual basis rooted in the modulation of mitochondrial dynamics, P110 stands as a high-potential candidate. However, careful optimization of dosing, formulation, and delivery, as well as comprehensive preclinical validation, is essential before this therapeutic candidate can be reliably advanced into clinical trials. Should these hurdles be successfully overcome, P110 could not only provide substantial therapeutic benefit by preserving RPE function in dry AMD but also serve as a model for developing other targeted mitochondrial therapies in ocular neurodegeneration (Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Yako et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2025).
