Proposal for Nintedanib (PDGFR/VEGFR/FGFR kinase inhibitor; Boehringer Ingelheim)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Nintedanib is a synthetic small‐molecule drug originally discovered and developed by Boehringer Ingelheim through an extensive lead optimization program that began in the late 1990s. Its design emerged during early cancer drug discovery efforts focused on angiogenesis inhibition, inspired in part by Judah Folkman’s pioneering work. The compound belongs to the indolinone chemical class – more precisely, it is a 6‐methoxycarbonyl‐substituted indolinone derivative – and was identified after systematic structure–activity relationship (SAR) studies that evaluated numerous six‐substituted indolinone analogues. In particular, modifications such as moving an amido group on the oxindole scaffold were shown to eliminate unwanted CDK4 inhibition while retaining potent VEGFR inhibition, and these insights eventually led to the convergence on the lead compound now known as nintedanib (Roth et al., 2016, pp. 1–8). The synthetic strategy employed a convergent approach that involved classical malonic ester addition reactions, followed by hydrogenation under acidic conditions that induced decarboxylative cyclization, and concluded with condensation steps and salt formation (resulting in the monoethanesulfonate form) to yield a highly crystalline final product (Roth et al., 2015, pp. 5–10, 39–43). As an indolinone derivative, nintedanib exemplifies a well‐studied class of therapeutic compounds that modulate receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs). Similar compounds in this class have been widely used in antiangiogenic and antifibrotic therapies in oncology and pulmonary medicine, and their ability to simultaneously inhibit multiple RTK pathways has been key to overcoming resistance mechanisms frequently encountered with single‐target inhibitors (Roth et al., 2015, pp. 1–5; Roth et al., 2016, pp. 22–25).  

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, nintedanib was developed with an emphasis on its anticancer potential. Its original targeting of the vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR) and subsequent demonstration of triple angiokinase inhibition – simultaneously inhibiting VEGFR, platelet‐derived growth factor receptors (PDGFR), and fibroblast growth factor receptors (FGFR) – made it a candidate for addressing tumor angiogenesis. Early preclinical studies confirmed that nintedanib potently inhibited endothelial cell proliferation and in vivo tumor growth, leading to its progression into numerous Phase I through III clinical trials for non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), ovarian cancer, and other malignancies (Azuma et al., 2015, pp. 3–5; Roth et al., 2016, pp. 20–22). In addition, the compound’s remarkable specificity, as evidenced by low nanomolar inhibition of PDGFR and VEGFR family members (with IC₅₀ values in the range of 13–65 nM for different targets), underscored its potential as a broad‐spectrum antiangiogenic agent (Roth et al., 2015, pp. 10–15; Roth et al., 2016, pp. 12–14).  

Beyond its oncology applications, accumulating clinical evidence revealed that nintedanib also exhibits strong antifibrotic properties. Its efficacy in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) – with demonstrated improvements in forced vital capacity decline and overall clinical outcomes – led to its regulatory approval for IPF treatment in regions such as Europe and the United States (Roth et al., 2015, pp. 36–39; Roth et al., 2016, pp. 20–22). These diverse therapeutic indications illustrate that while nintedanib was originally intended as an anticancer agent, its mechanism of inhibiting multiple RTK‐mediated pathways allowed its repurposing for fibrotic diseases. Although nintedanib has not yet been extensively used in dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD), recent preclinical evidence in ocular models has suggested that its kinase inhibition profile could be exploited to modulate dysfunctional signaling in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells (Hatano et al., 2019, pp. 10–13, 16–17). Notably, studies in retinal models have demonstrated that nintedanib can suppress VEGF secretion and reduce neovascularization, both of which provide indirect evidence supporting its ocular tolerability and hint at potential utility in diseases such as dry AMD.  

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, nintedanib exerts its pharmacological effect by acting as a competitive inhibitor of the ATP‐binding site located within the catalytic domain of receptor tyrosine kinases. Structural studies, including co‐crystallization and high‐resolution X‐ray diffraction analyses, have revealed that its indolinone core is critical for binding; the molecule forms two canonical hydrogen bonds with key residues within the kinase “hinge” region – for example, interactions with the nitrogen of a conserved cysteine residue (such as Cys919 in VEGFR2) and with a carbonyl oxygen (such as Glu917) – thereby stabilizing its association with the enzyme (Roth et al., 2016, pp. 8–12). The presence of a methyl carboxylate substituent on the indolinone core further extends into the kinase specificity pocket to reinforce binding, and a methyl piperazinyl side chain interacts with solvent‐exposed portions of the protein, contributing both to the agent’s selectivity and to its sustained inhibitory profile (Roth et al., 2015, pp. 39–43; Roth et al., 2016, pp. 8–12).  

Functionally, nintedanib displays potent inhibition of multiple RTKs. Its IC₅₀ values have been reported to be in the low nanomolar range for VEGFR subtypes (e.g., approximately 21 nM for VEGFR2 and 13 nM for VEGFR3), as well as for PDGFR isoforms (roughly 41–58 nM) and FGFR family members (with values ranging from 37 nM for FGFR2 to higher values for other FGFRs) (Azuma et al., 2015, pp. 3–5; Roth et al., 2015, pp. 10–15; Roth et al., 2016, pp. 12–14). By competitively occupying the ATP‐binding pocket, nintedanib prevents receptor autophosphorylation, thereby blocking subsequent activation of downstream signaling cascades, including the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and protein kinase B (Akt) pathways (Reck, 2015, pp. 2–3; Roth et al., 2016, pp. 8–12). This broad inhibition interrupts not only angiogenic signaling in endothelial cells and pericytes but also fibrotic signaling in fibroblasts and myofibroblasts. An additional key feature of nintedanib’s mode of action is the sustained duration of receptor blockade; experimental studies have shown that even after a short 1-hour exposure, inhibition of VEGFR2 phosphorylation can persist for at least 32 hours, suggesting a relatively slow dissociation rate from the kinase active site (Roth et al., 2015, pp. 10–15; Roth et al., 2016, pp. 8–12).  

In the specific context of retinal pigment epithelial cells, PDGFRβ and FGFR1 are among the RTKs implicated in maintaining cellular differentiation, regulating cytoskeletal organization, and modulating phagocytic activity. Dysregulation of these signaling pathways in RPE cells may lead to a decrease in the effective expression of key phagocytic receptors such as integrin αvβ5 and MERTK – receptors that are vital for the ingestion and clearance of photoreceptor outer segments (POS). Although much of the detailed mechanistic work in RPE cells remains in the early stages, the existing experimental evidence supports the notion that balanced RTK signaling is necessary for optimal RPE function. By inhibiting overactive signaling through PDGFRβ and FGFR1, nintedanib could restore a more physiologic phosphorylation state that permits proper receptor recycling and expression at the cell surface, thereby supporting phagocytosis and maintaining cellular integrity (Hatano et al., 2019, pp. 10–13, 16–17). Additionally, by modulating these upstream kinases, downstream events that regulate cytoskeletal dynamics – potentially including reorganization of actin filaments and stabilization of focal adhesions – could be favorably altered. Although direct evidence in RPE cells is still emerging, analogous effects in other cell types have been observed when RTK signaling is normalized by multi-target inhibitors such as nintedanib (Hilberg et al., 2018, pp. 14–15, 19–20).  

Expected Effect:  
Within the RPE, efficient phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments is fundamental to retinal homeostasis, and failure of this process is believed to contribute to the pathogenesis of dry AMD. The hypothesis proposes that dysregulated PDGFRβ and FGFR1 signaling in RPE cells impairs the expression and functional activity of phagocytic receptors, notably integrin αvβ5 and MERTK, which are responsible for recognizing and internalizing shed POS. Under normal conditions, these receptors are tightly regulated by extracellular signals, many of which are modulated by RTK activity. In the diseased state, abnormal RTK phosphorylation – perhaps via overactivation of PDGFR and FGFR pathways – could lead to a decrease in phagocytic receptor levels and result in defective POS clearance, contributing to the accumulation of cellular debris, chronic inflammation, and eventual geographic atrophy observed in dry AMD.  

By virtue of its potent inhibitory properties, nintedanib is expected to act by normalizing the aberrantly high RTK signaling in RPE cells. Its inhibition of PDGFRβ, in particular, would be anticipated to reduce excessive downstream signaling that might otherwise lead to a suppression of phagocytic receptor synthesis or recycling. In parallel, FGFR1 inhibition could correct imbalances in signaling that contribute to impaired cytoskeletal remodeling. The cytoskeleton is intimately involved in the process of phagocytosis, not only by providing the structural basis for membrane extension during engulfment but also by facilitating receptor clustering and the formation of phagocytic cups. Therefore, it is plausible to expect that the use of nintedanib would lead to enhanced expression of integrin αvβ5 and MERTK at the RPE cell surface as normal feedback loops are reinstated, thereby promoting increased rates of POS ingestion in assays of RPE differentiation (Hatano et al., 2019, pp. 10–13, 16–17).  

Furthermore, preclinical studies have shown that in settings where RPE cells are exposed to pathological stimuli (such as necrotic cell lysates, which serve as a surrogate for sterile inflammatory conditions seen in retinal diseases), treatment with nintedanib results in a concentration-dependent suppression of aberrant VEGF secretion. This observation suggests that nintedanib is not only capable of regulating angiogenic processes in the retina but may also normalize other aspects of RPE cell function that rely on balanced RTK signaling (Hatano et al., 2019, pp. 10–13). In the context of the proposed assays, one would expect that treatment with nintedanib will restore proper receptor tyrosine kinase activity, result in the upregulation of phagocytic receptor expression, and enhance the mechanical and structural reorganization required for efficient POS uptake. These outcomes could be measured both biochemically – through assays of receptor expression and phosphorylation levels – and functionally, via quantification of POS ingestion in differentiated RPE cultures (Roth et al., 2016, pp. 8–12; Rashdan & Hanna, 2014, pp. 2–3).  

In addition to its direct impact on RTK signaling, nintedanib’s inhibition is anticipated to indirectly influence other cytoskeleton-regulating pathways that are governed by MAPK and Akt cascades. These pathways have been associated with the regulation of actin dynamics and the assembly of focal adhesions, both of which are essential for robust phagocytic activity. Thus, normalization of these signaling pathways may foster an environment in RPE cells where cytoskeletal remodeling is optimized, ultimately leading to improved structural integrity and function—key factors in maintaining retinal health in the face of degenerative processes (Reck, 2015, pp. 2–3; Hilberg et al., 2018, pp. 19–20).  

Overall Evaluation:  
In summary, nintedanib presents a compelling profile as a therapeutic candidate for repurposing in dry age‐related macular degeneration. One of its primary strengths is its well‐documented ability to inhibit multiple receptor tyrosine kinases simultaneously. This triple angiokinase inhibition—encompassing VEGFR, PDGFR, and FGFR—has been extensively characterized in both in vitro and in vivo models, resulting in robust antitumor and antifibrotic effects in clinical studies for cancer and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (Roth et al., 2015, pp. 36–39; Roth et al., 2016, pp. 20–22). The compound’s chemical structure, based on its indolinone core, not only permits tight binding and competitive inhibition at the ATP‐binding site but also provides the necessary selectivity that minimizes off-target effects, a critical feature when considering repurposing for a delicate tissue such as the retina (Roth et al., 2016, pp. 8–12; Roth et al., 2015, pp. 39–43).  

From a therapeutic history perspective, the extensive clinical experience with nintedanib in non‐small cell lung cancer and IPF engenders a level of confidence in its pharmacokinetic profile and tolerability when administered systemically. Although its use in ocular applications remains experimental, preliminary preclinical findings provide encouraging data. For instance, studies have demonstrated that nintedanib can inhibit VEGF secretion from retinal pigment epithelial cells exposed to pathological stimuli and can reduce retinal and choroidal neovascularization in animal models. These findings support the notion that, with appropriate reformulation or local delivery (e.g., intravitreal injection), nintedanib could achieve therapeutic levels in the retina without incurring unacceptable toxicity (Hatano et al., 2019, pp. 10–13, 16–17).  

The mechanistic basis for considering nintedanib in dry AMD is particularly appealing. Dry AMD is characterized in part by the dysfunction of RPE cells, including impaired phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments—a process tightly regulated by surface receptors such as integrin αvβ5 and MERTK. Dysregulation of upstream receptor tyrosine kinases, notably PDGFRβ and FGFR1, is hypothesized to contribute to the diminished expression or function of these phagocytic receptors. Nintedanib, by virtue of its potent inhibition of these kinases, holds the potential to recalibrate RTK signaling networks toward a more balanced state. This recalibration may not only restore proper levels of phagocytic receptors on RPE cells but also promote cytoskeletal remodeling necessary for the engulfment and processing of POS (Hatano et al., 2019, pp. 10–13, 16–17). The downstream effects of such restoration could conceivably slow or even reverse aspects of RPE dysfunction and subsequent retinal degeneration in dry AMD.  

Nonetheless, there are important considerations and potential weaknesses. One significant challenge lies in the delivery of nintedanib to the retina at therapeutic concentrations. Systemic administration of nintedanib is associated with low oral bioavailability and extensive first-pass metabolism (as observed in its use for IPF), which implies that direct ocular administration, likely via intravitreal injection, would be necessary for effective treatment of dry AMD. Although preclinical studies have demonstrated tolerability in ocular models, comprehensive toxicology studies specific to the retinal environment are required to ensure that the compound does not adversely affect other critical retinal cell types, including photoreceptors and inner retinal neurons (Roth et al., 2015, pp. 15–19; Roth et al., 2016, pp. 12–14).  

Another potential limitation is the broad scope of kinase inhibition exerted by nintedanib. While its ability to simultaneously target VEGFR, PDGFR, and FGFR is a strength in terms of overcoming compensatory mechanisms, it also raises the possibility of off-target effects that may disrupt other cellular processes in the retina. The retina is a highly specialized and tightly regulated tissue, and even subtle perturbations in signaling networks can lead to unintended consequences. Therefore, it will be critical to establish that nintedanib’s inhibition of RTKs in RPE cells does not interfere with other essential pathways—especially those involved in retinal development and neuroprotection (Hilberg et al., 2018, pp. 14–15; Hilberg et al., 2018, pp. 19–20).  

In addition, while the hypothesis that nintedanib can enhance phagocytic receptor expression (e.g., upregulating integrin αvβ5 and MERTK) is supported by the general principle that RTK signaling modulates these receptors, direct evidence linking nintedanib treatment to increased phagocytosis in RPE cells is still at an early, hypothesis‐driven stage. Rigorous experimental validation will be needed, potentially involving detailed biochemical assays to measure receptor expression and functional assays to assess POS ingestion rates in differentiated RPE cultures as well as in relevant animal models of dry AMD (Roth et al., 2016, pp. 8–12; Rashdan & Hanna, 2014, pp. 2–3).  

Furthermore, while the ocular tolerability of nintedanib has been indirectly inferred from its use in non‐small cell lung cancer and preclinical models of choroidal neovascularization, comprehensive studies specifically designed to evaluate its safety profile in the context of dry AMD are lacking. Given that dry AMD is a chronic condition that would likely require long‐term treatment, issues of sustained drug exposure, potential cumulative toxicity, and the risk of eliciting off‐target effects must be thoroughly investigated (Hatano et al., 2019, pp. 10–13; Hatano et al., 2019, pp. 16–17).  

Overall, nintedanib’s established preclinical and clinical data in oncology and fibrosis, combined with its potent multi-targeted inhibition of key receptor tyrosine kinases, provides a strong foundation for its consideration as a repurposed therapeutic candidate in dry AMD. The molecular rationale—that abnormal PDGFRβ and FGFR1 signaling impair the expression and function of essential phagocytic receptors in RPE cells—is both biologically plausible and supported by insights from related pathologies. The anticipated restoration of phagocytic capacity via improved expression of integrin αvβ5 and MERTK, along with enhanced cytoskeletal remodeling conducive to POS uptake, represents an attractive therapeutic mechanism that could address a central defect in dry AMD pathogenesis (Hatano et al., 2019, pp. 10–13, 16–17).  

In conclusion, while the repurposing of nintedanib for dry AMD is supported by a strong mechanistic framework and encouraging early preclinical findings, significant gaps remain that must be addressed before further clinical development is warranted. Critical next steps include:  
1. Detailed in vitro studies using differentiated human RPE cells to measure the impact of nintedanib on PDGFRβ and FGFR1 phosphorylation status, the expression levels of integrin αvβ5 and MERTK, and functional phagocytic assays assessing POS uptake.  
2. In vivo investigations in relevant animal models of dry AMD to evaluate retinal pharmacokinetics, tissue distribution, and the long-term safety profile following local ocular administration.  
3. Exploration of optimal delivery strategies to maximize retinal exposure while minimizing systemic absorption and potential off-target effects.  
4. Further mechanistic studies to elucidate the downstream signaling pathways – including MAPK and Akt cascades – that may be modulated by nintedanib in the RPE and contribute to cytoskeletal remodeling.  

By addressing these areas, it should be possible to rigorously validate whether nintedanib can indeed “normalize” RTK signaling in the RPE and restore the phagocytic function that is compromised in dry AMD. The strengths of nintedanib lie in its potent, well-characterized kinase inhibition and its established clinical safety record in other disease indications. However, challenges remain in adapting its pharmacologic profile to the unique requirements of retinal therapy and ensuring that its broad RTK profile does not inadvertently disrupt other critical ocular functions. If these challenges can be overcome through careful preclinical and translational research, nintedanib may represent a transformative therapeutic option for addressing an unmet need in dry AMD (Roth et al., 2016, pp. 1–5, 20–22; Hatano et al., 2019, pp. 10–13).  

Overall, our evaluation suggests that while caution must be exercised due to potential pharmacokinetic and off-target concerns, the available biochemical and clinical evidence provides sufficient justification for further investigation. The detailed molecular insights into nintedanib’s action, its demonstrated efficacy in modulating kinase pathways central to angiogenesis and fibrosis, and preliminary data in ocular models collectively support its candidacy for repurposing in dry AMD. Rigorous further studies will determine its ultimate utility, but current data indicate that nintedanib is a promising candidate worthy of additional mechanistic and translational evaluation (Roth et al., 2016, pp. 8–12; Reck, 2015, pp. 2–3; Hatano et al., 2019, pp. 10–13, 16–17).
