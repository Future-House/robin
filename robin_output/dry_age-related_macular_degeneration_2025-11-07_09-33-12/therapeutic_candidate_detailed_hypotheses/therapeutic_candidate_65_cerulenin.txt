Proposal for Cerulenin (Fatty acid synthase inhibitor; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. C2389)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Cerulenin is a natural product originally isolated from the filamentous fungus Cephalosporium caerulens and was the first discovered fatty acid synthase (FAS) inhibitor (Angeles & Hudkins, 2016; López & Diéguez, 2007). Chemically, this compound is classified as an epoxy-keto fatty acid analogue; it contains a reactive epoxide ring conjugated to a diene/amidic structure that mimics fatty acid substrates. This reactive epoxide is key to its mechanism because it enables cerulenin to irreversibly bind to its target enzyme via covalent modification. As such, cerulenin belongs to the class of FAS inhibitors that historically have been used as biochemical probes to investigate lipid metabolism and, subsequently, as lead compounds in the development of antineoplastic agents, given the upregulation of de novo lipogenesis in many cancers (Angeles & Hudkins, 2016; López & Diéguez, 2007; Abramson, 2011). The molecule’s natural origin, combined with its potent inhibitory activity, has made it a valuable tool in studies of metabolic regulation and lipogenesis over several decades.

Therapeutic History:  
In the past, cerulenin was primarily developed and investigated as an antimicrobial and antifungal antibiotic due to its capacity to irreversibly inhibit fatty acid synthesis in both bacterial and eukaryotic systems (Angeles & Hudkins, 2016; Fhu & Ali, 2020). Subsequent research in the fields of oncology and metabolic regulation revealed that several tumor types exhibit elevated expression of FAS, making FAS inhibitors promising candidates for anticancer therapy. Preclinical studies demonstrated that cerulenin could induce apoptosis in tumor cells, delay disease progression in xenograft models, and even affect systemic metabolic parameters such as food intake and body weight in rodent models (López & Diéguez, 2007; Heath, White, & Rock, 2002; Goodman & McFadden, 2007). Despite these promising results, its clinical utility has been constrained by poor chemical stability—primarily due to its reactive epoxide moiety—and significant toxicity stemming from irreversibility and off-target interactions with other cysteine-containing proteins (Angeles & Hudkins, 2016; Fhu & Ali, 2020). Furthermore, while modifications have led to second-generation analogues like C75, designed to improve stability and selectivity, there are no records of cerulenin being specifically applied to ophthalmologic conditions such as dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) or similar retinal lipotoxicity diseases (Menendez & Lupu, 2017; Lawrence, Zilfou, & Smith, 1999). Although cerulenin has been explored in other in vivo models (for example, for retinitis pigmentosa in preclinical studies), no clinical trials have been registered for its use in AMD (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, cerulenin exerts its biological effects by targeting the fatty acid synthase complex. Its mechanism involves the formation of an irreversible covalent adduct with an active-site cysteine residue (specifically, Cys1305) within the ketosynthase (KS) domain of type I FAS. This is achieved by an epoxide ring-opening reaction at the C-2 position of cerulenin, leading to the nucleophilic attack by the thiol (-SH) group of the catalytic cysteine residue (Abramson, 2011; Johansson et al., 2008). Crystallographic studies have provided precise structural insights into this interaction, showing that such covalent modification not only blocks the initial acyl-transfer step but also induces conformational changes—such as the flipping of key residues like F1646 and the rotation of M1251—that ultimately disrupt the hydrophobic acyl-binding tunnel required for fatty acid chain elongation (Abramson, 2011; Johansson et al., 2008; Fhu & Ali, 2020). In addition to inhibiting FAS directly, cerulenin has also been shown to impair protein palmitoylation, a post-translational modification that plays an important role in regulating membrane association and signaling functions of numerous proteins including Ras oncoproteins (Lee, Stephens, & Englund, 2007; Lawrence, Zilfou, & Smith, 1999). This dual mechanism—targeting both de novo fatty acid synthesis and protein acylation—underscores the broad-spectrum bioactivity of cerulenin and its potential impact on multiple cellular pathways that are dysregulated in disease states (Menendez & Lupu, 2017; Lawrence, Zilfou, & Smith, 1999).

Expected Effect:  
The hypothesis underpinning the proposed repurposing of cerulenin for dry AMD centers on its ability to attenuate de novo lipogenesis in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells. In the context of dry AMD, pathologic accumulation of lipofuscin and toxic bisretinoid compounds such as A2E is thought to result from excessive lipid synthesis and impaired degradation of photoreceptor outer segment (POS) material. Excess levels of intracellular fatty acids, often derived from hyperactive FAS, contribute to the generation of these lipofuscin precursors—ultimately impairing lysosomal function and leading to RPE dysfunction (Angeles & Hudkins, 2016; Fhu & Ali, 2020). By inhibiting FAS activity in the RPE, cerulenin is expected to reduce the synthesis of fatty acids that serve as precursors for lipofuscin components. This reduced lipogenesis should, in theory, alleviate the accumulation of lipofuscin, restore optimal levels of lysosomal pH, and enhance the activity of lysosomal enzymes such as cathepsin D—thereby improving the degradation and turnover of POS material (Radka & Rock, 2022; Troudi, Pagès, & Brunel, 2021). Furthermore, since oxidative stress is a key driver of abnormal lipid metabolism in RPE cells, the ability of cerulenin to lower fatty acid synthesis may concurrently reduce the burden of oxidative damage and improve cellular homeostasis (Vázquez et al., 2008; Lee, Stephens, & Englund, 2007). It is also noteworthy that FAS expression has been detected and functionally characterized in various extrahepatic tissues; while direct evidence of its expression in RPE cells is less extensively documented in the provided citations, the extrapolation from other cellular models supports the likelihood that modulating FAS activity in RPE would confer benefits in lipid turnover and lysosomal efficiency (Lawrence, Zilfou, & Smith, 1999; Johansson et al., 2008). Thus, within the proposed assay—where RPE cells are subjected to oxidative stress that mimics the environment of dry AMD—cerulenin is anticipated to lower intracellular lipid levels, minimize the generation of lipofuscin precursors, and ultimately restore RPE functional capacity.

Overall Evaluation:  
Cerulenin presents an intriguing therapeutic candidate for repurposing in dry AMD owing to its well-documented ability to inhibit fatty acid synthase through a unique irreversible, covalent mechanism. Its molecular footprint, characterized by a reactive epoxide ring, has been exploited in multiple studies to evaluate cellular lipid synthesis and, by extension, to modulate metabolic pathways that underpin several disease processes (Angeles & Hudkins, 2016; Abramson, 2011). Mechanistically, its capacity to bind to a crucial cysteine residue in the FAS enzyme and to interfere with lipid assembly positions it as a potentially valuable agent to counter the lipid-driven pathogenesis implicated in RPE dysfunction and lipofuscin accumulation in dry AMD (Abramson, 2011; Johansson et al., 2008).

The strengths of cerulenin as a candidate include its small-molecule profile, which facilitates cellular entry; its established use as a research tool in in vitro and in vivo models; and its dual activity profile that not only curtails de novo lipogenesis but may also impede aberrant protein palmitoylation. Such multifaceted activity could be beneficial in a complex tissue environment like the retina, where multiple overlapping metabolic dysfunctions contribute to disease progression (Lee, Stephens, & Englund, 2007; Lawrence, Zilfou, & Smith, 1999). In particular, by reducing the synthesis of fatty acids in RPE cells, cerulenin could deplete one of the key substrates for the formation of lipofuscin precursors such as A2E, thereby potentially improving lysosomal degradation of shed POS and arresting a vicious cycle of intracellular lipid accumulation and oxidative stress (Radka & Rock, 2022; Troudi, Pagès, & Brunel, 2021).

However, several weaknesses must be carefully considered. Cerulenin’s inherent chemical instability, attributed to its reactive epoxide ring, raises significant concerns regarding its pharmacokinetic profile and overall toxicity, especially in a sensitive tissue like the retina where off-target effects may exacerbate cellular stress rather than alleviate it (Angeles & Hudkins, 2016; Heath, White, & Rock, 2002). The irreversible nature of its enzyme inhibition—while beneficial for ensuring sustained suppression of FAS—may lead to collateral damage in non-target tissues, and the narrow therapeutic window observed in other preclinical studies suggests that dose optimization could be challenging (Fhu & Ali, 2020; Menendez & Lupu, 2017). Moreover, although the hypothesis posits that inhibition of de novo lipogenesis in the RPE will mitigate lipid accumulation and improve lysosomal function, there is a paucity of direct evidence linking cerulenin or similar FAS inhibitors to improved outcomes in dry AMD models specifically. The documented applications of cerulenin are primarily in the realms of oncology and metabolic regulation, with little precedent for its use in ocular conditions (Lawrence, Zilfou, & Smith, 1999; ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.). This suggests that while the mechanistic rationale is sound, the translational leap to an ocular indication will require additional supportive data both from in vitro RPE assays and from appropriate animal models of AMD.

Another point of concern is that cerulenin’s nonselective inhibition of fatty acid synthase may also impair essential lipid synthesis in cells that require robust regenerative capacity, potentially leading to unintended cytotoxicity or impaired cell membrane maintenance. In the context of the RPE, where cellular health is paramount for photoreceptor support, any off-target lipid synthesis inhibition must be tightly controlled (Goodman & McFadden, 2007; Fhu & Ali, 2020). Furthermore, its use in vivo has been historically hampered by issues such as poor aqueous solubility—it often requires solvents like DMSO for administration—and its propensity for rapid degradation in protic environments, all of which represent substantial formulation challenges that must be overcome for ocular delivery (Angeles & Hudkins, 2016; Vázquez et al., 2008).

Given these considerations, while cerulenin offers a compelling proof-of-concept relating to the modulation of lipogenesis and lysosomal function, its practical development as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD would likely necessitate further chemical modifications. The precedent set by the development of derivatives such as C75—which were specifically engineered to overcome the limitations of cerulenin by removing the problematic epoxide group and enhancing stability—suggests that optimization is both possible and necessary (Angeles & Hudkins, 2016; Menendez & Lupu, 2017). Optimized analogues with improved pharmacokinetic and safety profiles may hold greater promise in the ocular setting than the parent compound.

In summary, cerulenin’s capacity to inhibit fatty acid synthase at the molecular level through irreversible covalent binding is well-established and aligns with the proposed therapeutic mechanism of reducing RPE lipid accumulation and lipofuscin precursor formation. However, its historical limitations in terms of chemical stability, toxicity, and nonselectivity present significant hurdles for its repurposing in dry AMD. The compound’s extensive use in metabolic and oncologic research provides a strong foundation for understanding its biochemical actions, but the absence of direct evidence in retinal models and the challenges associated with its delivery to ocular tissues temper enthusiasm for immediate clinical translation (Angeles & Hudkins, 2016; Fhu & Ali, 2020; Menendez & Lupu, 2017). Therefore, while there is sufficient mechanistic rationale to pursue further preclinical investigations—potentially in parallel with the development of more stable derivatives—the current state of evidence suggests that cerulenin itself may not be ideal as a therapeutic agent for dry AMD without significant further optimization. More comprehensive in vitro studies using RPE cell models and targeted in vivo studies in AMD-relevant animal models are needed to validate whether the inhibition of de novo lipogenesis by cerulenin can indeed translate into restored lysosomal function and improved photoreceptor outer segment degradation in this disease context (Lee, Stephens, & Englund, 2007; Radka & Rock, 2022).
