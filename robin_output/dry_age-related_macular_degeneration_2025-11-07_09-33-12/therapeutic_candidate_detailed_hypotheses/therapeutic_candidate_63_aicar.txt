Proposal for AICAR (AMPK activator; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. A9978)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
AICAR, or 5‐aminoimidazole‐4‐carboxamide‐1‐β‐D‐riboside, is a synthetic nucleoside analog that has been extensively used as a pharmacological activator of adenosine monophosphate–activated protein kinase (AMPK). AICAR is taken up by cells and phosphorylated by adenosine kinase to produce ZMP, a structural analog of AMP that mimics conditions of cellular energy depletion. This mimicry leads to the activation of AMPK, a master regulator of cellular energy homeostasis, which in turn induces a host of metabolic adaptations. AICAR belongs to the class of low molecular weight AMP analogs often used to study cellular metabolism and energy sensing. Its discovery came about through research directed at understanding AMP’s role in cellular energy regulation, and it has since been synthesized and optimized for in vitro and preclinical studies; it is commercially available from suppliers such as Sigma-Aldrich (Cat. No. A9978) (Nita & Grzybowski, 2023; Chan et al., 2020). As part of a broader group of metabolic modulators, AICAR’s synthetic origin and well‐characterized pharmacology have made it a valuable research tool in both basic science and translational models. The compound’s ability to robustly activate AMPK positions it as a promising candidate for conditions where restoration of energy balance, autophagic flux, and mitochondrial health is desirable, such as in dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) (Chan et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, AICAR has been used primarily as a metabolic modulator in preclinical research, rather than as an approved clinical drug. Its pharmacological profile has been most widely characterized in studies of skeletal muscle metabolism, ischemia, and metabolic stress, where its capacity to induce a “starvation-like” state by activating AMPK has provided crucial insights into cellular energy sensing. Several investigations have also demonstrated the potential of AICAR in ocular research. Notably, although AICAR has not yet been advanced to clinical trials specifically for AMD, preclinical studies have revealed its potential in improving retinal cell function. For instance, research using in vitro retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSC-RPE) has shown that treatment with agents like AICAR and other AMPK activators can enhance mitochondrial protein content and respiratory capacity in a manner that is beneficial for cellular energy maintenance (Fisher et al., 2022). In addition, AICAR has been evaluated in models of bacterial endophthalmitis, where it successfully restored AMPK activity, reduced inflammatory cytokine production, and enhanced phagocytic function of retinal immune cells through AMPK-dependent mechanisms (Kumar et al., 2016). Although these studies were conducted in the context of infectious retinal inflammation rather than degenerative disorders, they provide important proof-of-concept evidence that AICAR-mediated AMPK activation can modulate key pathways relevant to RPE function. This preclinical evidence is complemented by a wealth of literature demonstrating that metabolic modulators acting on the AMPK pathway (including metformin and trehalose) can restore autophagic flux and improve mitochondrial homeostasis in RPE cells, thereby reducing cellular stress and dysfunction associated with dry AMD (Vessey et al., 2022; Chan et al., 2020; Hyttinen et al., 2018).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, the mechanism of action of AICAR is centered on its ability to mimic AMP, leading to the activation of AMPK. Once internalized, AICAR is phosphorylated by adenosine kinase to form ZMP, which binds to the γ-subunit of AMPK. This binding induces a conformational change that results in the phosphorylation of the α-subunit at Thr172, thereby converting AMPK to its active form. Activated AMPK then orchestrates a multifaceted metabolic reprogramming: it upregulates catabolic pathways that generate ATP and downregulates anabolic processes that consume ATP. One critical downstream effect is the stimulation of ULK1-dependent autophagy. AMPK phosphorylates ULK1, initiating the formation of autophagosomes and thereby enhancing the clearance of damaged proteins and organelles—a process that is particularly important in post-mitotic cells such as RPE cells (Chan et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2024). Concurrently, activated AMPK also promotes mitochondrial biogenesis via the upregulation of peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor gamma coactivator-1α (PGC-1α). PGC-1α acts as a master transcriptional regulator of mitochondrial gene expression, enabling the synthesis of new mitochondria and the enhancement of oxidative phosphorylation capacity. In addition, AMPK activation suppresses mTOR signaling, which is normally inhibitory to autophagy, thereby further facilitating the degradative process (Zhang et al., 2020; Blasiak et al., 2021). Some studies have also demonstrated that AMPK activation enhances NAD⁺ biosynthesis, which is integral to sirtuin (SIRT1) function; SIRT1 then deacetylates and activates PGC-1α, creating a positive feedback loop that sustains mitochondrial and autophagic functions (Nita & Grzybowski, 2023; Chan et al., 2020). Moreover, the immunomodulatory and metabolic effects of AICAR observed in retinal immune cells—such as the reduction in glycolytic enzyme expression and enhanced bacterial clearance—are mediated by similar AMPK-dependent pathways, highlighting the broad cellular impact of this compound (Kumar et al., 2016). Collectively, these molecular interactions support the hypothesis that AICAR-induced AMPK activation can restore impaired autophagic flux, promote mitochondrial renewal, and ultimately enable enhanced photoreceptor outer segment (POS) phagocytosis in RPE cells affected by dry AMD (Chan et al., 2020; Hyttinen et al., 2018).

Expected Effect:  
In the specific context of dry AMD, the expected effect of AICAR hinges on its ability to correct key cellular deficits observed in dysfunctional RPE cells. Dry AMD is characterized by the accumulation of cellular debris, impaired autophagic clearance, mitochondrial dysfunction, and a compromised capacity for phagocytosis of POS. By activating AMPK, AICAR is anticipated to restore autophagic flux via ULK1 phosphorylation, leading to increased LC3 lipidation and improved autophagosome formation that are necessary for the degradation of accumulated lipofuscin and other cytotoxic aggregates (Zhou et al., 2024). Furthermore, the stimulation of PGC-1α activity by AMPK is expected to drive mitochondrial biogenesis, thereby replenishing the population of functional mitochondria and enhancing ATP production. This enhanced mitochondrial respiration would create a more robust energetic state within the RPE cells, which is critical for the energetically demanding process of POS phagocytosis (Fisher et al., 2022; Chan et al., 2020). The coupling of improved energy metabolism with enhanced autophagy is particularly relevant since effective POS clearance requires both sufficient energy and a competent degradative system. Notably, studies using iPSC-RPE models have demonstrated that activation of the AMPK pathway can lead to increased mitochondrial protein content—such as UQCRC2, COX II, and NDUFB8—and significant improvements in maximal respiration and spare respiratory capacity in cells derived from AMD donors (Fisher et al., 2022). These findings underpin the expectation that, in dry AMD, AICAR treatment would restore autophagic activity and mitochondrial function sufficiently to also improve the phagocytic clearance of POS. Enhanced POS clearance would, in theory, limit the accumulation of deleterious extracellular deposits and intracellular waste products that contribute to RPE cell degeneration. Moreover, by mitigating oxidative stress through improved mitochondrial turnover and more efficient autophagy, AICAR could reduce the chronic low-grade inflammation observed in dry AMD, thereby potentially slowing disease progression (Golestaneh et al., 2018; Vessey et al., 2022). It is also important to note that while some studies (e.g., those focusing on complement regulation in RPE cells) indicate that AICAR may have AMPK-independent effects in certain contexts (Chung et al., 2017), the weight of evidence supports an AMPK-mediated cascade that directly addresses the pathological hallmarks of dry AMD, including autophagy block and mitochondrial deficits (Chan et al., 2020; Hyttinen et al., 2018).

Overall Evaluation:  
Evaluating AICAR as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD involves weighing its mechanistic rationale, preclinical evidence, potential benefits, and possible limitations. Among its primary strengths is the robust mechanistic foundation by which AICAR acts. By serving as a potent AMPK activator, AICAR targets several interconnected pathways that are known to be dysregulated in dry AMD. Specifically, AMPK activation is recognized for promoting ULK1-dependent autophagy, enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis through PGC-1α activation, and suppressing mTOR signaling—all of which are critical for restoring cellular homeostasis in RPE cells (Chan et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020). These effects address the core pathophysiological processes in dry AMD, namely autophagy impairment and mitochondrial dysfunction. Furthermore, the evidence from iPSC-RPE studies, which demonstrate improvements in mitochondrial protein content and respiratory capacity upon activation of the AMPK pathway, lend additional support to the potential utility of AICAR in this context (Fisher et al., 2022). In vitro and in vivo data from bacterial endophthalmitis models, although primarily focused on acute inflammatory conditions, further highlight AICAR’s capacity to enhance phagocytic activity and modulate cellular metabolism via AMPK activation (Kumar et al., 2016). Such immunomodulatory effects may be beneficial in the chronic, low-grade inflammatory milieu present in dry AMD.  

Despite these promising attributes, several challenges and limitations must be acknowledged. A significant concern is that much of the existing evidence for AICAR’s efficacy originates from preclinical studies that have not been conducted explicitly in well-established models of dry AMD. For instance, while studies in iPSC-RPE models and bacterial endophthalmitis provide valuable insights, they do not fully replicate the complex, slowly progressive nature of dry AMD observed in the clinical setting (Fisher et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2024). Moreover, some reports suggest that AICAR’s regulatory effects on certain pathways, such as complement factor expression in RPE cells, may occur independently of AMPK activation (Chung et al., 2017), raising questions about the specificity of its action and the potential for off-target effects. Another limitation is related to the pharmacokinetic profile and tissue delivery of AICAR. While intravitreal administration has been successfully employed in animal models of ocular inflammation (Kumar et al., 2016), the optimal dosing regimen, potential toxicity, and long-term safety profile of AICAR in the retinal environment remain to be fully defined. Additionally, compared with other AMPK activators like metformin—which has a well-established safety record in clinical use—AICAR’s clinical experience is considerably more limited, potentially posing hurdles for rapid translation into human studies (Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Chan et al., 2020).  

A further point of caution is the inherent complexity of the AMPK signaling network. Although AMPK activation is broadly beneficial in conditions of energy stress, chronic and uncontrolled activation may lead to aberrant metabolic reprogramming or unintended effects on cellular growth and survival. Given that RPE cells are particularly sensitive to alterations in their metabolic milieu, it will be essential to carefully titrate AICAR dosing to achieve a therapeutic effect without eliciting deleterious side effects. Moreover, the multifactorial nature of dry AMD, which involves genetic predispositions (such as complement factor H risk variants), oxidative stress, and environmental factors, suggests that AICAR may need to be combined with other therapeutic agents to fully address the disease’s pathogenesis (Golestaneh et al., 2018; Kumar et al., 2016).  

Overall, the preclinical evidence substantiates a strong mechanistic rationale for repurposing AICAR as a therapeutic candidate in dry AMD. Its ability to activate AMPK, trigger ULK1-dependent autophagy, and promote mitochondrial biogenesis directly targets the cellular deficits that are central to the disease. The compound’s previous successful application in metabolic studies and ocular models, albeit mostly in inflammatory contexts, supports its potential for translation into dry AMD therapy. However, further studies are required to (1) validate its effects in dedicated dry AMD models, (2) optimize the route of administration and dosing schedule for ocular delivery, and (3) fully elucidate its long-term safety profile in the retinal environment. In summary, while AICAR presents several attractive features as a repurposed drug candidate for dry AMD, additional preclinical research and careful consideration of its pharmacodynamics will be critical steps before embarking on clinical trials (Chan et al., 2020; Fisher et al., 2022; Chan et al., 2020; Nita & Grzybowski, 2023; Chung et al., 2017).  

Conclusion:  
The comprehensive literature review indicates that AICAR—by virtue of its well-characterized role as an AMPK activator—has significant potential as a therapeutic candidate for dry age-related macular degeneration. Its mechanism of action, which includes the promotion of ULK1-dependent autophagy and stimulation of mitochondrial biogenesis via the PGC-1α pathway, addresses several key pathological features observed in AMD RPE cells. The expected outcomes, which include enhanced autophagic clearance of photoreceptor outer segments, restoration of mitochondrial respiratory function, and overall cellular energy rebalancing, are well aligned with the therapeutic needs in dry AMD. Although most supporting evidence is preclinical and has been demonstrated in models not entirely exclusive to AMD, the mechanistic overlap with AMD pathogenesis is compelling. Further detailed studies in AMD-specific models are warranted to verify these effects and to optimize the delivery and dosing regimen of AICAR in ocular tissues. Ultimately, the strengths of AICAR, including its established AMPK activation profile and its multifaceted impact on autophagy and mitochondrial function, render it a promising candidate to be prioritized for further investigation in the context of dry AMD treatment (Chan et al., 2020; Fisher et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2020; Hyttinen et al., 2018; Nita & Grzybowski, 2023; Vessey et al., 2022).  

Strengths of the candidate include its dual action on both autophagic and mitochondrial pathways, which are critically impaired in dry AMD, and a substantial body of preclinical data—both in retinal cell and animal models—demonstrating the beneficial impact of AMPK activation on cellular metabolism and phagocytic clearance. The potential for improved energy metabolism and enhanced clearance of cellular debris could translate into meaningful clinical benefits by slowing or reversing disease progression. Conversely, weaknesses to be addressed include the need for dedicated studies in dry AMD-specific models, the possibility of off-target effects arising from AMPK-independent actions of AICAR, and the challenges associated with its delivery and safety in the delicate retinal environment. In light of these considerations, while AICAR shows promise based on mechanistic rationale and supportive preclinical findings, it remains essential to validate these outcomes in comprehensive AMD models before clinical translation can be confidently pursued (Chung et al., 2017; Kumar et al., 2016; Chan et al., 2020; Vessey et al., 2022).  

In conclusion, the current evidence supports the potential of AICAR, via AMPK activation, as a compelling candidate for repurposing in the treatment of dry AMD. The integration of enhanced autophagy, improved mitochondrial biogenesis, and potential augmentation of photoreceptor outer segment phagocytosis provides a multifaceted approach to addressing the complex pathology of this disease. Based on the literature, further investigative efforts—including in vitro assays in patient-derived iPSC-RPE lines and in vivo studies using established dry AMD animal models—should be prioritized to determine the translational feasibility of AICAR in this setting. The robust mechanistic basis and preliminary efficacy data underpin a recommendation to advance AICAR into preclinical development for dry AMD, while carefully optimizing dosing, delivery routes, and assessment of long-term safety to mitigate any adverse effects (Chan et al., 2020; Fisher et al., 2022; Blasiak et al., 2021; Nita & Grzybowski, 2023; Vessey et al., 2022).
