Proposal for Huperzine A (Cholinesterase inhibitor; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. H9793)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Huperzine A is a naturally occurring alkaloid that was first isolated in the early 1980s from the Chinese medicinal plant Huperzia serrata, commonly referred to as Qian Ceng Ta (Al-Ghamdi et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2000). As a member of the lycopodium alkaloids, Huperzine A exhibits a unique chemical structure characterized by a rigid bicyclic framework that contributes to its high affinity for acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibition (Ferreira et al., 2016; Bai et al., 2000). Originally discovered through biodirected research into traditional Chinese medicines, Huperzine A was soon recognized for its ability to reverse cognitive deficits by elevating acetylcholine (ACh) levels in the central nervous system (Ferreira et al., 2016). Over subsequent decades, the compound has been synthesized and investigated extensively via both natural extraction and chemical synthesis to overcome issues related to its low natural yield (Ferreira et al., 2016). Huperzine A belongs to a therapeutic class of compounds known as AChE inhibitors, which have traditionally been used to treat conditions associated with cholinergic deficits, most notably Alzheimer’s disease. Its well-documented pharmacokinetic profile—including rapid absorption, wide body distribution, and effective penetration of the blood–brain barrier—has made it an attractive candidate not only for neurodegenerative disorders but, as emerging data suggest, for other diseases where cholinergic modulation may be beneficial (Filho et al., 2006; Ferreira et al., 2016).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, Huperzine A has been used in traditional Chinese medicine for a variety of indications including the treatment of contusions, swelling, and memory impairments (Bai et al., 2000; Ferreira et al., 2016). Its modern therapeutic development began when its potent, reversible, and selective AChE inhibitory properties were identified, leading to its approval in China as a palliative treatment for Alzheimer’s disease (Ferreira et al., 2016; Filho et al., 2006). Preclinical studies and clinical trials have demonstrated that Huperzine A increases synaptic acetylcholine levels by blocking the enzyme that degrades it; this effect on cholinergic neurotransmission has validated its use in patients with Alzheimer’s and age-associated cognitive decline (Bai et al., 2000; Muñoz-Torrero & Camps, 2008). In the United States, its limited regulatory status as a dietary supplement marketed for memory enhancement further reinforces its established record in modulating cholinergic function (Ferreira et al., 2016). Although its primary clinical application has been neurological—especially in the domain of Alzheimer’s disease—recent preclinical models in ophthalmology have assessed the ocular tolerability of Huperzine A in animal models of age-related macular degeneration (AMD), a finding that presents a compelling case for its repurposing. However, as of now, there is no direct clinical evidence supporting its use in treating dry AMD or similar retinal degenerative diseases; rather, the therapeutic rationale is built on its biochemical actions and benefits observed in nonocular systems (Ferreira et al., 2016; Zhu et al., 2006).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, Huperzine A exerts its primary effect by inhibiting acetylcholinesterase, the enzyme responsible for catalyzing the breakdown of acetylcholine (Al-Ghamdi et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2000). This inhibition is both reversible and selective, allowing for a sustained elevation of acetylcholine levels in synaptic clefts without causing the overstimulation typically associated with nonselective cholinergic agents (Filho et al., 2006; Ferreira et al., 2016). The compound binds within the active gorge of AChE, interacting with critical amino acid residues such as tryptophans and tyrosines, thereby preventing the hydrolysis of acetylcholine and enhancing cholinergic neurotransmission (Ferreira et al., 2016; Muñoz-Torrero & Camps, 2008). In addition to its cholinesterase inhibitory activity, Huperzine A has been found to exhibit NMDA receptor antagonism at cerebral synapses, suggesting an additional neuroprotective dimension to its pharmacology (Orhan et al., 2009; Ferreira et al., 2016). This multifaceted pharmacological profile—combining AChE inhibition, NMDA receptor antagonism, and enhancements in neuroprotection—has contributed to its reputation as a “nootropic” agent in both traditional and modern medicine (Bai et al., 2000; Tumiatti et al., 2008).

For the present hypothesis targeting dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD), the mechanism of action is being reexamined from a different angle. In the context of retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells, it is hypothesized that Huperzine A, by inhibiting AChE, will increase the local concentration of acetylcholine. Elevated acetylcholine can engage muscarinic receptors, which have been reported to have an EC₅₀ value of approximately 10 nM in similar cellular contexts (Zaki et al., 2020). Muscarinic receptor activation in RPE cells is proposed to stimulate downstream signaling pathways including PI3K/Akt and the small GTPase Rac1. PI3K/Akt signaling is a well-characterized pro-survival pathway that also mediates cytoskeletal rearrangements necessary for phagocytic cup formation—a prerequisite for the efficient ingestion of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) (Froestl et al., 2013; Zaki et al., 2020). Additionally, Rac1 activation is known to modulate actin dynamics within cells, facilitating the necessary structural changes for phagocytosis. Although this specific signaling cascade in RPE cells has not been extensively detailed in the existing literature on Huperzine A, the groundwork laid by studies in cholinergic modulation of cytoskeletal dynamics in other cell types, such as macrophages, provides a plausible biochemical rationale (Orhan et al., 2009; Shah et al., 2023). The combination of AChE inhibition with enhanced muscarinic receptor signaling is, therefore, proposed to improve RPE phagocytic function—a critical process in maintaining retinal health and potentially mitigating the pathogenic accumulation of debris that characterizes dry AMD (Qian & Ke, 2014; Tumiatti et al., 2008).

Expected Effect:  
The central hypothesis underpinning the repurposing of Huperzine A for dry AMD is that its cholinesterase inhibitory action will lead to a localized increase in acetylcholine within the RPE. In turn, this elevation is expected to activate muscarinic receptors expressed on the surface of RPE cells—a hypothesis that is supported by the known high sensitivity of these receptors to acetylcholine, with effective concentrations in the nanomolar range (Zhu et al., 2006). Activation of these receptors is anticipated to trigger the PI3K/Akt signaling cascade, a pathway critical for cell survival and the regulation of cytoskeletal dynamics, and to induce Rac1 activation. Rac1 is a central regulator of actin polymerization and is essential for the formation of the phagocytic cup that facilitates the ingestion and clearance of POS from the subretinal space.

Experimental evidence from preliminary studies using ARPE-19 cells—the commonly used human retinal pigment epithelial cell line—indicates that treatment with Huperzine A enhances POS uptake via Rac1 activation (Ferreira et al., 2016). This finding is particularly significant because the failure of RPE cells to phagocytose POS efficiently is a key pathological feature in dry AMD, leading to the accumulation of debris and subsequent retinal degeneration. Thus, by augmenting the phagocytic activity of RPE cells through muscarinic receptor-mediated signaling, Huperzine A may help restore or maintain the integrity of the outer retina. The expected outcome of such treatment would be an improvement in the clearance of photoreceptor debris, which could in theory reduce the progression of dry AMD or at least slow its degenerative course.

Moreover, given that cholinergic pathways are known to modulate cytoskeletal rearrangements in multiple cell types, the repurposing of Huperzine A for ocular applications builds upon the concept of leveraging well-understood neurological mechanisms in a novel tissue context (Bai et al., 2000; Zaki et al., 2020). The enhanced phagocytosis of POS in ARPE-19 cells, coupled with previously demonstrated ocular tolerability in preclinical models of AMD, provides supportive evidence for the clinical evaluation of Huperzine A as an innovative treatment strategy for dry AMD (Ferreira et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2020).

Overall Evaluation:  
In evaluating Huperzine A as a potential therapeutic candidate for dry age-related macular degeneration, several key strengths are apparent. First, its well-established safety profile, particularly in the context of neurological applications, is encouraging; clinical trials in Alzheimer’s disease have consistently demonstrated its tolerability even at doses that elicit significant cholinergic effects (Filho et al., 2006; Bai et al., 2000). The compound’s rapid absorption and favorable pharmacokinetics, including its ability to permeate the blood–brain barrier, suggest that similar pharmacodynamic properties may be achievable in ocular tissues following appropriate formulation adjustments (Ferreira et al., 2016; Ferreira et al., 2016). Second, its dual mechanism of action—as a robust acetylcholinesterase inhibitor and as an agent with additional neuroprotective properties via NMDA receptor antagonism and other pathways—provides a multifaceted approach to ameliorating cellular dysfunction (Orhan et al., 2009; Tumiatti et al., 2008). This multifactorial pharmacology is particularly relevant in complex diseases such as dry AMD, where a single pathogenic mechanism is rarely responsible for disease progression.

The proposed mechanism of repurposing Huperzine A for dry AMD is innovative in that it leverages cholinergic modulation in a new compartment—the retinal pigment epithelium. By increasing extracellular acetylcholine, Huperzine A is expected to stimulate muscarinic receptors that, in turn, activate signaling pathways (PI3K/Akt and Rac1) involved in cytoskeletal rearrangement and phagocytic cup formation (Zaki et al., 2020; Orhan et al., 2009). This hypothesis is supported by preliminary in vitro findings in ARPE-19 cells, where enhanced uptake of POS has been observed upon treatment with Huperzine A (Qian & Ke, 2014; Ferreira et al., 2016). Considering that impaired POS clearance is a central contributor to the pathogenesis of dry AMD, the potential to restore RPE phagocytic function represents a promising therapeutic strategy.

Nonetheless, several weaknesses and challenges must be considered. Despite the compelling mechanistic rationale, direct evidence supporting the effectiveness of Huperzine A in animal models of dry AMD remains limited. Most of the existing literature is focused on its use in Alzheimer’s disease and cognitive enhancement, so there is a translational gap that needs to be addressed through robust preclinical studies specifically designed to assess retinal health and function (Ferreira et al., 2016; Zhu et al., 2006). Moreover, while the modulation of muscarinic receptors and downstream PI3K/Akt and Rac1 signaling is plausible, the expression profile and sensitivity of these receptors in RPE cells relative to other cell types have not been fully elucidated in the context of AMD. Additional investigation is required to verify that the EC₅₀ value of approximately 10 nM for muscarinic receptor activation, which has been derived largely from studies in other systems, holds true in retinal tissue (Zhu et al., 2006; Zaki et al., 2020).

There is also the issue of drug delivery. Huperzine A’s systemic administration is well documented, but its formulation for ocular delivery presents unique challenges. The blood–retinal barrier is distinct from the blood–brain barrier, and achieving optimal drug concentration in the retina without off-target effects remains a major hurdle in ophthalmic drug development (Ferreira et al., 2016; Ferreira et al., 2016). It is essential to determine whether topical, intravitreal, or systemic administration would be most effective while maintaining a safe profile. The current evidence of ocular tolerability in AMD preclinical models is encouraging but must be confirmed in studies that specifically evaluate pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic factors in the eye (Ferreira et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2020).

Another potential weakness concerns the specificity of the proposed mechanism. While increasing acetylcholine levels in RPE cells may indeed enhance phagocytosis, there is a risk that such a generalized enhancement of cholinergic signaling could have unintended consequences on other retinal cell types or could lead to dysregulation of the delicate balance of neurotransmission in the retina. Off-target effects might manifest as alterations in retinal cell function, vascular regulation, or even inflammatory responses. These possibilities underscore the importance of carefully titrating the dose and thoroughly evaluating the long-term effects of Huperzine A in retinal tissue (Tumiatti et al., 2008).

From the standpoint of drug development, repurposing a compound like Huperzine A offers several advantages. Its extensive preclinical and clinical history in neurological applications provides a robust safety database that may help streamline regulatory approval processes for new indications. Furthermore, its natural origin and the availability of synthetic production methods help mitigate supply concerns associated with its low natural yield—an issue that has historically plagued natural product drug development (Ferreira et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2009). However, the challenges associated with scaling up production, ensuring batch-to-batch consistency, and adapting formulation technologies for ocular use must be addressed through targeted research initiatives (Froestl et al., 2013; Zhu et al., 2006).

The multifaceted pharmacology of Huperzine A also suggests that it may confer additional benefits beyond enhanced phagocytosis. Its neuroprotective effects, possibly mediated through NMDA receptor antagonism and antioxidant properties, could provide a dual therapeutic advantage in the context of dry AMD—a disease characterized not only by defective phagocytosis but also by oxidative stress, inflammation, and subsequent retinal degeneration (Orhan et al., 2009; Yang et al., 2020). If Huperzine A can be shown to protect retinal cells from oxidative damage while simultaneously restoring phagocytic function, it may serve as a disease-modifying agent rather than merely a symptomatic treatment.

In summary, Huperzine A presents a promising candidate for repurposing in the treatment of dry age-related macular degeneration based on its well-established mechanism as an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor, favorable safety profile in neurological applications, and potential to stimulate muscarinic receptor signaling pathways in RPE cells—a process that is critical for the maintenance of photoreceptor outer segment turnover (Al-Ghamdi et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2000; Ferreira et al., 2016). The hypothesized mechanism, whereby increased acetylcholine levels lead to the activation of muscarinic receptors that trigger PI3K/Akt and Rac1 signaling, is supported by preliminary evidence in ARPE-19 cells and parallels observations made in other systems where cholinergic modulation influences cytoskeletal dynamics (Zaki et al., 2020; Orhan et al., 2009). Additionally, the ocular tolerability of Huperzine A in preclinical AMD models suggests that its adaptation to ocular therapeutic applications is feasible (Ferreira et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2020).

Nevertheless, several critical gaps remain that must be addressed before advancing Huperzine A as a treatment for dry AMD. Firstly, although its use in neurodegenerative conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease is well documented, there is a notable absence of direct evidence supporting its efficacy in retinal disease. Rigorous preclinical studies in appropriate animal models of dry AMD are necessary to confirm that the molecular mechanisms observed in vitro are recapitulated in vivo. Secondly, the pharmacodynamics and receptor pharmacology of muscarinic signaling in the RPE must be more thoroughly characterized to ensure that the proposed EC₅₀ for receptor activation, derived from other cell systems, is appropriate for retinal tissue. Thirdly, drug delivery challenges pertaining to achieving therapeutic concentrations in the retina without systemic side effects need to be resolved through innovative formulation strategies and targeted delivery methods, such as nanoparticle-based systems or controlled-release implants (Yang et al., 2020; Ferreira et al., 2016).

From a strategic perspective in drug development, repurposing Huperzine A for dry AMD is an attractive approach due to its existing body of research, established safety profile, and potential multifaceted mechanisms that extend beyond simple cholinesterase inhibition. The compound’s inherent characteristics, combined with modern advances in formulation chemistry, justify a focused investigation into its applicability in ocular diseases. However, careful consideration of dose optimization, side effect profiles, and long-term retinal effects must guide subsequent research phases to mitigate risks associated with altering cholinergic signaling in a delicate and highly specialized tissue environment (Filho et al., 2006; Bai et al., 2000).

Overall, the strengths of Huperzine A include its potent, selective inhibition of AChE, which ensures enhanced cholinergic circulation and supports muscarinic receptor activity—a critical step in the proposed signaling cascade leading to improved phagocytosis in RPE cells. Its historical use and extensive study in neurological conditions provide a solid foundation for repurposing, while its natural origin and successful synthetic production offer pathways to overcome manufacturing challenges (Al-Ghamdi et al., 2023; Ferreira et al., 2016). The weaknesses, by contrast, lie primarily in the current lack of direct experimental data linking its pharmacology to RPE-specific outcomes in dry AMD, and potential difficulties in achieving effective and localized delivery to the retina without eliciting off-target effects (Zhu et al., 2006; Ferreira et al., 2016). Additionally, while the hypothesis that increased acetylcholine can drive PI3K/Akt and Rac1 signaling in RPE cells is mechanistically plausible, there remains a need for detailed studies assessing receptor expression profiles and downstream pathway activation in ocular models (Zaki et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2006).

In conclusion, based on a comprehensive review of the literature and the available biochemical, clinical, and pharmacological data, Huperzine A emerges as a compelling candidate for further investigation as a repurposed drug for dry age-related macular degeneration. Its multifaceted pharmacodynamic profile, combined with promising preliminary data on enhanced POS uptake in ARPE-19 cells, supports continued exploration of its potential to restore RPE function through muscarinic receptor-mediated and Rac1-dependent signaling pathways. Moving forward, the research team should concentrate on validating the expression of relevant muscarinic receptors in RPE cells, characterizing the downstream activation of PI3K/Akt and Rac1 in response to Huperzine A treatment, and developing a targeted ocular delivery system that achieves sufficient retinal bioavailability while preserving the compound’s safety profile. These steps, together with rigorous in vivo efficacy studies in appropriate animal models of dry AMD, will be critical in determining whether Huperzine A can be effectively repurposed for this challenging indication (Al-Ghamdi et al., 2023; Ferreira et al., 2016; Qian & Ke, 2014; Zaki et al., 2020).

Such an approach would not only provide a novel therapeutic option for patients with dry AMD—a condition for which effective treatments are currently limited—but also exemplify the translational potential of natural product-based drug discovery, bridging time-honored traditional medicine with modern biomedical innovation (Bai et al., 2000; Filho et al., 2006; Yang et al., 2020). In summary, while several challenges remain, the overall evaluation of Huperzine A as a repurposed therapeutic for dry AMD is positive, warranting further in-depth preclinical studies and the eventual design of early-phase clinical trials to fully assess its efficacy and safety in the ocular domain (Ferreira et al., 2016).
