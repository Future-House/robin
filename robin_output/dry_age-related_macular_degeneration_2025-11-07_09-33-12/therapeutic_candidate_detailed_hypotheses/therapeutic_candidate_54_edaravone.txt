Proposal for Edaravone (Free radical scavenger; Mitsubishi Tanabe Pharma)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Edaravone (3-methyl-1-phenyl-2-pyrazolin-5-one, also known as MCI-186 or Radicut) is a synthetic small-molecule free radical scavenger that was originally discovered and developed for its neuroprotective properties. Developed by Mitsubishi Tanabe Pharma, edaravone belongs to the class of pyrazolone derivatives that have potent antioxidant activities. Its design allows it to function in both aqueous and lipid environments by donating electrons to a range of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and converting these free radicals into an inactive metabolite (2-oxo-3-(phenylhydrazono)-butanoic acid). The chemical structure of edaravone confers low molecular weight and high diffusibility, making it well-suited for rapid cellular uptake and efficient interaction with intracellular free radicals. This property has been exploited in its established clinical use in acute ischemic stroke, and its physicochemical characteristics have also encouraged exploration in other CNS and ocular conditions. The free radical scavenger class of compounds has a long history of use in protecting tissues from oxidative damage, and edaravone, in particular, has been shown to mitigate the destructive effects of excessive ROS generation—a process implicated in numerous degenerative conditions (Masuda et al., 2017; Imai et al., 2010).

Therapeutic History:  
Edaravone has a well-documented clinical history primarily in the management of acute ischemic stroke in Japan, where it was approved in 2001 based on its ability to reduce infarct size and improve neurological outcomes. Its established safety profile in human neurological diseases, including modern trials in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), underlines its potential for systemic administration. Over the years, extensive preclinical investigations have demonstrated that edaravone confers significant neuroprotection by lowering ROS levels, preventing lipid peroxidation, and inhibiting apoptosis in stressed tissue. Although edaravone’s approved indications do not include dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD), its neuroprotective and antioxidant properties have been tested in various retinal disease models. For example, studies in animal models of light-induced photoreceptor degeneration, retinal detachment, and even laser-induced choroidal neovascularization have consistently shown that edaravone can reduce oxidative damage, suppress inflammatory cascades, and protect retinal neurons (Imai et al., 2010; Roh et al., 2011). While these models are not direct representations of dry AMD, they share key pathophysiological features with dry AMD, such as high oxidative stress in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and photoreceptor degeneration. Additionally, evidence from in vitro studies using RPE cells supports the idea that edaravone can lower intracellular ROS and thereby improve cell survival under oxidative challenge (Abokyi et al., 2020; Masuda et al., 2017). This history of broad-based use in conditions where excessive ROS plays a central role forms the rationale for repurposing edaravone for dry AMD, a disease in which chronic oxidative stress is a central pathogenic factor.

Mechanism of Action:  
Edaravone exerts its protective effects primarily through its potent antioxidant activity. At the molecular level, it scavenges a variety of ROS—including hydroxyl radicals, superoxide anions, and hydrogen peroxide—by donating an electron to these reactive species. This electron donation converts active free radicals into less harmful molecules and leads to the formation of an inactive end‐product, thereby interrupting the self‐propagating chain reactions of lipid peroxidation (Imai et al., 2010; Masuda et al., 2017). Such oxidative reactions are known to damage cellular components including lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids.

In the context of the retinal pigment epithelium, which is characterized by a high rate of oxidative metabolism and exposure to intense light, ROS accumulation has several deleterious consequences. One key aspect of dry AMD pathogenesis is the oxidative inactivation of lysosomal enzymes, particularly cathepsin D, which is essential for the degradation of photoreceptor outer segments (POS). Under conditions of excessive ROS, lysosomal membranes become compromised by lipid peroxidation, resulting in increased lysosomal pH and subsequent inactivation of cathepsin D. This inefficiency in lysosomal digestion leads to the accumulation of undigested POS and lipofuscin—a process that further exacerbates cellular dysfunction in the RPE (Klettner et al., 2013).

Edaravone’s capacity to scavenge ROS is therefore hypothesized to maintain the functional integrity of lysosomes in RPE cells. By reducing the oxidative burden, edaravone can help preserve the acidic environment (low pH) required for optimal cathepsin D activity. Additionally, oxidative stress is known to impair autophagic flux in RPE cells, as evidenced by alterations in the conversion of LC3-I to its lipidated form LC3-II, which is a marker for autophagosome formation. Enhanced LC3-II flux is associated with efficient autophagy—a process crucial for the clearance of damaged proteins and organelles. By mitigating ROS, edaravone is expected to promote proper LC3-II flux and autophagic activity, further contributing to the clearance of POS debris and damaged organelles in RPE cells (Masuda et al., 2017; Pinelli et al., 2023).

Moreover, several studies have indicated that edaravone can modulate intracellular signaling pathways associated with oxidative stress. For instance, it has been shown to reduce the phosphorylation of stress-activated kinases such as p38 and JNK, both of which are activated in response to elevated ROS levels. Inhibition of these pathways may contribute not only to decreased apoptotic signaling but also to the stabilization of lysosomal function and autophagic processes (Imai et al., 2010; Roh et al., 2011). Overall, the molecular interactions of edaravone—scavenging of free radicals, preservation of lysosomal pH, protection of critical proteases like cathepsin D, and restoration of autophagic flux—form the basis for its proposed repurposing in dry AMD.

Expected Effect:  
The primary expected effect of edaravone in the context of dry AMD is a reduction of oxidative stress within the RPE, thereby preventing the cascade of events that lead to lysosomal dysfunction and defective POS digestion. RPE cells are responsible for the cyclic phagocytosis of POS, and this process is heavily reliant on intact lysosomal function. Under oxidative stress, the lysosomal pH can drift toward alkalinity, which inactivates cathepsin D and impairs the degradation of phagocytosed material. By scavenging ROS, edaravone is anticipated to maintain the acidic environment of lysosomes, thus preserving the enzymatic activity of cathepsin D. This restoration of lysosomal function should translate into improved clearance of POS and reduce the buildup of lipofuscin and drusen—hallmarks of dry AMD pathology (Klettner et al., 2013).

In addition to its lysosome-stabilizing effects, edaravone is expected to enhance autophagic flux in RPE cells. The conversion of LC3-I to LC3-II is an essential step in autophagosome formation, and efficient autophagic flux is critical for the removal of damaged organelles and protein aggregates. Experimental evidence suggests that excessive ROS impairs this flux, leading to the accumulation of dysfunctional autophagosomes and cellular debris. By reducing oxidative stress, edaravone may help restore LC3-II flux and normal autophagic degradation pathways, thereby contributing to improved cellular homeostasis (Pinelli et al., 2023).

From a cellular assay perspective, treating dry AMD RPE cultures with edaravone is expected to result in measurable decreases in intracellular ROS markers such as 8-hydroxy-2-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) and 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE). Simultaneously, preservation of lysosomal pH would likely be observable via pH-sensitive fluorescence probes, and enhanced cathepsin D activity could be confirmed through enzyme assays. Furthermore, Western blot analysis might reveal a normalization of LC3-II levels, indicating improved autophagic flux. The overall effect would be increased phagocytic capacity of the RPE cells and a reduction in cellular stress markers, ultimately leading to enhanced retinal cell survival and function (Abokyi et al., 2020; Pinelli et al., 2023).

An additional expected benefit stems from the potential for optimizing ocular delivery. Intravitreal formulations—either as free drug or best yet, as encapsulated in liposomal vehicles—have been shown to achieve therapeutic antioxidant levels in the retina, ensuring that effective concentrations of edaravone reach the RPE. This approach bypasses systemic limitations and can protect against local oxidative damage directly, offering the possibility to sustain long-term improvements in POS digestion and phagocytic functions in dry AMD (Hironaka et al., 2011; Masuda et al., 2017).

Overall Evaluation:  
Edaravone emerges as a promising repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD based on a strong mechanistic rationale and an established track record in oxidative stress mitigation. Among its primary strengths is its potent free radical scavenging activity, which underpins its ability to counteract the oxidative stress that is central to the pathogenesis of dry AMD. Its chemical properties—and the fact that it has been used safely in the treatment of acute ischemic stroke and ALS—lend significant weight to its potential for repurposing in retinal diseases (Imai et al., 2010; Masuda et al., 2017).

A notable strength is the dual benefit edaravone is expected to offer: not only does it reduce ROS levels, but it also helps to preserve critical lysosomal functions by maintaining an optimal acidic pH, thereby securing cathepsin D activity. This is particularly important because lysosomal degradation of POS is an essential process in RPE cells, and its disruption is a key driver of dry AMD progression (Klettner et al., 2013). Similarly, by promoting normal LC3-II flux, edaravone supports the autophagic machinery required for cellular clearance, further protecting the RPE from the accumulation of toxic waste products (Pinelli et al., 2023).

The preclinical literature provides substantive evidence of edaravone’s efficacy in various models of retinal degeneration. Studies in animal models of retinal detachment, photoreceptor cell death, and experimentally induced oxidative damage all report protective effects following edaravone treatment, including reductions in markers of apoptosis and oxidative stress, as well as improvements in cell survival and retinal morphology (Imai et al., 2010; Roh et al., 2011). In several of these studies, the protective effects of edaravone were enhanced when delivered via liposomal formulations, suggesting that optimized ocular delivery systems can overcome inherent pharmacokinetic challenges and achieve sustained retinal protection (Hironaka et al., 2011).

However, several potential weaknesses must be considered. First, while the underlying rationale for edaravone’s use in dry AMD is robust, direct evidence demonstrating its efficacy in dry AMD models is still limited. Most existing studies have focused on acute retinal damage models such as light-induced degeneration or retinal detachment rather than on the chronic degenerative process observed in dry AMD. Consequently, the specific impact of edaravone on the inactivation of cathepsin D, modulation of lysosomal pH, and restoration of LC3-II flux in RPE cells from AMD patients has not been comprehensively validated (Pinelli et al., 2023; Abokyi et al., 2020).

Furthermore, the optimal dosing regimen and long-term safety profile of edaravone for ocular use remain to be determined. Some studies have noted that while lower doses of edaravone provide neuroprotection, higher doses may introduce toxicity, particularly in the delicate context of retinal cells (Imai et al., 2010; Roh et al., 2011). Thus, careful titration and formulation development—potentially through intravitreal or liposomal delivery—will be essential to harness its therapeutic benefits without incurring adverse effects.

Another potential limitation lies in the multifactorial nature of dry AMD. Although oxidative stress is a driving force in the disease, the pathogenesis of dry AMD is also influenced by dysregulated complement activation, chronic inflammation, genetic predispositions, and metabolic disturbances. An intervention solely targeting ROS may not sufficiently address these other contributing factors. Therefore, edaravone might need to be used as part of a combination therapy strategy in order to achieve meaningful clinical outcomes in dry AMD patients (Jarrett & Boulton, 2012; Klettner et al., 2013).

In summary, edaravone is a scientifically attractive candidate for repurposing in dry AMD therapy. Its well-characterized antioxidant properties, combined with the potential to preserve lysosomal enzyme activity and autophagic flux in RPE cells, provide a mechanistic rationale that directly addresses key pathogenetic mechanisms in dry AMD. The compound’s extensive preclinical evidence in retinal models and established systemic safety profile further support its potential utility. However, the transition from acute retinal injury models to the chronic, multifaceted pathology of dry AMD requires additional focused studies. Specifically, further research is needed to validate its effects on cathepsin D activity, lysosomal pH regulation, and LC3-II flux within the context of RPE and to define the optimal dosing and delivery methods for sustained ocular protection. Given these considerations, edaravone merits further preclinical evaluation in dry AMD models and, if promising, could progress into clinical trials as an adjunct or combination therapy for this major cause of vision loss in the elderly (Abokyi et al., 2020; Imai et al., 2010; Masuda et al., 2017; Pinelli et al., 2023; Roh et al., 2011).
