Proposal for BTB06584 (V-ATPase inhibitor; Tocris Cat. No. 5208)

Below is our comprehensive evaluation of BTB06584 as a repurposed drug candidate for dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD).

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
BTB06584 is described as a V‐ATPase inhibitor available commercially (e.g., Tocris Cat. No. 5208) that is purported to act by selectively targeting the V₀ subunit of the vacuolar proton pump. V‐ATPase is a large multisubunit enzyme complex consisting of a cytosolic V₁ domain (responsible for ATP hydrolysis) and a membrane‐embedded V₀ domain (which translocates protons) that together acidify intracellular compartments such as lysosomes. BTB06584 appears to belong to the class of small molecules developed to modulate lysosomal acidification and, by extension, lysosome‐dependent degradative processes. Although several classes of V‐ATPase inhibitors (for example, the plecomacrolides bafilomycin A₁ and concanamycin A, as well as benzolactone enamides and others) have been extensively characterized as experimental probes, BTB06584 is postulated to be unique in its relatively selective action on the V₀ sector. This selectivity is important because it offers the theoretical possibility of “partial” inhibition rather than the broad, complete blockade observed with classical inhibitors that are often associated with high toxicity (Shine et al., 2014, p. 7; Santos-Pereira et al., 2021, pp. 21–23). In many published studies, V‐ATPase inhibitors have served as critical biochemical tools to dissect lysosomal pH regulation and phagosome degradation, especially in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells, where impaired acidification is linked to cellular dysfunction in AMD (Shine et al., 2014, pp. 4–7). No detailed synthesis or discovery history has been provided in the existing context, but BTB06584 is assumed to be a synthetic agent designed in line with modern small‐molecule drug development paradigms targeting the V₀ domain.

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, V‐ATPase inhibitors have been widely used as experimental probes to study lysosomal acidification and related cellular processes. Classical inhibitors such as bafilomycin A₁ and concanamycin A have been employed in many in vitro and in vivo studies to raise lysosomal pH, alter autophagy and phagocytosis, and to trigger apoptosis in sensitive cell types (Shine et al., 2014, p. 7). In ocular research, V‐ATPase inhibition—albeit through these broader agents—has been demonstrated to impair photoreceptor outer segment (POS) degradation in RPE cells, which serves as an experimental proxy for early AMD‐related pathology (Shine et al., 2014, pp. 4–7; Valapala et al., 2016, pp. 4–6). Moreover, genetic models in zebrafish and mice further underscore the importance of proper V‐ATPase function in retinal homeostasis (Valapala et al., 2016, pp. 4–6; Sinha et al., 2016, pp. 3–5). However, to date, the literature available in our context does not report any prior clinical or preclinical use of BTB06584 specifically for treating dry AMD or for modulating lysosomal pH in RPE cells; indeed, several excerpts explicitly note that BTB06584 is not mentioned by name in the primary‐literature reports on V‐ATPase inhibitors (Santos-Pereira et al., 2021, pp. 23–24; Shine et al., 2014, pp. 1–4). In other words, while the class of V‐ATPase inhibitors has been widely explored—primarily in cancer and osteoporosis research (Holliday, 2017, pp. 4–6; Markitantova & Simirskii, 2025, pp. 9–11)—there is no direct record that BTB06584 has been used clinically or in animal models related to macular degeneration. This gap in the therapeutic history means that any proposed repurposing effort must contend with a virtual absence of detailed preclinical data in AMD‐relevant systems.

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, V‐ATPase functions as an ATP‐driven proton pump essential for maintaining the acidic pH of endolysosomal compartments. Lysosomal enzymes, including cathepsin D, require an optimal acidic environment (typically around pH 4.5) for their catalytic function (Shine et al., 2014, p. 7; Shine et al., 2014, pp. 4–7). Disruption of V‐ATPase activity, whether pharmacologically by agents such as bafilomycin A₁ or via genetic manipulation (for example, loss of βA3/A1‐crystallin, which is known to modulate V‐ATPase function), leads to a rise in lysosomal pH, reduced activity of acid‐dependent proteases, and impaired degradation of photoreceptor outer segments (Valapala et al., 2016, pp. 4–6; Valapala et al., 2016, pp. 1–4). In contrast to complete inhibition—which often results in a strong, detrimental rise in pH—the hypothesis for BTB06584 is that it will “partially” inhibit the V‐ATPase. The goal would be to fine‐tune the activity of the enzyme so that instead of producing a supraphysiologic pH (as with full inhibition), the lysosomal environment is restored to an optimal acidic set point (around pH 4.5) that promotes maximal cathepsin D activity and efficient POS clearance (Shine et al., 2014, pp. 4–7; Sinha et al., 2016, pp. 3–5). Structural studies of V‐ATPase have shown that selective targeting of subunit isoforms, particularly those within the V₀ sector, can influence not only proton pumping but also other functions such as membrane fusion during phagosome–lysosome fusion events (Santos-Pereira et al., 2021, pp. 21–23; Shine et al., 2014, pp. 1–4). If BTB06584 indeed exhibits specificity for the V₀ subunit—and perhaps even for particular isoforms expressed in RPE—it might exert a modulatory effect that differs from the broad and often toxic inhibition seen with compounds like bafilomycin A₁. However, as noted in the literature summaries, there is currently no detailed primary data regarding the binding kinetics, structural interaction, or precise molecular pharmacology of BTB06584 (Santos-Pereira et al., 2021, pp. 23–24; Santos-Pereira et al., 2021, pp. 26–28). In other words, while the proposed mechanism is logically consistent with our understanding of V‐ATPase biology, it remains unsubstantiated by direct experimental evidence for this particular compound.

Expected Effect:  
In the context of dry AMD, RPE dysfunction is characterized, in part, by altered lysosomal pH and impaired degradation of POS, leading to the accumulation of lipofuscin and subsequent oxidative stress and cellular demise (Shine et al., 2014, pp. 4–7; Valapala et al., 2014, pp. 16–17). The central hypothesis is that dysregulated V‐ATPase activity in AMD RPE leads to lysosomal pH drift—either excessive alkalinization due to inhibition by A2E or a failure to maintain optimal acidity—and that this pH imbalance impacts cathepsin D activity. Cathepsin D is optimally active at a pH of around 4.5, and even a modest deviation toward alkalinity (for example, pH 5.0) can reduce its activity by up to 80% (Valapala et al., 2016, pp. 1–4; Shine et al., 2014, p. 7). BTB06584 is expected to “partially” inhibit V‐ATPase in a manner that normalizes lysosomal pH without “shutting off” the proton pump entirely. In RPE cells, particularly in A2E-treated ARPE-19 assays, such a fine-tuning effect could restore an optimal acidic environment, thereby reactivating cathepsin D and other hydrolases; this, in turn, would facilitate proper degradation of ingested photoreceptor outer segments and reduce the buildup of undigested material and associated autofluorescence (Sinha et al., 2016, pp. 3–5; Valapala et al., 2014, pp. 16–17). Importantly, the candidate’s hypothesized selective action on the V₀ subunit—if proven—aligns with evidence that manipulating particular V‐ATPase subunit isoforms can affect not only proton pumping but also membrane fusion events that are essential for proper lysosomal function (Santos-Pereira et al., 2021, pp. 21–23; Shine et al., 2014, pp. 4–7). Given that the RPE expresses specific V‐ATPase isoforms (for example, different V₀ a isoforms) and that genetic studies have linked loss of V‐ATPase activity to defects in POS degradation (Valapala et al., 2016, pp. 4–6; Shine et al., 2014, p. 7), the restoration of lysosomal homeostasis through a “partial inhibitor” like BTB06584 could theoretically improve the clearance of POS. However, it must be emphasized that the expected effect of normalizing pH from a suboptimal or variable level to precisely 4.5 is a very delicate modulation – one that must be demonstrated through rigorous in vitro assays in ARPE-19 cells (or better yet, in iPSC-derived RPE models) followed by in vivo animal studies.

Overall Evaluation:  
The proposed strategy of repurposing BTB06584 for dry AMD is innovative in that it aims to address a central pathological feature—lysosomal dysfunction—by modulating, rather than completely inhibiting, the V‐ATPase. The strength of the candidate lies in its potential for selective inhibition of the V₀ subunit, which is critical given that generalized inhibition via broad-spectrum compounds (like bafilomycin A₁) has been associated with significant toxicity and dysfunction in multiple cell types (Shine et al., 2014, p. 7; Shine et al., 2014, pp. 1–4). Furthermore, the concept of “fine-tuning” lysosomal pH to optimize cathepsin D activity is appealing because it targets the underlying mechanism that controls the degradation of POS in RPE cells—a process known to be perturbed in AMD (Shine et al., 2014, pp. 4–7; Valapala et al., 2016, pp. 1–4).  

Nonetheless, there are notable weaknesses. Chief among these is the absence of direct experimental evidence supporting the efficacy of BTB06584 in RPE models or animal models of dry AMD. None of the available literature excerpts provide primary data regarding its chemical characterization, mechanism of action in retinal cells, or any preclinical studies using BTB06584 (Santos-Pereira et al., 2021, pp. 23–24; Shine et al., 2014, pp. 1–4). Consequently, while the hypothesis is congruent with the general concept that selective modulation of V‐ATPase can restore lysosomal function, the candidate remains largely unvalidated in the context of ocular therapeutics. In addition, there is an inherent risk associated with modulating a central enzyme such as V-ATPase: even partial inhibition might affect other vital cellular processes, including autophagy, phagosome–lysosome fusion, and even neurotransmitter release in neural tissues (Santos-Pereira et al., 2021, pp. 21–23; Shine et al., 2014, pp. 1–4). Moreover, the narrow therapeutic window required to normalize acidification (without overshooting into detrimental alkalinization) poses significant challenges; this alone necessitates careful dose-response profiling in multiple models before any clinical translation can be considered.  

Overall, while the theoretical framework supporting BTB06584 is robust in terms of targeting a mechanism that is central to RPE pathology in dry AMD, the lack of direct data leaves critical gaps. At this stage, BTB06584 represents a promising but highly speculative candidate. To advance its development, we recommend that rigorous in vitro studies (using ARPE-19 cells and iPSC-derived RPE under A2E/lipofuscin stress) be undertaken to characterize its effect on lysosomal pH, cathepsin D activity, and POS degradation. These should be complemented by mechanistic assays—such as assessments of lysosomal fusion events and measurements of autophagic flux—to fully delineate its mode of action. If favorable, subsequent in vivo studies in appropriate AMD models will be essential to evaluate efficacy and systemic toxicity.  

In summary, our overall evaluation is that while the candidate BTB06584 is conceptually attractive because it promises selective modulation of V‐ATPase activity—potentially correcting the lysosomal pH imbalance that underlies defects in POS clearance in dry AMD—the current literature does not supply direct supportive evidence for its use in retinal disease, and its safety/efficacy profile remains unproven. Further preclinical investigation is therefore necessary before considering BTB06584 as a viable therapeutic candidate for dry AMD (Shine et al., 2014, p. 7; Santos-Pereira et al., 2021, pp. 23–24; Shine et al., 2014, pp. 1–4).
