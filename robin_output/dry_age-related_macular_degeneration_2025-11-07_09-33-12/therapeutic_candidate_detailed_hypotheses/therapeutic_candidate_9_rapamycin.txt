Proposal for Rapamycin (mTORC1 inhibitor; LC Laboratories Cat. No. R-5000)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Rapamycin is a naturally derived macrolide initially isolated from the bacterium Streptomyces hygroscopicus and belongs to the class of mTOR (mammalian target of rapamycin) inhibitors. This compound, originally discovered for its immunosuppressive properties, has evolved into a prominent therapeutic candidate across several fields including oncology, immunology, and neurodegeneration. Its chemical derivation and structural complexity allow for potent and selective inhibition of the mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1). Rapamycin is widely used in clinical settings as an immunosuppressant following organ transplantation and has been repurposed for other indications based on its ability to modulate intracellular signaling pathways that regulate cellular growth, survival, and autophagy. As such, its potential application in dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) is based on a body of work indicating that targeting mTORC1 can restore autophagic and lysosomal function in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells—a crucial factor given that RPE dysfunction is central to AMD pathogenesis (Falcão et al., 2024, 2025).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, rapamycin has been extensively used and investigated in various clinical and preclinical settings. Its initial clinical use as an immunosuppressive agent in organ transplant patients laid the groundwork for understanding its mechanisms of action and safety profile in humans. Beyond its immunosuppressive applications, rapamycin has been evaluated for its anti-proliferative effects in several types of cancer and for its capability to extend lifespan in model organisms; these studies underscore the drug’s pleiotropic effects on cellular metabolism and autophagy. Specifically regarding ocular therapeutics, preclinical studies on retinal degenerative diseases such as retinitis pigmentosa and glaucoma have demonstrated that mTOR modulation by rapamycin can exert neuroprotective properties in retinal tissues (Wang et al., 2022; Chang et al., 2022). In the realm of AMD, several investigations have focused on the role of autophagy and lysosomal degradation of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) in RPE cells, where rapamycin has been shown to promote autophagy and decrease the buildup of toxic aggregates, such as lipofuscin (Falcão et al., 2024). Moreover, clinical studies and safety evaluations—in particular, intravitreal injections in non-human primates—have provided encouraging data regarding the ocular safety profile of rapamycin, thus supporting its further exploration as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD (Falcão et al., 2024; Chang et al., 2022).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, rapamycin exerts its effects by binding to the intracellular receptor FKBP12 (FK506-binding protein 12). This drug–FKBP12 complex then interacts with mTORC1, leading to its allosteric inhibition. Under normal anabolic conditions, active mTORC1 phosphorylates several downstream targets, including the transcription factor EB (TFEB). TFEB is sequestered in the cytosol in its phosphorylated state, thus preventing the transcription of genes associated with lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy. When rapamycin inhibits mTORC1, the phosphorylation of TFEB is reduced, permitting its nuclear translocation. Once inside the nucleus, TFEB initiates the transcription of numerous genes involved in autophagy and lysosomal function, thereby enhancing the cell’s capacity to degrade and recycle cellular debris such as accumulated photoreceptor outer segments (Falcão et al., 2024, 2025).

In the context of retinal pigment epithelium, mTORC1 normally integrates growth factor signals, nutrient availability, and stress responses to tightly regulate cellular homeostasis. The inhibition of mTORC1 by rapamycin thus releases the “brake” on autophagy, as evidenced by increased expression of autophagic markers such as LC3-II and lysosomal proteins such as LAMP1. This cascading effect leads to an enhanced intracellular clearance system (phagolysosomal flux), which is particularly critical for RPE cells that are responsible for the daily degradation of shed POS. Several studies have demonstrated that rapamycin-treated RPE cells exhibit improved POS degradation, a reduction in lipofuscin-like granules, and overall restoration of lysosomal function (Falcão et al., 2024). Moreover, the molecular signatures observed in rapamycin-treated cells mimic those seen under conditions of caloric restriction, further supporting the notion that mTORC1 inhibition can activate protective autophagic pathways (Davis, 2015; Falcão et al., 2024). This advanced understanding of rapamycin’s mechanism of action provides a compelling rationale for its application in dry AMD, where the accumulation of toxic protein aggregates and dysregulated autophagy are key pathological features (Falcão et al., 2024).

Expected Effect:  
The central hypothesis driving the evaluation of rapamycin in dry AMD is that its inhibition of mTORC1 will lead to the activation of TFEB by reducing its cytosolic phosphorylation state, thereby promoting nuclear translocation. In iPSC-derived RPE models, this induction of TFEB activity is expected to upregulate a host of autophagy and lysosomal genes, which in turn should enhance the cellular degradation of photoreceptor outer segments—an essential process in maintaining retinal homeostasis (Falcão et al., 2024, 2025). Accordingly, the anticipated in vitro effects upon rapamycin treatment include a robust increase in autophagic flux evidenced by elevated levels of LC3-II and LAMP1, paired with improved processing and clearance of POS debris. This effect is crucial because impaired clearance mechanisms contribute to the accumulation of lipofuscin and other cellular waste materials in RPE cells, both of which are hallmarks of dry AMD pathology (Falcão et al., 2024).

Supporting this expected outcome are observations from previous studies where rapamycin has been associated with a reduction in autofluorescent granules and a restoration of normal cellular clearance dynamics in RPE cells (Falcão et al., 2024). Additionally, safety assessments performed through intravitreal administration in non-human primates have demonstrated that rapamycin can be delivered to the retina with minimal adverse events, thereby underpinning its feasibility for clinical translation (Falcão et al., 2024; Chang et al., 2022). Importantly, the hypothesis that rapamycin can act at IC₅₀ values as low as approximately 0.1 nM in RPE cells further reinforces the potential sensitivity and efficacy of this candidate in modulating the pathway of interest. Given the expression of mTOR, TFEB, LC3, and LAMP1 in RPE cells—and the established role of these proteins in the autophagic and lysosomal clearance of POS—the expected improvement in autophagic turnover should directly translate into enhanced cell function and potentially mitigate the progression of dry AMD (Wang et al., 2022; Intartaglia et al., 2022).

Overall Evaluation:  
Rapamycin emerges as a robust and mechanistically sound therapeutic candidate for dry AMD based on its established ability to inhibit mTORC1 and consequently activate autophagy and lysosomal gene expression through TFEB nuclear translocation. One of the major strengths of this candidate lies in the well-characterized molecular mechanism that connects mTORC1 inhibition with enhanced clearance of POS debris—a process that is fundamentally disrupted in dry AMD. The convergence of multiple lines of evidence, ranging from biochemical assays that show increased LC3-II and LAMP1 expression in rapamycin-treated RPE cells to preclinical safety data in non-human primates, bolsters confidence in its potential utility (Falcão et al., 2024, 2025).

Furthermore, the multifaceted benefits of mTORC1 inhibition extend beyond autophagy induction; rapamycin also offers neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects, both of which are advantageous in the treatment of retinal degenerative diseases (Chang et al., 2022). The leverage of TFEB activation not only addresses the aberrant accumulation of toxic cellular debris but may also promote a broader upregulation of lysosomal biogenesis, thereby preempting further functional decline of RPE cells. These properties make rapamycin a strong candidate for mitigating early events in AMD pathogenesis, particularly in the context of experimental iPSC-RPE models where enhanced phagocytic capacity can be directly measured.

Nevertheless, several challenges and potential weaknesses require careful consideration. Despite its promising mechanism and preclinical rationale, clinical trials evaluating rapamycin in the context of ocular diseases have yielded mixed results, with some studies documenting only marginal improvements in visual function and others noting issues related to the drug’s solubility and delivery (Marchesi et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022). In advanced AMD, where the disease pathology is more complex and multifactorial, rapamycin’s efficacy might be limited, and there is a need for precise dosing strategies to balance mTORC1 inhibition with the preservation of physiological mTOR functions that are crucial for RPE cell survival (Wang et al., 2022; Chang et al., 2022). Additionally, the potential for off-target effects or immunosuppressive consequences linked to systemic exposure—although minimized by intravitreal delivery—necessitates further investigation into long-term safety profiles (Wang et al., 2022).

Another potential limitation is the inherent complexity of autophagy regulation in the RPE, which can be influenced by factors such as oxidative stress, metabolic dysfunction, and age-related changes in lysosomal function. While rapamycin is effective in reactivating autophagic pathways under controlled experimental settings, its impact within the pathogenic environment of a diseased retina, particularly if inflammatory processes are simultaneously active, may be less predictable (Davis, 2015; Falcão et al., 2024). Moreover, the therapeutic window for achieving beneficial rapamycin concentrations without incurring adverse events is not yet fully defined, underscoring the importance of pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic studies specifically tailored to ocular tissues (Falcão et al., 2024).

Taken together, the current body of evidence suggests that rapamycin’s capacity to restore autophagic homeostasis in RPE cells represents a solid mechanistic foundation for its use in dry AMD. Its ability to upregulate autophagy and lysosomal function through TFEB activation directly addresses known defects in the clearance pathways of RPE cells—a critical factor in the pathogenesis of dry AMD. Moreover, the safety data from preclinical intravitreal delivery studies provide additional incentive to advance this candidate further along the translational pipeline. However, given the mixed outcomes observed in some clinical contexts and the challenges related to drug formulation and optimal dosing, further comprehensive studies are warranted to clarify its efficacy in early versus advanced stages of AMD (Mei et al., 2022).

In conclusion, rapamycin as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD shows significant promise from both a mechanistic and translational standpoint. Its well-documented ability to inhibit mTORC1, thereby releasing TFEB and ramping up autophagic and lysosomal gene expression, positions it uniquely to tackle the underpinnings of RPE dysfunction characteristic of dry AMD. While preclinical data support its efficacy and safety with intravitreal administration in non-human primates, challenges remain regarding its formulation, potential off-target effects, and the delineation of its therapeutic window in human subjects. Consequently, while rapamycin’s molecular mechanism and early experimental results are highly encouraging, further studies—especially those that integrate pharmacokinetic profiling, dose optimization, and long-term outcome measurements in relevant in vivo models—are essential before definitive conclusions can be drawn regarding its clinical utility for dry AMD (Mei et al., 2022; Si et al., 2023).

Overall, the strengths of rapamycin include its robust and well-characterized action on mTORC1, its downstream activation of autophagic and lysosomal pathways via TFEB, and supportive preclinical safety data in ocular models. In contrast, its weaknesses lie in the variable efficacy observed in clinical studies, formulation challenges due to low solubility, and the need for careful dosing to avoid potential adverse effects related to systemic immunosuppression. These considerations collectively argue for the continued investigation of rapamycin as a therapeutic option for dry AMD with a focus on optimizing delivery strategies and characterizing its long-term impact on retinal structure and function (Wang et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2025).
