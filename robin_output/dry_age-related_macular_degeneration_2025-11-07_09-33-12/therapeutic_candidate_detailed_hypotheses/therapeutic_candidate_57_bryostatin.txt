Proposal for Bryostatin-1 (PKC modulator; NIH repository)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Bryostatin‐1 is a naturally derived macrolide originally isolated from marine invertebrates and belongs to a class of compounds collectively known as bryostatins. These compounds exhibit potent modulation of protein kinase C (PKC) activity by binding to the enzyme’s diacylglycerol binding site on the regulatory domain. Bryostatin‐1 has been characterized as a partial agonist of PKC and is noted for its ability to both acutely activate and chronically down‐regulate specific PKC isoforms. The molecular architecture of bryostatin‐1 confers a unique binding profile that distinguishes it from phorbol esters; it induces translocation of target PKC isoforms to membranes without eliciting the full tumor‐promoting effects observed with phorbol esters. The compound is synthetically challenging to produce, yet semisynthetic methodologies have enabled its preparation in sufficient quantities for preclinical and early clinical applications. As a member of the marine natural products family, bryostatin‐1 is frequently investigated for its anticancer and neuroprotective properties, and its chemical structure serves as the basis for designing simplified analogs with improved pharmacological profiles (Kollár et al., 2014; Le Tourneau et al., 2007).  

Therapeutic History:  
Bryostatin‐1 has a long preclinical history that spans oncology and neurology. In oncology, bryostatin‐1 has been extensively studied for its role in modulating PKC in various tumor types and has been evaluated in numerous clinical trials addressing B‐cell malignancies and renal cancers. Despite promising preclinical results, its cancer‐directed clinical efficacy has been limited by suboptimal outcomes and adverse side effects such as myalgia. More recently, bryostatin‐1 has been repurposed in neurological contexts. Several studies have demonstrated its neuroprotective capabilities in models of Alzheimer’s disease, where it has shown to improve synaptic function, restore dendritic spine morphology, induce synaptogenesis through the upregulation of brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), and enhance cognitive function. Phase II clinical trials in Alzheimer’s patients have provided translational evidence of its ability to modulate PKCε in the central nervous system (Chen et al., 2024; Nelson et al., 2017). However, a search of clinical trial databases has revealed that no formal clinical trials have been registered evaluating bryostatin‐1 in the setting of macular degeneration, retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) dysfunction, or Rab7 signaling‐related pathways (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.). Although there is evidence supporting bryostatin‐1’s capacity to affect cellular pathways—such as inducing autophagy and promoting PKC‐mediated cell survival—in neuronal and cancer cells, its application to ocular diseases like dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) remains unexplored in the clinical context (Safarpour, 2009; Sarangarajan & Apte, 2005).  

Mechanism of Action:  
Bryostatin‐1 functions primarily as a modulator of protein kinase C, a family of serine/threonine kinases that play central roles in regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and intracellular trafficking. On a molecular level, bryostatin‐1 binds competitively to the diacylglycerol (DAG) binding site located in the C1 domain of PKC isoforms. This binding event leads to the translocation of PKC from the cytosol to membranous compartments, thereby bringing the kinase into close proximity with its substrates. Unlike potent activators such as phorbol esters, bryostatin‐1 acts as a partial agonist. Short‐term exposure results in the activation of specific PKC isoforms, whereas prolonged exposure leads to their down‐regulation via proteasomal degradation. Structural studies have demonstrated that the complex hydrogen‐bond networks formed by the A- and B-rings of bryostatin‐1 contribute significantly to its isoform selectivity and binding affinity (Kollár et al., 2014; Le Tourneau et al., 2007).  

In the context of the hypothesized mechanism for dry AMD, it is proposed that bryostatin‐1 can activate atypical PKC isoforms at very low effective concentrations (EC₅₀ ≈ 0.1 nM), potentially including enzymes that are involved in the regulation of Rab7 signalling. Although bryostatin‐1 has been studied predominantly for its activation of classical (e.g., PKCα, PKCβ) and novel PKC isoforms (e.g., PKCδ, PKCε), several experimental reports suggest that PKC activity regulates various aspects of vesicular trafficking, including phagosome maturation. In phagocytic cell lines, enhanced PKC activation has been linked to improved phagolysosomal fusion, which is essential for the degradation of ingested material. This connection is of particular interest in RPE cells, where the degradation of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) via the phagosome–lysosome pathway is central to retinal homeostasis. The hypothesis extends this mechanism by proposing that the activation of PKC by bryostatin‐1 may lead to the phosphorylation of Rab7 effector proteins, which are known to control the late stages of phagosome maturation and lysosome fusion. Although direct experimental evidence connecting bryostatin‐1‐mediated PKC activation to Rab7 effector phosphorylation in RPE cells is limited, existing literature has established the importance of PKC in regulating phagocytic processes and intracellular vesicle trafficking (Saddala et al., 2020; Sarangarajan & Apte, 2005). Furthermore, mechanistic parallels drawn from studies in neuronal systems—where bryostatin‐1 has been shown to influence autophagic flux and promote synaptic health—support the feasibility of such a cellular response in other highly phagocytic cells like the RPE (Chen et al., 2024).  

Expected Effect:  
Dry AMD is characterized by the dysfunction of RPE cells, which leads to impaired clearance of POS and subsequent accumulation of toxic lipofuscin deposits. RPE dysfunction is associated with disrupted phagosome maturation and lysosomal degradation pathways, contributing to retinal degeneration and vision loss. The therapeutic hypothesis for bryostatin‐1 posits that its administration will enhance the maturation of phagosomes by stimulating PKC isoforms that, in turn, phosphorylate Rab7 effectors. Effective phosphorylation of these effectors is expected to facilitate late phagosome–lysosome fusion, thereby improving the trafficking and degradation of POS. Empirical studies in RPE cells have shown that defects in phagolysosomal fusion lead to the accumulation of nondegradable materials and are closely linked to the pathogenesis of dry AMD (Safarpour, 2009; Sarangarajan & Apte, 2005).  

The candidate mechanism suggests that, upon administration, bryostatin‐1 will rapidly bind to its target PKC isoforms in RPE cells, causing these kinases to translocate to relevant subcellular membranes. This translocation is anticipated to bring PKC in proximity to Rab7 effectors situated on late phagosomes. Activation of these kinases would then promote the phosphorylation of regulatory proteins that control vesicular fusion events. As a result, the impaired phagocytosis observed in dry AMD would be corrected through improved maturation of phagosomes and enhanced fusion with lysosomes. Such a cascade is expected to lead to enhanced clearance of POS debris and, ultimately, a reduction in lipofuscin accumulation, which is central to the pathophysiology of dry AMD. Although supportive evidence for this cascade exists in the context of non‐ocular phagocytic cells and in neuronal models of autophagy activation, it remains imperative to directly evaluate these effects in primary RPE cell cultures or relevant animal models to solidify the connection between PKC activation by bryostatin‐1 and Rab7‐mediated phagosome maturation (Saddala et al., 2020).  

Overall Evaluation:  
Bryostatin‐1 presents an intriguing therapeutic candidate for dry AMD owing to its potent modulation of PKC, a central regulator of intracellular signaling pathways that govern vesicular trafficking, phagocytosis, and autophagy. One of the key strengths of bryostatin‐1 lies in its well‐documented activity in modulating PKC isoforms that are critical in diverse physiological contexts, including neuronal function and cancer cell biology. Its demonstrated neuroprotective efficacy in Alzheimer’s disease clinical studies supports the compound’s translational potential and suggests that its PKC‐modulating effects might be harnessed in alternate tissues such as the retina (Chen et al., 2024). Additionally, the critical role of PKC in promoting phagosome–lysosome fusion has been substantiated in other cell types, which provides a strong theoretical basis for its application in RPE cells where phagocytic clearance is essential for maintaining retinal integrity (Saddala et al., 2020; Safarpour, 2009).

Despite these promising aspects, several challenges and weaknesses must be considered. Foremost, no direct studies to date have evaluated bryostatin‐1 in the context of dry AMD or in RPE cells. Clinical trial databases have not registered any studies for bryostatin‐1 in macular degeneration, underscoring the novelty of this repurposing strategy but also highlighting a significant gap in the literature (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.). The hypothesis that bryostatin‐1 will stimulate atypical PKC isoforms to phosphorylate Rab7 effectors, although plausible, remains largely speculative. While various studies have linked PKC activity to phagosome maturation and autophagy regulation, the precise isoforms relevant to RPE cell function—and whether these isoforms are the same ones activated by bryostatin‐1 in neuronal or cancer models—have not been comprehensively elucidated. Therefore, it will be crucial to determine, through rigorous in vitro assays and animal models, whether the purported EC₅₀ of approximately 0.1 nM is achievable in RPE cells and whether this concentration effectively modifies the Rab7‐related molecular machinery.

Additional considerations include the potential for off‐target effects and systemic toxicity. Past clinical studies of bryostatin‐1, particularly in oncology, have reported adverse effects such as myalgia and thrombocytopenia. However, the pharmacokinetic profile observed in Alzheimer’s trials suggests that the dosing regimens used for neurological disorders might be translatable to ocular delivery with appropriate formulation strategies. Localized ocular delivery, such as intravitreal injection or controlled‐release implants, may enable targeted modulation of PKC in the retina while mitigating systemic exposure and associated adverse events (Chen et al., 2024; Nelson et al., 2017). Moreover, the challenge of achieving adequate drug penetration to the RPE in vivo is nontrivial. Approaches to enhance ocular bioavailability, including nanoparticle encapsulation or conjugation to targeting ligands, should be explored concurrently with the design of preclinical studies to better validate this therapeutic candidate.

From a mechanistic standpoint, the dual nature of bryostatin‐1—characterized by initial activation followed by downregulation of PKC isoforms—necessitates precise temporal control of its pharmacodynamic effects. In the context of phagosome maturation, transient activation may be beneficial to restore proper lysosomal fusion; however, prolonged downregulation might counteract the desired effect. It is therefore important that future studies carefully delineate the dosing regimen (both concentration and administration frequency) required to achieve an optimal therapeutic window in RPE cells. Additionally, the specificity of bryostatin‐1 for PKC isoforms implicated in Rab7 effector phosphorylation must be confirmed, as the RPE expresses a complex array of PKC isoforms that may play distinct roles in cellular homeostasis and stress responses (Kollár et al., 2014; Le Tourneau et al., 2007).

The breadth and diversity of bryostatin‐1’s effects in preclinical models have generated considerable interest, yet its repurposing for dry AMD remains at an early stage of conceptual development. Given that disruption of phagosome maturation in RPE cells is a well‐established contributor to the pathogenesis of dry AMD—and that PKC signaling has been implicated in regulating this process—the rationale for exploring bryostatin‐1 in this indication is scientifically sound. Nonetheless, the transition from a neuroprotective agent in Alzheimer’s disease to an effective modulator of RPE function in dry AMD will depend on new studies that specifically address the cellular context of the retina. In this regard, preliminary investigations using primary RPE cultures or animal models of dry AMD will be critical to validate the hypothesis that PKC activation by bryostatin‐1 can enhance Rab7‐mediated phagolysosomal fusion and improve POS clearance (Saddala et al., 2020; Safarpour, 2009).

In summary, bryostatin‐1 offers both significant promise and notable challenges as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry age‐related macular degeneration. Its ability to modulate PKC in a manner that may restore essential phagolysosomal functions in the RPE positions it as an innovative approach to address a major unmet need in ocular therapeutics. The compound’s successful use in Alzheimer’s disease trials provides encouraging translational evidence of its safety and efficacy in modulating neuronal PKC activity. However, the absence of direct evidence in retinal cells, the uncertainty regarding which specific PKC isoforms are most relevant to RPE phagocytosis, and potential issues related to dosing and systemic toxicity form significant hurdles that must be overcome. Moving forward, it will be critical to conduct comprehensive in vitro studies to assess the effects of bryostatin‐1 on RPE phagosome maturation, coupled with robust in vivo experiments in established dry AMD models. Such studies should focus on confirming the expression and role of the putative target proteins, including Rab7 effectors, and determining whether the activation of these pathways can indeed translate into improved clearance of POS and reduced retinal degeneration (Saddala et al., 2020; ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.; Chen et al., 2024; Sarangarajan & Apte, 2005).

In conclusion, the overall evaluation of bryostatin‐1 as a drug candidate for dry AMD reveals a compound with strong mechanistic rationale and significant translational potential based on its established pharmacological properties in PKC modulation and neuroprotection. The strengths of bryostatin‐1 include its potent activity at nanomolar concentrations, a unique mode of action that distinguishes it from classical PKC activators, and supporting evidence from clinical studies in neurological disorders. Conversely, its weaknesses lie primarily in the absence of direct studies in ocular tissues, uncertainty regarding the specific PKC isoforms involved in RPE phagosome maturation, and potential challenges in achieving localized delivery without systemic side effects. An interdisciplinary approach that integrates detailed molecular studies with innovative drug delivery strategies will be critical in validating bryostatin‐1’s viability for the treatment of dry AMD. Based on the current literature, investing in rigorous preclinical testing to confirm the hypothesized mechanistic link between PKC activation, Rab7 effector phosphorylation, and enhanced phagosome maturation in RPE cells is warranted before progressing to clinical evaluation (Saddala et al., 2020; ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.; Safarpour, 2009; Chen et al., 2024).
