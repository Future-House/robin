Proposal for Brefeldin A (Golgi transport inhibitor; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. B7651)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Brefeldin A (BFA) is a natural product originally isolated in 1958 from Penicillium decumbens, and its discovery marked the beginning of intensive studies on its unique ability to disrupt intracellular protein trafficking (Hung, 2020). Structurally, BFA is a macrolide characterized by a complex fused system comprising a cyclopentane moiety linked to a 13-membered lactone, and this distinctive architecture has spurred extensive synthetic and medicinal chemistry efforts over the past decades (Seehafer et al., 2013; Stierle & Stierle, 2015). By targeting the secretory pathway, BFA acts as a Golgi transport inhibitor, primarily through its binding to a protein complex involving a guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) and the small G protein Arf1. This binding event results in the rapid disassembly of the Golgi apparatus and redistribution of its components back into the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) (Hung, 2020). BFA is therefore classified among natural product inhibitors of intracellular trafficking, a group that has long served as invaluable research tools in cell biology to dissect the mechanisms of vesicular transport and Golgi-ER dynamics. Its broad bioactivities—as an antiviral, antifungal, and antimitotic agent—have principally been exploited in preclinical research rather than clinical therapeutics (Seehafer et al., 2013; Stierle & Stierle, 2015).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, BFA has been employed almost exclusively as a biochemical probe to investigate protein processing, secretion, and Golgi morphology in diverse eukaryotic cells. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that BFA’s ability to stall ER-to-Golgi trafficking can modulate various cellular processes including secretion of cytokines, hormones, and growth factors (Hung, 2020). Its documented effects on blocking transport have been leveraged in cancer cell studies where BFA-induced apoptosis and cell cycle arrest were observed, owing to mislocalization of key signaling molecules (Stierle & Stierle, 2015). Despite these promising cellular effects, the clinical development of BFA as a conventional therapeutic agent has been hindered by issues such as poor bioavailability and significant toxicity with prolonged exposure (Seehafer et al., 2013). Importantly, while BFA has not been directly applied in clinical studies for dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) or any similar degenerative retinal diseases, its mechanistic action on intracellular trafficking, ER stress, and protein secretion renders it an intriguing candidate for repurposing. In the context of AMD, where complement dysregulation and lysosomal dysfunction in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells play a critical pathogenic role, BFA could potentially offer a novel means of attenuating the buildup of complement components and misfolded proteins (Georgiannakis et al., 2015; Cerniauskas et al., 2020). While none of the published literature explicitly documents the use of BFA for AMD therapy, related studies in RPE cellular models have highlighted the importance of complement modulation and lysosomal restoration, establishing a rationale for investigating trafficking inhibitors such as BFA (Cerniauskas et al., 2020).

Mechanism of Action:  
On a molecular level, the mechanism of action for Brefeldin A is well characterized. BFA binds to the complex comprising a catalytic GEF and the small GTPase Arf1, inhibiting the exchange of GDP for GTP on Arf1. This inhibition precludes proper recruitment and coat assembly for vesicle budding at the Golgi membranes, leading to a collapse of the Golgi structure and the retrograde redistribution of Golgi proteins to the ER (Hung, 2020). The immediate consequence of this inhibition is a profound disruption in the secretory pathway, which not only halts the export of proteins but also results in an accumulation of proteins within the ER. In cell types where the regulated secretion of proteins is critical for homeostasis, such as in RPE cells, this effect can have dual ramifications. On the one hand, it may help prevent the secretion of misfolded or aberrantly processed proteins, including complement components that are implicated in local inflammation and lysosomal deposition (Cerniauskas et al., 2020). On the other hand, the blockade of Golgi trafficking may transiently lower the secretion of proteins that otherwise contribute to the local pool of complement proteins which, if misfolded or overproduced, could lead to an increase in intracellular complement activation and subsequent deposition of the membrane attack complex (MAC, particularly C5b-9) within lysosomes (Georgiannakis et al., 2015; Fernandez-Godino & Pierce, 2018). In RPE cells, where robust daily phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) is essential, the maintenance of lysosomal function is critical; overburdening of lysosomes with complement-derived debris has been implicated in the formation of drusen and lipofuscin accumulation, both of which underlie AMD pathology (Cerniauskas et al., 2020). The proposed intervention using BFA therefore leverages its well-established ability to modulate protein trafficking to indirectly reduce the burden of misfolded or overabundant complement proteins on the lysosomal system, thereby potentially restoring cathepsin activity and overall lysosomal function in RPE cells.

Expected Effect:  
The central hypothesis for repurposing BFA in dry AMD is that brief pulses of BFA administration will selectively inhibit the ER-to-Golgi trafficking route in RPE cells, thereby reducing the processing and secretion of misfolded complement proteins. RPE cells are known to contribute to local complement activation through the production and turnover of various complement components; in AMD, aberrant complement regulation leads to increased turnover, formation of complement split products, and the deposition of terminal complement complexes (C5b-9) in the lysosomes (Cerniauskas et al., 2020). The expected outcome of BFA treatment would be a reduction in the export of these misfolded or excessively generated complement proteins, ultimately leading to decreased local complement activation and a reduction in lysosomal overload by MAC proteins. Experimental evidence from studies using compstatin analogues (e.g., Cp40) in Y402H-RPE cell models has shown that complement inhibition can restore lysosomal function and cathepsin activity, thereby reducing lysosome size, diminishing drusen-like deposits, and improving the overall cellular phenotype (Cerniauskas et al., 2020; Georgiannakis et al., 2015). By analogy, transient BFA treatment is anticipated to produce similar beneficial effects on lysosomal physiology in RPE cells, provided that the inhibition is carefully timed to avoid prolonged exposure which could be deleterious (Cerniauskas et al., 2020). Furthermore, because RPE cells are responsible for the daily clearance of photoreceptor outer segments, maintaining an unencumbered lysosomal system is crucial for long-term retinal health. The expected biochemical cascade following BFA administration would therefore include a reduction in ER stress due to diminished protein export, lower secretion of misfolded complement proteins, and a subsequent decrease in complement-mediated lysosomal damage. This in turn should lead to a stabilization or even an improvement in cathepsin levels and activity, promoting a more efficient degradation of cellular debris and restoration of POS clearance (Georgiannakis et al., 2015; Cerniauskas et al., 2020).

Overall Evaluation:  
The potential repurposing of Brefeldin A as a therapeutic agent for dry AMD is innovative and grounded in a detailed understanding of both its mechanism of action and the pathophysiology of AMD. Several strengths underlie this approach. First, BFA has a well-characterized molecular mechanism that directly affects the secretory pathway by inhibiting ER-to-Golgi trafficking. This offers a mechanistic basis for reducing the secretion of misfolded or aberrantly processed complement proteins—the accumulation of which is a central driver of lysosomal dysfunction and local complement activation in RPE cells (Hung, 2020). Second, preclinical research in AMD models has demonstrated that modulating complement activity, whether through direct inhibition of complement components or by enhancing lysosomal function, can reverse pathological phenotypes associated with the disease (Cerniauskas et al., 2020; Georgiannakis et al., 2015). This suggests that a strategy aimed at indirectly reducing complement deposition via interference with protein trafficking holds considerable promise. Third, the idea of using brief pulses of BFA rather than continuous dosing could allow for a transient therapeutic effect that minimizes the known toxicities associated with prolonged BFA exposure—thus potentially overcoming one of the key limitations that has prevented its clinical development in other contexts (Seehafer et al., 2013; Stierle & Stierle, 2015).

Nonetheless, there are significant challenges and weaknesses that must be addressed before this approach can be advanced. One of the primary concerns relates to the non-selectivity of BFA’s action. Because BFA disrupts a fundamental cellular process, its effects are not confined solely to the trafficking of misfolded complement proteins. It is likely to inhibit the normal secretion of multiple proteins that are essential for RPE cell function, which could inadvertently impair the phagocytosis and processing of photoreceptor outer segments (Hung, 2020; Seehafer et al., 2013). Such disruption could paradoxically exacerbate the very lysosomal dysfunction it is intended to ameliorate, particularly if dosing is not carefully titrated. Furthermore, while transient inhibition may mitigate toxicity, achieving the precise balance between therapeutic efficacy and cellular viability will require rigorous optimization in both in vitro and in vivo models. Another potential drawback is related to pharmacokinetics and delivery. BFA’s poor bioavailability and potential systemic toxicity have long been recognized as obstacles for its use beyond experimental settings (Seehafer et al., 2013). Delivering BFA specifically to the RPE while avoiding off-target effects in other ocular or systemic tissues will demand innovative formulation strategies, such as localized drug delivery systems, which inherently add another layer of complexity to the drug development process. Additionally, while the hypothesis is supported by analogous studies demonstrating that complement modulation can restore lysosomal function in AMD models (Cerniauskas et al., 2020; Georgiannakis et al., 2015), there is currently no direct evidence that BFA’s inhibition of ER-to-Golgi trafficking will reproduce these beneficial effects in the delicate environment of the retina. The potential for unforeseen interactions with other components of the RPE’s protein processing machinery or compensatory cellular responses further complicates the translational prospects of this approach.

In summary, as an expert drug development team leader tasked with evaluating promising repurposed drugs for dry AMD, I find the proposal to repurpose Brefeldin A to be both innovative and mechanistically intriguing. The strengths of this candidate lie in its well-characterized molecular target—namely, the inhibition of Arf1-mediated vesicle budding at the Golgi—and its potential to modulate a key pathological feature of AMD, namely the dysregulated secretion and accumulation of complement proteins that leads to lysosomal impairment and MAC deposition (Georgiannakis et al., 2015; Cerniauskas et al., 2020). However, the inherent risks associated with disrupting a central cellular process cannot be overstated. The lack of selectivity in BFA’s action, potential toxicity from even brief exposures, and challenges in targeting the RPE specifically are serious obstacles that must be systematically addressed. Further preclinical studies are required to delineate the dose-response relationship in RPE cell models, to examine the long-term effects of transient BFA-induced trafficking inhibition on cellular health, and to validate the hypothesis that such intervention can indeed reduce lysosomal MAC deposition and rescue cathepsin activity. Only with convincing evidence from rigorous in vitro and in vivo experiments would it be justified to consider advancing BFA as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD. In conclusion, while the proposed use of BFA presents a compelling mechanistic rationale with some supportive preclinical analogies from the literature (Georgiannakis et al., 2015; Cerniauskas et al., 2020), significant challenges regarding specificity, delivery, and safety need to be overcome before this approach can be deemed a viable therapeutic option for dry AMD.
