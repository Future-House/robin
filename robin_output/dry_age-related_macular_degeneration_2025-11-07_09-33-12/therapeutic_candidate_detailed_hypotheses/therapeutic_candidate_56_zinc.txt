Proposal for Zinc Sulfate (ZnSO₄; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. Z0251)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Zinc sulfate (ZnSO₄; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. Z0251) is an inorganic salt that dissociates readily in aqueous solutions to yield zinc ions (Zn²⁺) and sulfate anions. Zinc sulfate is a well‐characterized compound produced via chemical synthesis and has been used for decades as a nutritional supplement to treat zinc deficiency. It belongs to the class of trace mineral supplements, and, more broadly, to inorganic therapeutic agents. In many clinical and veterinary practices, zinc sulfate has been utilized as a dietary supplement to support immune function, antioxidant defenses, and wound healing. Its broad therapeutic use is partly validated by its inclusion in complex formulations—for example, in the Age-Related Eye Disease Study (AREDS) protocol for age-related macular degeneration (AMD) where zinc supplementation was associated with a reduction in disease progression risk (Qin, 2007). Zinc sulfate has also found application in ophthalmic preparations due to extensive ocular safety data supporting its use; indeed, zinc sulfate eye drops have been evaluated for ocular safety in early-phase clinical trials (Qin, 2007). This candidate is particularly attractive for repurposing since its manufacturing and formulation are well established, and its molecular properties as a zinc donor could potentially address deficiencies in zinc-dependent enzymatic processes observed in dry AMD.

Therapeutic History  
Historically, the use of zinc in medical treatments dates back to its role as an essential trace element in numerous biological systems. In the context of ocular diseases, mounting evidence has implicated zinc deficiency in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) dysfunction and the pathogenesis of dry AMD. Preclinical studies in animal models have demonstrated that zinc-deficient conditions lead to significant pathological consequences in the RPE, including increased lipofuscin accumulation and impaired phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) (Julien et al., 2011). In vitro studies using ARPE-19 cells have similarly shown that disruption of zinc homeostasis produces lysosomal dysfunction which contributes to the build-up of toxic metabolic by-products associated with AMD pathology (Rajapakse et al., 2017). Moreover, several investigations have shown that zinc supplementation can rescue lysosomal acidification deficits and restore autophagic flux in RPE cells exposed to toxic compounds such as A2E—a bisretinoid that accumulates abnormally in dry AMD (Choi et al., 2024). Although many of these reports have employed zinc ionophores (e.g., ClioQ, 1H10) to facilitate zinc delivery into cells, they highlight a fundamental therapeutic rationale: zinc is an essential cofactor for proper lysosomal enzyme function, and its supplementation can reverse key features of cellular dysfunction associated with dry AMD (Choi et al., 2024). Clinical approaches, particularly those emerging from the AREDS trials, have also shown that high-dose zinc supplementation (typically delivered as zinc oxide or zinc sulfate) can slow the progression of AMD (Qin, 2007). While these formulations primarily aimed to address generalized zinc deficiency and oxidative stress, they establish a precedent for the repurposing of zinc salts—specifically zinc sulfate—in ocular disorders such as dry AMD.

Mechanism of Action  
At the molecular level, zinc acts as an indispensable cofactor for a multitude of enzymes, including the zinc-dependent cathepsins B and L. Cathepsins are lysosomal proteases critical for the degradation of phagocytosed POS, autophagic cargo, and other intracellular substrates that accumulate over time. In the RPE, the efficient clearance of POS is a daily and metabolically demanding process; thus, any impairment in cathepsin activity can lead to the accumulation of undegraded material, contributing to the formation of lipofuscin and subsequent cellular toxicity (Julien et al., 2011; Choi et al., 2024). Zinc ions contribute structurally and catalytically to the proper folding and activity of these cathepsins. Under conditions of zinc deficiency or altered intracellular zinc distribution—as has been observed in aged RPE and AMD patients—the activity of cathepsin B and L becomes suboptimal, leading to lysosomal dysfunction (Julien et al., 2011; Rajapakse et al., 2017).

Several reports have demonstrated that exposure to bisretinoids such as A2E leads to dose-dependent cell death and lysosomal alkalinization in ARPE-19 cells; this in turn impairs the activity of lysosomal hydrolases that require an acidic environment for optimal function (Choi et al., 2024). Zinc supplementation, when delivered intracellularly via ionophores in these models, restored lysosomal acidification and enabled the reactivation of the degradative machinery, including zinc-dependent enzymes (Choi et al., 2024). This reacidification is crucial because lysosomal proteases, including cathepsin B/L, demonstrate maximal catalytic activity at a pH of approximately 4.5–5.0—a condition that is disrupted in diseased RPE cells (Choi et al., 2024). Moreover, zinc has been shown to influence autophagy dynamics: in models where A2E treatment led to an arrest in autophagic flux (evidenced by increased p62 and LC3-II levels), zinc supplementation was able to reduce p62 levels and partially restore proper autophagic turnover (Choi et al., 2024). These observations suggest that zinc not only acts as a structural cofactor for lysosomal enzymes but also indirectly supports the maintenance of lysosomal pH homeostasis and autophagic function. The proposal under evaluation extends this line of reasoning by hypothesizing that direct supplementation with Zn²⁺—provided in the form of zinc sulfate—could similarly restore cathepsin B/L activity in RPE cells, thereby improving phagolysosomal enzyme function and photoreceptor outer segment degradation in dry AMD models (Choi et al., 2024).

Expected Effect  
Based on the aforementioned mechanistic rationale, the expectation is that ZnSO₄ supplementation will elevate intracellular Zn²⁺ concentrations in RPE cells, thereby reconstituting the proper activity of zinc-dependent enzymes. In the context of dry AMD, where lysosomal dysfunction is a key pathological feature, the restoration of cathepsin function is anticipated to improve the degradation of POS. Successful clearance of POS is critical to prevent the buildup of lipofuscin and other deleterious aggregates that contribute to RPE degeneration (Rajapakse et al., 2017). In cell-based assays—such as those using ARPE-19 cells exposed to A2E—the addition of zinc sulfate is hypothesized to act by increasing the bioavailability of Zn²⁺, which in turn would lead to several interrelated outcomes: (1) reacidification of lysosomes as zinc supports proton pump function and stabilizes lysosomal membranes, (2) enhanced enzymatic activity of cathepsins B and L, leading to improved proteolytic clearance, and (3) restoration of autophagic flux as evidenced by decreased p62 accumulation and normalized LC3-II turnover (Choi et al., 2024; Rajapakse et al., 2017). Furthermore, improved phagolysosomal function would likely reduce the intracellular accumulation of A2E and other toxic by-products, ultimately leading to increased cell viability and better overall RPE function. It is important to note that although many of the existing studies have utilized zinc ionophores to deliver Zn²⁺ into cells, the existing ocular safety profile for zinc sulfate formulations (as evidenced by previous clinical studies using zinc sulfate eye drops) supports the feasibility of adapting ZnSO₄ for this application (Qin, 2007). Moreover, gene expression studies confirm that RPE cells express high levels of cathepsin B and L, and their dysfunction correlates with AMD pathogenesis; thus, elevating Zn²⁺ levels may have a directly beneficial impact on these key enzymatic pathways (Julien et al., 2011).

Overall Evaluation  
The repurposing of zinc sulfate as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD rests on several well-founded observations. First, there is a robust body of preclinical evidence indicating that zinc supplementation can restore lysosomal function in RPE cells challenged with A2E-induced toxicity, which is a well-characterized in vitro model of dry AMD (Choi et al., 2024). This evidence includes improvements in lysosomal pH, reductions in A2E accumulation, and restored autophagic flux. Second, clinical data from large-scale studies such as AREDS have established that zinc supplementation—albeit delivered in a different form than zinc sulfate—confers a statistically significant reduction in the progression risk of AMD (Qin, 2007). Third, mechanistic studies have underlined the pivotal role of zinc as a cofactor for cathepsin B and L, enzymes that are crucial for the phagolysosomal degradation of POS. In the diseased state, reductions in zinc availability or disruptions in zinc homeostasis result in diminished cathepsin activity, impaired clearance of cellular debris, and subsequent lipofuscin accumulation (Julien et al., 2011; Rajapakse et al., 2017).

Despite these strengths, the candidate does have notable limitations. A significant concern is that most of the supportive in vitro studies have used zinc ionophores (such as ClioQ or 1H10) to ensure efficient intracellular delivery of zinc (Choi et al., 2024). Zinc sulfate, being a simple salt, does not possess intrinsic ionophore properties, which may limit its immediate cellular uptake and availability to lysosomal compartments. This suggests that while ZnSO₄ has a favorable safety profile and is easily formulated, its efficacy in rescuing intracellular cathepsin activity may be contingent on formulation strategies that enhance cellular uptake, such as combining it with a permeation enhancer or designing specialized delivery vehicles. Additionally, direct evidence linking zinc sulfate supplementation to the restoration of cathepsin B/L activity in RPE cells is currently inferred from studies using alternative zinc delivery methods rather than from studies employing ZnSO₄ directly (Rajapakse et al., 2017). Therefore, while the hypothesis is strongly supported by mechanistic reasoning and parallel evidence from related studies, targeted experiments specifically using ZnSO₄ in models of dry AMD are necessary to confirm its therapeutic potential.

Another consideration is the multifactorial nature of AMD pathogenesis. While lysosomal dysfunction and cathepsin impairment are certainly central features, AMD is also driven by oxidative stress, inflammation, and complement dysregulation. Zinc exerts broader antioxidant effects partly through its involvement in metallothionein systems and by modulating the activity of antioxidant enzymes (Rajapakse et al., 2017). Thus, while restoring cathepsin activity represents a compelling mechanistic target, zinc sulfate supplementation might also confer additional benefits that go beyond enzyme cofactor replacement. However, these pleiotropic effects may complicate the interpretation of the therapeutic outcomes, making it challenging to ascribe clinical benefits solely to the restoration of cathepsin activity.

In summary, zinc sulfate represents a promising therapeutic candidate for dry AMD on several fronts. Its inherent ability to donate Zn²⁺, a cofactor crucial for the activity of lysosomal proteases such as cathepsins B and L, forms the basis of its proposed mechanism of action. Preclinical evidence indicates that zinc supplementation can restore lysosomal pH, reduce A2E accumulation, and enhance autophagic flux in RPE cells—key elements that underpin the pathological cascade of dry AMD (Choi et al., 2024). Moreover, clinical trial data from studies using zinc compounds, including those with zinc sulfate formulations as ocular drops, further underscore the potential efficacy and safety of zinc-based therapies in AMD (Qin, 2007). Nevertheless, the translational success of ZnSO₄ hinges on addressing the challenge of effective cellular uptake. Without the use of an ionophore or an advanced delivery system, there is a risk that ZnSO₄ may not achieve the intracellular zinc concentrations required to modulate cathepsin activity and, by extension, lysosomal function in a robust manner. Additionally, while mechanistic studies have provided indirect evidence linking zinc levels to cathepsin function in RPE cells (Julien et al., 2011), direct validation of the therapeutic hypothesis with ZnSO₄ remains limited.  

Strengths of the candidate include its well-established production, favorable safety profile in ocular applications, and substantial supporting evidence from both preclinical and clinical studies that underscore the importance of zinc in maintaining lysosomal integrity and protease function (Choi et al., 2024; Rajapakse et al., 2017). The repurposing strategy is bolstered by a strong theoretical rationale: correction of zinc deficiency in the RPE should restore cathepsin effectiveness and thereby improve the degradation of POS, a function known to be compromised in dry AMD (Choi et al., 2024).

Conversely, weaknesses include the fact that most experimental paradigms demonstrating zinc’s benefits have relied on assisted delivery mechanisms (ionophores) and have not been directly replicated with zinc sulfate alone. This introduces uncertainty regarding the bioavailability of zinc ions when administered as ZnSO₄ in the intended therapeutic context. Furthermore, AMD is a complex, multifactorial disease, and while addressing lysosomal enzyme dysfunction is promising, it remains only one aspect of the overall pathology. Without addressing complementary pathological mechanisms such as chronic oxidative stress and inflammation, zinc sulfate alone may not suffice as a standalone therapy. Finally, the lack of direct evidence from ZnSO₄-specific studies in relevant dry AMD models (as opposed to studies using zinc ionophores) highlights the need for further targeted research before progressing to clinical trials.

Overall, the evaluation of zinc sulfate as a potential therapeutic candidate for dry AMD reveals a strong theoretical foundation conditioned on its role as a zinc donor to rescue Zn-dependent cathepsin activity in the RPE. The candidate leverages a well-known mechanism where zinc acts both structurally and catalytically within lysosomal proteases, and clinical data from related zinc supplementation studies provide an encouraging backdrop. However, the translational challenges concerning the effective delivery of Zn²⁺—particularly in the absence of an ionophore—must be carefully addressed. In conclusion, zinc sulfate exhibits considerable potential as part of a multifaceted therapeutic strategy targeting lysosomal dysfunction in dry AMD, with the caveat that further studies are needed to optimize its delivery, validate its mechanism in precise AMD models, and integrate it effectively within a broader therapeutic regimen that tackles the multifactorial nature of the disease (Choi et al., 2024; Qin, 2007).
