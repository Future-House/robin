Proposal for Ezetimibe (NPC1L1 inhibitor; Merck)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Ezetimibe (SCH-58235) is a synthetic small molecule developed as a specific inhibitor of intestinal sterol absorption and clinically positioned as an LDL-cholesterol–lowering agent and as therapy for rare sterol storage disorders such as sitosterolemia (National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute, 2001). Ezetimibe’s pharmacological class is “cholesterol absorption inhibitor,” a class characterized by selective action at the intestinal epithelium to reduce uptake of dietary and biliary cholesterol, thereby decreasing hepatic delivery of chylomicron-derived cholesterol, upregulating LDL receptor–mediated clearance, and lowering plasma LDL-C; in human and animal studies, agents in this class also increase fecal neutral sterol excretion and augment reverse cholesterol transport (RCT) from plasma to feces (National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute, 2001; Radiant Research, 2008; Ostlund, 2014; Bujold, 2012). Mechanistically, this class targets the Niemann–Pick C1-Like 1 (NPC1L1) protein at the apical surface of intestinal enterocytes, the principal protein-mediated pathway for sterol influx from mixed micelles; inhibition of NPC1L1 decreases absorption efficiency and increases fecal sterol output (Guo et al., 2024; Murillo, 2016; Bujold, 2012). Within ophthalmology, ezetimibe has been proposed in preclinical AMD literature as a systemic modulator of cholesterol handling that could mitigate diet-induced lipoprotein stress and downstream retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)/Bruch’s membrane (BrM) pathology, but published ocular outcome data are not yet available (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022). In short, ezetimibe is a clinically established synthetic NPC1L1 inhibitor designed to reduce intestinal sterol uptake and circulating atherogenic lipoproteins; its repurposing to dry AMD rests on the hypothesis that systemic lipid lowering will secondarily ameliorate RPE lipid burden and associated lysosomal/autophagy dysfunction (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022; Guo et al., 2024).

Therapeutic History  
Clinical use and systemic metabolic effects. Ezetimibe is an approved LDL-lowering therapy for hypercholesterolemia and sitosterolemia, typically dosed at 10 mg daily, and is widely used in combination with statins to achieve LDL targets with improved tolerability compared with high-intensity statin monotherapy in cardiovascular populations (National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute, 2001; Cho, 2023). Human metabolic studies using stable isotope tracers demonstrate that ezetimibe increases endogenous fecal neutral sterol excretion and reduces intestinal cholesterol absorption as expected for an NPC1L1 inhibitor; crossover and parallel-arm trials confirm effects on absorption efficiency and synthesis compensation, particularly when combined with statins (Radiant Research, 2008; Ostlund, 2014; Organon and Co., 2003). In randomized human feeding studies, ezetimibe reduces cholesterol absorption and lowers LDL-C; combined with phytosterols, effects on fecal sterols and LDL are additive, consistent with distinct yet complementary intestinal mechanisms (Washington University School of Medicine, 2009). In atherosclerosis-prone mice, ezetimibe reduces plaque burden; genetic deficiency of NPC1L1 similarly prevents atherosclerosis, and these intestinal manipulations enhance macrophage-to-feces RCT, implicating transintestinal cholesterol efflux (TICE) as a significant route for whole-body cholesterol disposal (Bujold, 2012).

Non-ocular lipid disease contexts. Beyond hypercholesterolemia, ezetimibe has been clinically evaluated in sitosterolemia (inherited sterol transport disorder), producing reductions in plasma plant sterols and LDL-C consistent with intestinal absorption blockade; protocols emphasize its role as a specific cholesterol absorption inhibitor in these patients (National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute, 2001). Multiple cardiovascular trials have examined ezetimibe’s LDL-lowering efficacy alone and in combination, and protocol summaries and meta-trials reiterate its intestinal mechanism and complementarity with statins (Organon and Co., 2003; Cho, 2023).

Ophthalmic/retinal disease history. The AMD preclinical literature reviewed by Landowski and Bowes Rickman identifies dietary cholesterol as a modifiable driver of AMD-like pathology in aged complement factor H (CFH) transgenic mice: high-fat, cholesterol-enriched (HFC) diets increase circulating lipoproteins, and aged CFH-H/H mice on HFC or high-cholesterol diets exhibit decreased scotopic ERG b-wave amplitudes; based on these observations, the authors propose that therapeutics reducing intestinal cholesterol uptake (citing ezetimibe) “may prevent visual loss in aged CFH-H/H HFC mice,” with ongoing studies to test whether ezetimibe can mimic dietary modulation and influence eyecup ApoB48 and ApoA-1 (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022). However, those reports explicitly note that these are investigational questions; no completed preclinical data on retinal function, RPE lipid endpoints, autophagy, or lysosomal indices under ezetimibe treatment are presented in the cited excerpts (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022). Reviews of lipid-lowering therapies in diabetic retinopathy (DR) list ezetimibe among LDL-lowering agents of interest but underscore the absence of large randomized controlled trials with DR endpoints and provide no ocular pharmacokinetic or safety data for ezetimibe; the text highlights that intraocular delivery can dramatically alter retinal exposure relative to systemic administration, underscoring a translational gap for systemically delivered LDL-modifying agents in ocular disease (Jenkins et al., 2022). Across the provided literature, there are no reports of ezetimibe being used to treat dry AMD or direct analogs (e.g., Stargardt disease) in humans, and no completed preclinical animal studies demonstrating ezetimibe-induced improvements in RPE lipofuscin, lysosome/autophagy function, photoreceptor survival, or drusen-like deposits (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022).

Mechanism of Action  
Primary molecular target and tissue of action. The canonical molecular target for ezetimibe is NPC1L1, a sterol influx transporter localized to the apical membrane of small-intestinal enterocytes, which mediates uptake of dietary and biliary cholesterol from mixed micelles; blocking NPC1L1 reduces intestinal absorption and lowers delivery of cholesterol to the liver via chylomicrons, thus decreasing hepatic VLDL production and circulating LDL levels (Guo et al., 2024; Murillo, 2016). The LDL-lowering and fecal sterol-raising effects of ezetimibe in humans and mice, together with similar outcomes observed in NPC1L1 deficiency, support on-target action at the intestinal epithelium (Bujold, 2012; Radiant Research, 2008; Ostlund, 2014; Organon and Co., 2003). Ezetimibe’s systemic metabolic effects—reduced LDL-C and enhanced RCT/TICE—are therefore downstream consequences of intestinal NPC1L1 inhibition rather than direct hepatic or peripheral tissue binding (Bujold, 2012; Radiant Research, 2008).

Systemic lipid fluxes relevant to the eye. After intestinal absorption, cholesterol is packaged into chylomicrons (ApoB48-containing) that deliver dietary lipid to the liver; hepatic secretion of VLDL (ApoB100-containing) and intravascular remodeling generate LDL, which distributes cholesterol systemically, including to the eye; cellular uptake of LDL occurs via the LDL receptor (LDLR) on peripheral cells (including RPE), while HDL mediates reverse cholesterol transport from tissues back to the liver through ABCA1/ABCG1-dependent efflux (Unknown Reference). The RPE has LDL and scavenger receptor activity and participates in receptor-mediated uptake of lipoprotein-bound cholesterol; this receptor-mediated uptake is proposed to be a major route for retinal cholesterol acquisition from systemic circulation, complementing local synthesis (Zekavat et al., 2017). Thus, ezetimibe can plausibly modulate ocular lipid influx indirectly by lowering plasma LDL and chylomicron-remnant delivery to the outer retina, which may reduce lipoprotein deposition in Bruch’s membrane and sub-RPE basal deposits in models where circulating lipoproteins contribute materially to the ocular lipid burden (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022).

Retinal cholesterol homeostasis and AMD-relevant pathobiology. Retina and RPE cholesterol derive largely from in situ synthesis behind the blood–retinal barrier, stringently controlled by transcriptional networks (SREBP2, LXR/RXR) and efflux transporters (ABCA1/ABCG1); age-related dysfunction in these pathways, along with mitochondrial decline, is implicated in AMD (Guo et al., 2024; Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022). Lipids constitute a large fraction of drusen volume (~40%), and oxidized cholesterol species—notably 7-ketocholesterol (7KCh)—accumulate in the outer retina and RPE, where they inhibit autophagic flux (elevated p62), promote lipofuscin accumulation within lysosomes, activate NF-κB and NLRP3 inflammasomes, and trigger apoptosis, collectively linking cholesterol dysregulation to lysosomal dysfunction and inflammation in AMD (Guo et al., 2024). These pathogenic cascades are exacerbated by inefficiencies in photoreceptor outer segment (POS) phagocytosis and degradation by the RPE, processes that can be impaired by lipofuscin bisretinoids such as A2E and cholesterol crystal-driven inflammasome activation (Nociari et al., 2017; Guo et al., 2024). Independent preclinical work shows that enhancing cholesterol efflux (e.g., via LXR agonism) and inducing autophagy can normalize endosomal/lysosomal morphology and reduce RPE lipid accumulation in disease models such as Abca4-/-, and that RPE-specific ablation of ABCA1/ABCG1 produces lipid accumulation, RPE/photoreceptor degeneration, and vision loss—highlighting the centrality of cholesterol handling to retinal health (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022). These data provide a mechanistic framework in which interventions that reduce retinal lipid influx, promote efflux, or restore lysosomal/autophagic capacity could beneficially modulate AMD-relevant endpoints.

Distinguishing NPC1L1 (intestinal) from NPC1 (lysosomal). It is important to differentiate the intestinal sterol influx transporter NPC1L1 (targeted by ezetimibe) from the lysosomal cholesterol egress protein NPC1; loss or inhibition of NPC1 traps cholesterol in late endosomes/lysosomes, whereas modulation of NPC1L1 affects apical enterocyte uptake (Schmiedt, 2012). In human RPE, palmitoylation of NPC1 governs cholesterol export from late endosomes; mutation of key cysteines increases lysosomal cholesterol accumulation without altering gross NPC1 localization, underscoring that RPE cholesterol trafficking depends on NPC1 and intracellular sorting mechanisms, not on NPC1L1 (Li et al., 2022). The provided literature does not demonstrate expression or functional roles for NPC1L1 in RPE, and no direct ocular actions of ezetimibe on RPE cholesterol uptake or lysosomal function are reported (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022; Guo et al., 2024).

AMD-relevant systemic–ocular linkage in CFH models. In aged CFH-transgenic mice, high-fat/high-cholesterol diets increase circulating chylomicron/VLDL/LDL and are associated with decreased scotopic ERG responses and sub-RPE deposit phenotypes; these findings motivate testing whether decreasing intestinal cholesterol absorption (e.g., ezetimibe) can mimic dietary cholesterol restriction and preserve visual function (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022). The same review emphasizes RPE-derived lipoprotein accumulation in BrM and proposes that reducing intestinal cholesterol could reduce ocular ApoB48/ApoA-1 signals in the eyecup, consistent with lower lipoprotein flux to or from RPE/BrM (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022). However, these are prospective hypotheses; experimental results were not reported in the excerpts (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022).

Expected Effect  
Proposed hypothesis and assay context. The working hypothesis asserts that ezetimibe will “block cholesterol uptake in RPE (IC50 ~0.5 µM), reducing cholesterol-driven lysosomal dysfunction and lipofuscin, thereby improving POS degradation in dry AMD models,” and claims that ezetimibe “reduces cholesterol content in RPE, normalizes lysosomal pH, and enhances POS clearance in high-cholesterol culture,” with supportive statements about oral safety and ocular penetration in animals. The provided literature does not support these specific assertions: there are no data in the excerpts demonstrating NPC1L1 expression in RPE, ezetimibe binding or potency against an RPE target, direct effects on RPE cholesterol content, lysosomal pH normalization, lipofuscin reduction, or POS degradation, and no ocular penetration measurements are reported (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022; Guo et al., 2024; Jenkins et al., 2022). Therefore, a direct RPE-cell autonomous mechanism for ezetimibe is currently unsubstantiated by the cited literature.

Mechanistically grounded expectations in vitro (RPE systems). Given the lack of evidence that RPE expresses NPC1L1, the expected direct effect of ezetimibe in RPE culture under high-cholesterol conditions is uncertain. RPE cholesterol uptake from the extracellular milieu is thought to be mediated predominantly by LDLR and scavenger receptors acting on lipoprotein particles, not by NPC1L1-mediated micellar uptake as in enterocytes (Zekavat et al., 2017). Moreover, intracellular lysosomal cholesterol egress in RPE depends on NPC1/NPC2 and is modulated by protein palmitoylation and other lysosomal trafficking determinants, not by NPC1L1 (Li et al., 2022; Schmiedt, 2012). Consequently, unless ezetimibe has unidentified off-target activity relevant to RPE cholesterol uptake or lysosomal function, one would not anticipate robust correction of lysosomal cholesterol storage, lysosomal pH, autophagic flux, lipofuscin accumulation, or POS digestion kinetics in RPE culture solely from ezetimibe exposure; the provided literature does not document such off-target activities (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022; Guo et al., 2024). In contrast, other interventions have demonstrated RPE-directed effects in vitro and in vivo: autophagy inducers reduce intracellular RPE lipid burden, and LXR agonism can normalize endosomal size and reduce complement activation in Abca4-/- RPE—establishing precedents for lipid/autophagy-targeted corrections in RPE models (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022). Additionally, β-cyclodextrins directly deplete lysosomal cholesterol and reduce lipofuscin bisretinoids in RPE models, highlighting an approach that directly targets the lysosome rather than systemic lipid flux (Nociari et al., 2017).

Mechanistically grounded expectations in vivo (mouse AMD-relevant models). In systems where circulating lipoproteins contribute substantially to outer-retinal lipid burden—e.g., aged CFH-transgenic mice exposed to HFC diets—reducing intestinal cholesterol absorption with ezetimibe should lower plasma chylomicron remnants/VLDL/LDL, reduce delivery of cholesterol to the outer retina via lipoprotein receptors, and potentially decrease RPE/BrM lipoprotein deposition and sequelae such as ERG attenuation and sub-RPE basal deposit accumulation; the CFH literature explicitly proposes this test and notes ongoing studies (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022). Mechanistically, this would be a systemic route: ezetimibe acts in the intestine, lowers circulating atherogenic lipoproteins, and thereby diminishes receptor-mediated uptake into RPE; it would not require NPC1L1 expression in the eye (Guo et al., 2024; Zekavat et al., 2017). If successful, ancillary effects could include downstream reductions in oxidized cholesterol species such as 7KCh in the outer retina, potentially normalizing autophagic flux and reducing inflammasome activation driven by lipid peroxidation, although such cascading benefits remain hypothetical in the absence of reported in vivo data (Guo et al., 2024). It should be noted that mice differ from humans in plasma lipoprotein distribution due to the absence of CETP (mouse plasma is HDL-dominant), which can alter the plasma lipid response to intestinal absorption blockade and complicate extrapolation of systemic lipid changes to ocular endpoints; diet manipulations can partially humanize lipoprotein profiles but introduce other confounders (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022). These interspecies differences emphasize the need for careful readouts of ocular lipid and function rather than reliance on plasma LDL changes alone.

Target expression and pathway linkage. The hypothesis’s protein target for direct ocular action is NPC1L1; however, the provided literature does not demonstrate NPC1L1 expression in human or mouse RPE, nor does it provide functional evidence for an NPC1L1-dependent uptake pathway in RPE cells (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022; Guo et al., 2024). In contrast, multiple sources document LDLR/scavenger receptor–mediated lipoprotein uptake in RPE and predominant local retinal cholesterol synthesis behind the blood–retinal barrier, suggesting that systemic LDL flux and local biosynthesis/efflux determine RPE lipid load (Zekavat et al., 2017; Guo et al., 2024). Within lysosomes, cholesterol egress uses NPC1/NPC2, and manipulation of these pathways (e.g., via palmitoylation state) alters lysosomal cholesterol burden in RPE (Li et al., 2022). Therefore, if ezetimibe were to produce benefits in AMD models, the most parsimonious mechanism would be indirect and systemic (reducing lipoprotein delivery to the eye) rather than a direct RPE NPC1L1-dependent effect (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022).

Safety and ocular exposure considerations. The clinical literature presented confirms extensive systemic use of ezetimibe, including long-duration combination therapy trials, supporting a favorable systemic safety profile; however, the provided excerpts contain no eye-specific pharmacokinetic or safety data and emphasize that systemic LDL-lowering agents generally lack large ocular endpoint trials and that ocular delivery can alter tissue exposure relative to systemic administration (Cho, 2023; Jenkins et al., 2022). Consequently, the assumption that orally dosed ezetimibe achieves pharmacologically relevant concentrations in ocular tissues is not substantiated by the cited material (Jenkins et al., 2022).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
- Strong systemic pharmacology and safety foundation. Ezetimibe is a clinically validated oral NPC1L1 inhibitor with robust human and animal data demonstrating reduced intestinal cholesterol absorption, increased fecal neutral sterol excretion, augmented reverse cholesterol transport, and lowered circulating LDL-C; these systemic effects are precisely the inputs one would manipulate to test lipid-driven outer-retinal pathology in diet-sensitive AMD models (Radiant Research, 2008; Ostlund, 2014; Organon and Co., 2003; Bujold, 2012).  
- Mechanistic plausibility via systemic–ocular linkage in selected models. Aged CFH-transgenic mice demonstrate diet-induced increases in circulating chylomicron/VLDL/LDL, sub-RPE lipid deposition, and ERG decrements; authors of a leading AMD lipid review specifically propose ezetimibe as a test agent to mimic dietary cholesterol restriction and potentially prevent visual loss in these mice, with ongoing studies to evaluate effects on ocular lipoprotein markers (ApoB48, ApoA-1) (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022).  
- Meaningful disease biology linkage. Independent lines of evidence implicate cholesterol and its oxidized derivatives (7KCh) in autophagy blockade, lysosomal lipofuscin accumulation, inflammasome activation, and RPE/photoreceptor cell death—key pathologies in dry AMD—supporting the concept that reducing retinal lipid influx or burden could yield functional benefit (Guo et al., 2024). Preclinical manipulations that increase efflux (LXR agonism) or induce autophagy normalize endosome/lysosome phenotypes in RPE models, further strengthening the causal pathway between lipid load and degradative capacity (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022).  
- Practical development advantages. Ezetimibe is already formulated for oral use, with known human dosing, tolerability, and pharmacodynamic biomarkers (fecal sterols, cholesterol absorption efficiency), enabling rapid, biomarker-driven translational studies if ocular efficacy can be shown in animals (Radiant Research, 2008; Organon and Co., 2003).

Weaknesses and key gaps  
- No evidence for direct RPE NPC1L1 expression or action. The provided literature does not document NPC1L1 expression in RPE or other ocular tissues, nor any cellular data showing ezetimibe reduces RPE cholesterol content, normalizes lysosomal pH, augments autophagic flux, lowers lipofuscin, or improves POS degradation; the specific potency claim (IC50 ~0.5 µM in RPE) is unsupported by the cited sources (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022; Guo et al., 2024). This undermines the core in vitro arm of the proposed hypothesis.  
- Retinal cholesterol is primarily locally synthesized. Due to the blood–retinal barrier, retinal cholesterol pools arise chiefly from in situ synthesis; modulating intestinal absorption may have limited impact on retinal cholesterol content unless circulating lipoproteins contribute substantially to outer-retinal lipid influx in the specific model under study (Guo et al., 2024). While RPE does possess LDL/scavenger receptor activity and can take up systemic lipoproteins, the quantitative contribution relative to local synthesis remains uncertain and likely context-dependent (Zekavat et al., 2017).  
- Lack of ocular pharmacokinetic and clinical endpoint data. There are no reported data on ezetimibe ocular penetration or exposure in animals or humans, and reviews emphasize the absence of large randomized retinal endpoint trials for LDL-lowering drugs, including ezetimibe; ocular efficacy with systemic dosing therefore remains speculative (Jenkins et al., 2022).  
- Species and model translation caveats. Mice lack CETP and are HDL-dominant, which affects systemic lipid responses to intestinal absorption inhibition and complicates extrapolation to human lipoprotein physiology; diet manipulations can humanize lipoproteins but introduce confounding metabolic changes that could independently influence RPE lysosomal/autophagy function (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022). In addition, anatomical differences between mouse and human retinas (rod-dominance, RPE density, POS phagocytic load) can alter lysosomal stress and degradative capacity, further complicating translation (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022).  
- Comparative mechanistic alternatives are more direct. Other interventions with demonstrated RPE-centric mechanisms—LXR agonists to increase ABCA1/ABCG1-mediated efflux, autophagy inducers, and β-cyclodextrins that directly solubilize lysosomal cholesterol and bind bisretinoids—have shown positive effects on RPE endosomal/lysosomal phenotypes or lipofuscin burden in preclinical models, suggesting more direct levers on the implicated pathogenic nodes than systemic intestinal absorption blockade (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022; Nociari et al., 2017).

Decision-oriented recommendations  
- Do not proceed on the assumption of a direct RPE target for ezetimibe. Before committing to RPE-centric in vitro efficacy claims, establish whether NPC1L1 is expressed in primary human RPE/iPSC-RPE and at what level; the provided literature indicates this is unknown, and NPC1L1 is classically enterocyte-restricted (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022; Guo et al., 2024; Murillo, 2016). In parallel, verify that ezetimibe has no off-target effects on LDLR- or scavenger receptor-mediated lipoprotein uptake in RPE and no direct impact on NPC1-dependent lysosomal cholesterol egress; current RPE mechanistic data implicate NPC1 palmitoylation in lysosomal cholesterol egress, not NPC1L1 activity (Li et al., 2022; Schmiedt, 2012).  
- Prioritize an in vivo, systemic-mechanism proof-of-concept study in a diet-sensitized AMD model. The strongest rationale for ezetimibe in AMD resides in aged CFH-transgenic mice where HFC diet increases circulating lipoproteins and impairs retinal function; test whether ezetimibe prevents ERG decline and reduces ocular ApoB48/ApoA-1 and sub-RPE lipid deposition under HFC challenge, as proposed in ongoing preclinical work (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022). Include endpoints that index RPE lysosome/autophagy status and lipid oxidation (e.g., p62/LC3 flux, lysosomal pH, A2E/7KCh, NLRP3 activation) to probe whether systemic lipid lowering secondarily normalizes degradative pathways (Guo et al., 2024; Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022).  
- Incorporate lipid handling comparators that act directly at the RPE. To de-risk mechanism, include a positive-control arm with an LXR agonist (to increase ABCA1/ABCG1) or an autophagy inducer known to reduce RPE lipid burdens, and consider a β-cyclodextrin arm to directly test whether lowering lysosomal cholesterol and bisretinoids rescues degradative function; these comparators will benchmark the magnitude of effect achievable with RPE-centric strategies (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022; Nociari et al., 2017).  
- Use plasma and fecal sterol biomarkers to confirm on-target intestinal action. Quantify fecal neutral sterol excretion and cholesterol absorption efficiency to verify effective NPC1L1 inhibition in vivo; these human-validated biomarkers have a mechanistic link to ezetimibe action and can anchor ocular outcome interpretations to on-target pharmacodynamics (Radiant Research, 2008; Ostlund, 2014; Organon and Co., 2003).  
- Plan for translational risks tied to retinal cholesterol sources. Given the primacy of local retinal cholesterol synthesis, negative results in models with minor circulating lipoprotein contributions to RPE lipid load would not falsify all systemic lipid-lowering hypotheses but would argue against ezetimibe’s utility except in specific lipid-sensitized genetic/dietary contexts (Guo et al., 2024; Zekavat et al., 2017). Selection of models should therefore be driven by evidence of systemic lipid influx effects on RPE/BrM biology (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022).

Bottom line. On current evidence, ezetimibe has credible potential to test the systemic lipid-influx axis of AMD pathogenesis in select diet-sensitized genetic models (e.g., aged CFH-H/H on HFC diets), with a plausible mechanism to reduce plasma lipoproteins and secondarily lower RPE/BrM lipid deposition; however, there is no substantiation for direct RPE action via NPC1L1, no reported ocular pharmacokinetics, and no preclinical efficacy data published on RPE lysosome/autophagy endpoints or lipofuscin under ezetimibe treatment. For dry AMD, ezetimibe should be considered a hypothesis-generating, low-to-moderate probability repurposing candidate with high translational uncertainty; it merits a focused, biomarker-anchored in vivo proof-of-concept in an appropriate model, but should not displace more direct RPE-targeting lipid/autophagy strategies in the near-term pipeline (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022; Nociari et al., 2017; Jenkins et al., 2022).

Detailed appraisal of disease biology and how ezetimibe might intersect  
- Cholesterol exposure and RPE degradative failure. Excess cholesterol in the outer retina oxidizes to 7KCh and other oxysterols that directly inhibit autophagic flux (p62 accumulation), acidify/injure mitochondria, and promote polymerization of oxidation products that become lipofuscin within lysosomes; lipofuscin accumulation impairs lysosomal phagocytosis and autophagy, reducing POS clearance—a core function of RPE (Guo et al., 2024). These processes activate NF-κB and NLRP3 inflammasome signaling and lead to RPE and photoreceptor apoptosis in rodent models, reproducing elements of dry AMD pathophysiology (Guo et al., 2024). Although ezetimibe does not directly target RPE degradative machinery, a systemic reduction in lipoprotein-delivered cholesterol could attenuate upstream substrate pressure for these oxidative and degradative stress pathways in models where systemic influx is important (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022).  
- RPE lipid handling machinery points to more proximal targets. ABCA1/ABCG1 are critical for RPE cholesterol efflux; their loss in RPE leads to lipid accumulation, degeneration, and vision loss, while pharmacologic LXR activation mimics endosomal normalization seen with acid sphingomyelinase inhibition in Abca4-/- RPE, illustrating the benefit of directly boosting efflux pathways (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022). Such direct manipulation of RPE lipid handling may offer a higher likelihood of efficacy than systemic manipulations in models where local synthesis dominates retinal cholesterol pools (Guo et al., 2024).  
- Lipoprotein biology and CFH genetic risk. CFH variants modulate RPE-derived lipoprotein accumulation in BrM, and HFC diets exacerbate ocular phenotypes in CFH-transgenic mice, positioning systemic lipoprotein burden as a modifiable driver in these contexts; ezetimibe is explicitly proposed in this literature as a candidate to reduce intestinal cholesterol uptake and test whether lowering circulating lipoproteins rescues visual function (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022).

Programmatic risk–benefit and go/no-go criteria  
- Go criteria for advancing to animal efficacy testing:  
  • Demonstration of on-target intestinal pharmacodynamics in the chosen species/diet (increased fecal neutral sterols; reduced fractional cholesterol absorption) at a clinically relevant dose (Radiant Research, 2008; Organon and Co., 2003).  
  • Aged CFH-transgenic mice on HFC diet with documented elevation of plasma chylomicron/VLDL/LDL and ERG decrements at baseline, to ensure model sensitivity to circulating lipoprotein changes (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022).  
  • Predefined ocular endpoints that read out RPE lipid and degradative status (ApoB48/ApoA-1 eyecup content, RPE/BrM lipid histology, LC3/p62 flux, lysosomal pH, A2E/7KCh levels, complement activation, ERG b-wave amplitude) (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022; Guo et al., 2024).  

- No-go criteria prior to significant resource commitment:  
  • Failure to demonstrate on-target intestinal pharmacodynamics at tolerated doses in the selected model (Radiant Research, 2008).  
  • Lack of effect on ocular lipoprotein readouts (ApoB48/ApoA-1) or ERG despite robust plasma LDL lowering and fecal sterol increase, suggesting that circulating lipoprotein flux is not rate-limiting for the ocular phenotypes in that model (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022).  
  • Evidence from expression analyses that NPC1L1 is not expressed in RPE (which would deprioritize direct cellular assays) and that systemic LDL reductions do not translate to reduced RPE lipid load (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022; Zekavat et al., 2017).

Summary conclusions  
- The overarching mechanistic thesis that cholesterol-driven lysosomal/autophagy dysfunction underlies key dry AMD phenotypes is well supported by preclinical data (7KCh-driven autophagy inhibition, lipofuscin accumulation, inflammasome activation, and apoptosis), and interventions that improve RPE lipid handling and degradative capacity show benefit in relevant models (Guo et al., 2024).  
- Ezetimibe’s capacity to reduce intestinal cholesterol absorption and lower circulating atherogenic lipoproteins is firmly established; translationally, this provides a credible lever to test whether systemic lipoprotein pressure influences RPE/BrM lipid deposition and function in susceptible genotypes/diets (Bujold, 2012; Radiant Research, 2008; Ostlund, 2014; Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022).  
- However, the specific claim that ezetimibe directly blocks RPE cholesterol uptake with sub-micromolar potency, normalizes lysosomal pH, reduces lipofuscin, and enhances POS degradation lacks support in the provided literature; moreover, NPC1L1 expression in RPE has not been demonstrated in these sources, and the intestine-restricted action of ezetimibe argues that any ocular benefits would be indirect via systemic lipid lowering (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022; Guo et al., 2024).  
- Given the lack of ocular PK data and the primacy of local retinal cholesterol synthesis, the prior probability of large clinical benefit in unselected dry AMD populations is modest; nonetheless, in CFH-sensitized, diet-exacerbated models the hypothesis is testable and may yield meaningful insights about the contribution of systemic lipoproteins to outer-retinal pathology (Guo et al., 2024; Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022).

Recommendation: Advance ezetimibe to a focused, biomarker-rich preclinical proof-of-concept in aged CFH-H/H mice under HFC diet, with stringent ocular endpoints and on-target intestinal PD confirmation. In parallel, deprioritize RPE-centric in vitro efficacy claims pending demonstration of NPC1L1 expression in RPE and direct cellular activity; maintain higher prioritization for agents with demonstrated RPE-direct mechanisms (LXR agonists, autophagy inducers, β-cyclodextrins) as nearer-term AMD therapeutics (Landowski & Bowes Rickman, 2022; Nociari et al., 2017).
