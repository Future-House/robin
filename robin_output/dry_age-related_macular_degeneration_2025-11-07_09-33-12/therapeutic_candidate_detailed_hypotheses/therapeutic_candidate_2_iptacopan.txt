Proposal for Iptacopan (LNP023; Cat. No. MedChemExpress HY-112345)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Iptacopan (LNP023) is a rationally designed, low–molecular-weight small molecule that selectively inhibits complement factor B (FB), the essential catalytic subunit of the alternative pathway (AP) C3/C5 convertases C3bBb and C3bBbC3b on activating surfaces (including stressed RPE) and within the amplification loop of complement activation (C3 → C3b → C3 convertase) (Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 7–8, 4–6). Medicinal-chemistry optimization from an early micromolar FB inhibitor hit (a naphthalene-dihydro-imidazole amine) introduced an indole to occupy the S1 pocket and a piperidine scaffold engaging the S3/non-primed region with a benzoic acid–mediated H-bond network, yielding iptacopan with high potency and selectivity for FB (crystallographically confirmed) (Schubart et al., 2023, pp. 10–13; Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 8–10). Iptacopan binds the catalytic serine-protease active site within the Bb subunit of FB—structural analyses show its indole and piperidine moieties seated in S1/S3, forming key hydrogen bonds within the catalytic domain, with the active-site triad (His–Asp–Ser) located in proximity; binding has been explicitly mapped to the catalytic site in Bb and shown to block substrate processing by the AP convertase (Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 4–6, 8–10).

Class and precedent: Iptacopan is an oral, proximal complement/AP inhibitor; this class has delivered robust target engagement and clinical benefit in multiple complement-driven diseases (e.g., PNH, C3G, IgAN), establishing translational viability for systemic small-molecule blockade of AP amplification in human disease (Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 8–10, 7–8; Schubart et al., 2023, pp. 13–14). Oral administration achieves rapid systemic exposure (Tmax ≈ 2 hours) with dose-dependent AP suppression and sustained steady-state inhibition at 200 mg twice daily in clinical studies, supporting convenient outpatient dosing with on-target PD (reduced plasma Bb and urinary sC5b-9) (Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 7–8; Schubart et al., 2023, pp. 13–14). Preclinically, systemic iptacopan potently blocks LPS-induced AP activation in mice in both plasma and eye tissues, indicating ocular target engagement after oral dosing (Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 4–6). Collectively, synthesis, mechanistic selectivity, and clinical-class performance position iptacopan as a well-characterized, orally bioavailable FB inhibitor suited for indications where local AP amplification contributes to pathology, including dry AMD (Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 8–10, 7–8; Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 4–6).

Therapeutic History  
- Non-ocular indications and clinical pharmacology. In early-phase clinical studies, iptacopan demonstrated rapid absorption (peak plasma concentration at ~2 hours), favorable tolerability, and dose-dependent inhibition of AP activity; sustained maximal inhibition was reported at 200 mg twice daily, the regimen advanced into multiple phase 2 trials (Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 7–8; Schubart et al., 2023, pp. 13–14). Across PNH and AP-driven renal diseases (C3G, IgAN), iptacopan showed robust target engagement and clinical activity: reductions in plasma Bb and urinary sC5b-9, reductions in plasma C3 (in many patients after months), kidney-pathology improvements (reduced glomerular C3 deposition), and meaningful efficacy outcomes (e.g., proteinuria reductions in C3G/IgAN; hemoglobin improvements and transfusion-free status in PNH) with generally favorable safety profiles and no serious infections in the cited trials (Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 8–10, 7–8; Schubart et al., 2023, pp. 13–14). Iptacopan has received regulatory approvals (including an accelerated approval for IgAN) and late-stage development status for multiple indications, validating systemic AP inhibition with this agent (Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 8–10, 7–8).  
- AMD program. A randomized, placebo-controlled phase 2 trial in early/intermediate AMD is ongoing (Novartis Pharmaceuticals, 2022), designed to test whether oral iptacopan prevents conversion to iRORA or late AMD over 24 months, with masked multimodal imaging and planned plasma trough PK by LC–MS; vaccinations for encapsulated organisms are required per complement-inhibition class risk management (no results are available in the excerpt) (“A Masked, Placebo-controlled Study to Assess Iptacopan in Age-related Macular Degeneration,” 2022). Reviews place iptacopan among oral FB inhibitors under evaluation for atrophic AMD as an alternative to intravitreal complement blockade, motivated by the blood–retina barrier and the burden of injections; however, the provided material contains no AMD efficacy outcomes for iptacopan (Wilke & Apte, 2024, pp. 8–9; Hammadi et al., 2023, pp. 30–31; Wilke & Apte, 2024, pp. 7–8).  
- Ocular-relevant preclinical context. Although iptacopan-specific dry-AMD model data are not provided, two lines of evidence are directly relevant: (1) in the Efemp1 R345W knock-in mouse (an RPE proteostasis model with AMD-like basal deposits), genetic Cfb deletion abolished AP activation and eliminated sub-RPE deposits, while oral pharmacologic FB blockade (with the small molecule DB-82-CB77) reduced deposits by 65% in females and reversed disease-associated pathway perturbations (UPR/inflammation upregulation and mitochondrial repression) (Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 4–6, 1–2, 9–10); (2) systemic iptacopan blocks AP activation in eye tissue in LPS-challenged mice, supporting oral delivery as a viable route to ocular target engagement, though iptacopan-specific AMD-model efficacy is not shown in the provided excerpts (Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 4–6).  
- Complement biology and lysosomal/autophagy pathology in RPE. In CFH Y402H high-risk iPSC-RPE, increased C3 turnover and lysosomal deposition of C5b-9 drive lysosomal swelling, membrane damage, Cathepsin D leakage to sub-RPE deposits, and reduced lysosomal function; blocking C3 with the compstatin analogue Cp40 reverses these phenotypes, restoring lysosomal number/size/function and lowering complement deposition (Cerniauskas et al., 2020, pp. 1–2, 2–4, 7–13). While these data establish that proximal complement blockade can normalize lysosomal/autophagy dysfunction in high-risk RPE, the excerpts do not directly test FB/AP inhibitors in this RPE system; thus, extrapolation to iptacopan remains mechanistic rather than empirically proven in the provided sources (Cerniauskas et al., 2020, pp. 7–13).

Mechanism of Action  
- Biochemical target and binding mode. FB is an abundant plasma glycoprotein (~0.2–0.4 mg/mL; ~2–3 μM) unique to the AP and, upon binding to C3b, is cleaved by factor D into Ba and Bb; Bb contains a VWA domain, MIDAS motif, and a serine-protease domain that provides the catalytic site for AP C3/C5 convertases on C3b-bearing surfaces (Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 4–6). Iptacopan binds the catalytic site of Bb in the S1/S3 pockets with a defined hydrogen-bonding network, directly inhibiting the proteolytic activity of the AP convertase and thereby blocking AP-mediated C3/C5 processing (Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 8–10, 7–8; Schubart et al., 2023, pp. 11–13).  
- Potency, selectivity, and functional inhibition. Iptacopan exhibits an enzymatic IC₅₀ ≈ 0.01 μM against FB and inhibits AP-induced C5b-9 formation in 50% human serum with IC₅₀ ≈ 0.13 μM; it prevents C3 deposition and complement-mediated lysis of PNH erythrocytes with IC₅₀ ≈ 0.4 μM and shows high selectivity over factor D and classical/lectin pathway activity in broad panels (Schubart et al., 2023, pp. 11–13; Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 8–10). Alternative affinity reporting (K_D ≈ 0.0079 mM) has been noted in one excerpt, but across structural/functional assays the compound consistently behaves as a highly potent and selective FB catalytic-site inhibitor (Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 7–8, 8–10).  
- Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics relevant to target engagement. Clinically, iptacopan is rapidly absorbed (Tmax ~ 2 hours), with strong steady-state AP inhibition at 200 mg twice daily; pharmacodynamic readouts demonstrate reduced circulating Bb and reduced urinary sC5b-9, consistent with systemic and tissue-level AP suppression (Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 7–8; Schubart et al., 2023, pp. 13–14). While precise human half-life and oral bioavailability values are not provided in the excerpts, iptacopan is described as orally bioavailable with a shorter half-life than antibodies, enabling relatively rapid restoration of AP function after washout—an attribute that informs adherence requirements and infection risk mitigation (Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 8–10, 10–11). Factor B itself has a long plasma half-life (30–70 h) and high abundance; iptacopan’s high affinity and mechanism allow sustained convertase inhibition because only newly synthesized FB needs to be neutralized at steady state for continued pathway blockade (Schubart et al., 2023, pp. 11–13).  
- Ocular target engagement. In mice, systemic administration of iptacopan potently blocks LPS-induced complement activation in both plasma and eye tissues, implying that oral dosing can achieve pharmacologically active exposure to ocular compartments, at least under inflammatory conditions (Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 4–6). In the Efemp1 R345W model, ocular AP activation is local (iC3b and Ba ≈ 2× in eye, not in plasma), aligning with a need for ocular engagement; although iptacopan’s ocular concentration-time profile in humans is not provided, the preclinical PD findings and the AMD phase 2 trial design together support the translational intent to suppress ocular AP activity via oral dosing (Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 1–2; Novartis Pharmaceuticals, 2022; Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 4–6).  
- Genetic and disease-mechanism validation. FB is genetically and mechanistically implicated in AMD pathogenesis; protective low-activity variants (e.g., R32Q) associate with reduced AMD risk, and AP dysregulation is a central pathogenic thread in AMD biology (Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 4–6). In an RPE proteostasis model (Efemp1 R345W), Cfb deletion abolishes AP activation and prevents sub-RPE deposit formation; pharmacologic FB suppression reduces deposit burden and reverses multiple mutation-associated pathway perturbations (UPR, immune/complement signatures, mitochondrial repression), supporting causal linkage between AP activity and RPE/Bruch’s membrane pathology (Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 4–6, 1–2, 9–10).  
- Comparison to other complement nodes in AMD models. Complement C3 blockade (Cp40) in CFH Y402H high-risk iPSC-RPE rescues lysosomal structure and function, reduces C3b and C5b-9 deposition (including lysosomal MAC), and diminishes drusen-like phenotypes, demonstrating that proximal complement suppression can reverse RPE degradative defects; this provides a mechanistic bridge to AP/FB inhibition, which is expected to decrease upstream C3 turnover and downstream MAC burden by curtailing the amplification loop, though FB-specific rescue of lysosomal function was not directly tested in that study (Cerniauskas et al., 2020, pp. 1–2, 2–4, 7–13).

Expected Effect  
- In vitro RPE assays and readouts. Based on the AP mechanism and the CFH Y402H iPSC-RPE data with C3 inhibition, iptacopan is expected to reduce AP convertase activity on RPE surfaces, lowering C3b/iC3b deposition and MAC (C5b-9) accrual on and within RPE cells, thereby alleviating lysosomal overburden, improving lysosomal integrity, and facilitating restoration of autophagic flux (e.g., normalized LC3-II/p62 dynamics) and degradative function (e.g., Cathepsin D activity and localization) (Cerniauskas et al., 2020, pp. 1–2, 2–4, 7–13). The Efemp1 model shows that AP activation is heightened locally and that Cfb deletion or FB inhibition normalizes stress/inflammation (including UPR) and mitochondrial pathways; by analogy, iptacopan should attenuate ER stress/UPR signaling and immune/complement activation signatures in RPE/eyecup proteomes, with secondary benefits for mitochondrial/energy pathways (Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 4–6, 1–2). Although direct demonstration that FB inhibition restores RPE lysosomal function is not presented in the excerpted Efemp1 work, the Cp40 RPE data make a strong mechanistic case that upstream complement suppression reduces complement load on lysosomes and rescues degradative capacity; iptacopan should achieve a similar effect by preventing AP amplification and thus lowering the rate of C3 activation fragment generation and MAC formation at the RPE (Cerniauskas et al., 2020, pp. 1–2, 2–4, 7–13).  
- Ex vivo/animal AMD-relevant endpoints. In female Efemp1 R345W mice, oral FB inhibition (DB-82-CB77) reduced sub-RPE deposits by 65% over 8 weeks, while Cfb deletion completely eliminated deposits and abrogated AP activation in eye, indicating that AP blockade can causally modify a basal laminar–like deposit phenotype; iptacopan, as a potent oral FB inhibitor with demonstrated ocular PD in mice, would be expected to recapitulate these effects on deposits and molecular signatures, subject to dose/exposure parity and sex-dependent biology (Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 1–2, 9–10, 4–6). The Efemp1 eyecup proteomics further suggest that FB blockade rebalances ocular homeostasis, dampening CR3/immune-complement signaling and upstream UPR stress while partially restoring mitochondrial proteins, reinforcing the hypothesis that complement amplification contributes to proteostasis failure and that its inhibition should normalize these axes (Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 4–6).  
- Clinical AMD endpoints. The ongoing phase 2 iptacopan study in AMD is designed to detect prevention of conversion to iRORA or late AMD by SD-/SS-OCT and multimodal imaging over 24 months; systemic trough PK will be monitored. If oral iptacopan meaningfully engages ocular AP in humans, we would anticipate reduced new-onset iRORA and slower disease progression relative to placebo, potentially accompanied by systemic PD markers (Bb suppression), though the trial as described does not include ocular PD sampling (Novartis Pharmaceuticals, 2022). As precedent, intravitreal C3 inhibition (pegcetacoplan) reduced GA growth in phase 2; conversely, C5 inhibition had mixed results, underscoring the importance of targeting upstream amplification (C3/AP) rather than solely the terminal pathway in AMD (Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 7–9). It remains to be shown whether systemic FB inhibition can reproduce these benefits without increasing CNV risk; complement inhibition broadly has been associated with higher rates of new-onset CNV in some trials, a safety signal that must be monitored in FB-targeting programs (Wilke & Apte, 2024, pp. 7–8).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
- Strong mechanistic and genetic rationale. FB is the essential catalytic subunit of AP convertases, and human genetics supports a causal role of FB activity in AMD (protective low-activity variants) (Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 4–6). In an RPE-driven mouse model (Efemp1 R345W), Cfb deletion abolishes ocular AP activation and eliminates sub-RPE deposits; pharmacologic FB inhibition reduces deposits and reverses disease-associated pathway dysregulation, linking AP amplification to RPE/proteostasis pathology and basal deposit accumulation (Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 4–6, 1–2, 9–10).  
- Demonstrated oral target engagement, including ocular PD in mice. Iptacopan is orally bioavailable with rapid absorption and consistent systemic PD (Bb and sC5b-9 suppression) in humans; preclinically, systemic iptacopan blocks AP activation in eye tissue, supporting feasibility of oral ocular target engagement (Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 7–8; Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 4–6).  
- Potency/selectivity with favorable clinical precedents. Iptacopan shows nanomolar enzymatic potency, selective FB catalytic-site binding, and clear functional AP inhibition in serum/cell assays; it has delivered convincing efficacy across multiple complement-driven indications with acceptable safety, validating the drug and target class clinically (Schubart et al., 2023, pp. 11–13; Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 8–10; Schubart et al., 2023, pp. 13–14).  
- Convergence with RPE lysosome/autophagy biology. In CFH Y402H high-risk iPSC-RPE, proximal complement blockade (C3 inhibitor Cp40) reverses lysosomal swelling/dysfunction and reduces complement deposition including MAC; these findings mechanistically support the hypothesis that halting AP amplification upstream will relieve lysosomal burden and restore degradative capacity in RPE, a central deficit in dry AMD (Cerniauskas et al., 2020, pp. 1–2, 2–4, 7–13).

Risks and Gaps  
- Lack of direct iptacopan data in AMD models or RPE lysosome assays. The provided excerpts do not report iptacopan efficacy in AMD-relevant animal models (e.g., Efemp1) or direct rescue of RPE lysosomal/autophagic function; the pharmacologic proof-of-concept in Efemp1 used a different FB inhibitor (DB-82-CB77) (Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 1–2, 9–10). Thus, the hypothesis that iptacopan will restore RPE degradative capacity is mechanistically plausible but not yet experimentally demonstrated in the cited AMD models (Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 4–6; Cerniauskas et al., 2020, pp. 7–13).  
- Ocular PK/PD in humans unproven. The AMD phase 2 protocol measures plasma trough iptacopan but does not include ocular PD; no human ocular PK is provided in the excerpts. Translational dependence on ocular exposure is high because AP activation in Efemp1 is local to the eye (elevated Ba/iC3b in ocular tissue but not plasma) (Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 1–2; Novartis Pharmaceuticals, 2022).  
- Heterogeneity and sex effects in preclinical model. Efemp1 females exhibit ocular AP activation and respond to FB inhibition; males have lower deposits, no ocular AP upregulation, and do not respond, implying biological heterogeneity that may translate to variable clinical responses and may necessitate biomarker-based enrichment (e.g., markers of ocular AP activation) (Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 1–2).  
- Class safety considerations. Complement inhibition increases susceptibility to infections with encapsulated bacteria; vaccination is mandated in the AMD study. Additionally, complement inhibition in GA trials has been associated with increased new-onset CNV versus sham, a signal that must be actively monitored; whether FB inhibition carries a similar CNV liability is unknown from the provided materials (Novartis Pharmaceuticals, 2022; Wilke & Apte, 2024, pp. 7–8).  
- Mixed history of systemic complement inhibitors in AMD. The sole completed RCT of systemic anti-C5 (eculizumab) in AMD failed to show GA benefit, raising delivery and mechanistic questions for systemic approaches, although intravitreal proximal inhibitors (C3) have shown GA-slowing, and the Efemp1 data support AP dependence for basal deposit biology (Hammadi et al., 2023, pp. 17–18; Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 7–9).

Program Implications and Next Steps  
- Preclinical toolkit alignment. To directly validate the central hypothesis for iptacopan, run head-to-head studies in AMD-relevant systems:
  - Efemp1 R345W mouse: oral iptacopan dosing matched for AP PD versus DB-82-CB77 benchmark; endpoints: TEM-assessed sub-RPE deposits, ocular Ba/iC3b, eyecup/neural retina proteomics (UPR/complement/mitochondrial modules), and RPE lysosomal/autophagy markers (LAMP1, CTSD activity, LC3-II/p62 flux), stratified by sex (Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 1–2, 9–10, 4–6).  
  - CFH Y402H iPSC-RPE: iptacopan in complement-competent human serum conditions; readouts: C3b/C5b-9 deposition, lysosomal integrity (Lysotracker, Galectin-8), CTSD localization/activity, autophagic flux and POS phagocytosis assays; benchmark to Cp40 rescue (Cerniauskas et al., 2020, pp. 1–2, 2–4, 7–13).  
  - Ocular exposure/PD: quantify iptacopan in ocular compartments (choroid/retina/RPE) and measure ocular AP biomarkers in vivo after oral dosing to confirm exposure–PD matching the LPS PD signal (Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 4–6).  
- Translational biomarker strategy. Incorporate systemic AP markers (Bb, Ba), exploratory ocular biomarkers (e.g., aqueous humor C3 fragments if feasible), and imaging-based deposit/ELM–Bruch’s metrics; in the clinic, deploy multimodal imaging for conversion endpoints as in Novartis Pharmaceuticals (2022), and prospectively monitor CNV incidence (Novartis Pharmaceuticals, 2022; Wilke & Apte, 2024, pp. 7–8).  
- Patient selection. Consider enriching for patients with systemic or local evidence of AP activation (genetics such as CFH risk alleles, complement biomarker profiles) analogous to the female-biased AP activation seen in Efemp1 (Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 1–2).  
- Dosing rationale and adherence. Target 200 mg twice daily based on human PD experience; given the shorter half-life vs antibodies and the need for sustained AP suppression, emphasize adherence and vaccine prophylaxis; PD sampling should verify on-target AP inhibition longitudinally (Schubart et al., 2023, pp. 13–14; Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 7–8; Ricklin, 2024, pp. 7–9; Novartis Pharmaceuticals, 2022).

Bottom line. Iptacopan is a mechanistically compelling, clinically validated oral FB inhibitor with demonstrated systemic PD, rapid onset, and preclinical evidence of ocular AP engagement; rigorous AMD-model testing and clinical biomarker integration are now required to close the translational loop from AP biology to RPE lysosomal rescue and clinical protection against atrophy progression (Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 7–8, 8–10; Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 4–6, 1–2; Cerniauskas et al., 2020, pp. 1–2; Novartis Pharmaceuticals, 2022).

Detailed Assessment Relative to the Stated Hypothesis  
- “Iptacopan will block alternative-pathway C3 convertase assembly on RPE surfaces (IC50 ~50 nM).” Support: Structural and enzymology data indicate direct catalytic-site binding to Bb with enzymatic IC₅₀ ≈ 10 nM against FB and functional inhibition in human serum at ~0.13 μM, consistent with potent AP convertase blockade; the proposed 50 nM figure is within the range bracketed by these assays (Schubart et al., 2023, pp. 11–13; Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 8–10).  
- “Reducing C3b deposition, alleviating lysosomal overburden, indirectly enhancing POS phagocytosis.” Support: In CFH Y402H RPE, proximal complement blockade at C3 reverses lysosomal swelling/fragility and reduces C3b/MAC burden; while FB inhibition was not directly tested, AP inhibition should reduce C3 turnover and MAC burden upstream, plausibly relieving lysosomal stress and enabling degradative function needed for POS processing; direct evidence for iptacopan specifically restoring lysosomal function or POS phagocytosis in RPE is not provided in the excerpts and remains to be shown (Cerniauskas et al., 2020, pp. 1–2, 2–4, 7–13).  
- “Genetic and pharmacologic inhibition of factor B reduces sub-RPE deposits and rescues lysosomal/autophagy dysfunction in CFH high-risk RPE models (Ong et al. Medicina 2024).” Clarification: The provided sources show that FB genetic deletion eliminates deposits and that an oral FB inhibitor reduces deposits and reverses pathway dysregulation in the Efemp1 mouse; rescue of lysosomal/autophagy dysfunction in CFH high-risk RPE is demonstrated with C3 inhibitor Cp40, not with an FB inhibitor in the excerpts provided (Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 4–6, 1–2, 9–10; Cerniauskas et al., 2020, pp. 1–2).  
- “Iptacopan has demonstrated oral bioavailability and target engagement in Phase II for AMD, with good ocular tissue penetration.” Clarification: Oral dosing, rapid absorption, and systemic target engagement are established; the AMD phase 2 trial is ongoing with plasma trough PK but no reported results; ocular penetration per se has not been quantified in humans in the provided excerpts, though preclinical iptacopan blocked ocular AP activation in mice (Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 7–8; Novartis Pharmaceuticals, 2022; Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 4–6).

Go/No-Go Considerations for Dry AMD  
- Go signals:  
  - Target validity in AMD pathobiology is strong (genetics, AP dependence of basal deposits, proximal complement benefits in RPE models) (Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 4–6; Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 1–2; Cerniauskas et al., 2020, pp. 1–2).  
  - Drug is clinically de-risked across multiple indications with robust AP PD and acceptable safety; oral delivery is patient-friendly vs intravitreal regimens (Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 8–10, 7–8; Schubart et al., 2023, pp. 13–14).  
  - Ocular PD feasibility is supported in mice for systemic iptacopan; pharmacologic AP blockade reduces deposits in Efemp1 with another orally bioavailable FB inhibitor (Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 4–6; Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 1–2).  
- Caution flags:  
  - No iptacopan AMD-model efficacy in the provided materials; need direct RPE lysosome/autophagy rescue readouts and deposit suppression with iptacopan prior to expensive late-phase commitments (Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 1–2; Cerniauskas et al., 2020, pp. 7–13).  
  - Macro safety signals observed with complement inhibitors in AMD (CNV incidence) necessitate vigilant monitoring and risk–benefit analyses tailored to FB inhibition (Wilke & Apte, 2024, pp. 7–8).  
  - Systemic delivery uncertainty—human ocular PK/PD data are lacking; trial design should incorporate robust systemic PD and exploratory ocular biomarkers where feasible (Novartis Pharmaceuticals, 2022).

Recommendation  
Advance iptacopan for dry AMD with a focused, staged plan:  
- Preclinical confirmation package: replicate Efemp1 findings with iptacopan (deposits, AP biomarkers, proteomics) and demonstrate RPE lysosomal/autophagy rescue in CFH Y402H iPSC-RPE under complement-competent conditions; quantify ocular exposure–PD after oral dosing (Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 1–2; Cerniauskas et al., 2020, pp. 1–2; Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 4–6).  
- Biomarker-enriched clinical strategy: leverage systemic AP biomarkers and imaging-based progression metrics from Novartis Pharmaceuticals (2022); consider enrichment by complement genetics/biomarkers reflecting AP activation (Novartis Pharmaceuticals, 2022).  
- Safety management: maintain vaccination requirements and active CNV surveillance; implement adherence support given the shorter half-life relative to biologics (Novartis Pharmaceuticals, 2022; Ricklin, 2024, pp. 7–9; Wilke & Apte, 2024, pp. 7–8).

On balance, iptacopan has substantial mechanistic, translational, and clinical-class support to merit continued development in dry AMD, contingent on closing the specific evidence gaps for RPE degradative rescue and direct AMD-model efficacy with this agent (Kavanagh et al., 2025, pp. 8–10; Crowley et al., 2023, pp. 1–2; Cerniauskas et al., 2020, pp. 1–2).
