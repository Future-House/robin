Proposal for Metformin (AMPK agonist; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. D150959)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Metformin is a well‐established biguanide originally derived from the natural compound galegine found in Galega officinalis. Over decades of development, it has been chemically optimized and synthesized into its modern, orally administrable form. Today, metformin is classified within the antidiabetic drug class and is routinely used to treat type 2 diabetes mellitus. Its discovery as an antihyperglycemic agent was serendipitous, and subsequent structural modifications have resulted in a drug with an excellent safety profile and pleiotropic effects beyond blood glucose regulation. As a small synthetic molecule available from reputable suppliers such as Sigma‐Aldrich (Cat. No. D150959), metformin has been repurposed in recent years for potential applications outside of metabolic diseases, including neurodegeneration and retinal degenerative disorders. This candidate belongs to a class of compounds that have historically been used for systemic metabolic control, but emerging evidence also suggests that their ability to activate AMPK—a critical cellular energy sensor—can be exploited to modulate autophagy and mitochondrial function in non‐metabolic tissues, including the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) (Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Zhao et al., 2020).

Therapeutic History:  
Clinically, metformin has long been the first-line treatment for type 2 diabetes due to its robust glucose-lowering capability and favorable long-term risk profile. The extensive clinical use of metformin in diabetic populations has allowed researchers to retrospectively observe its pleiotropic effects on other organ systems. Notably, retrospective epidemiological studies have demonstrated that diabetic patients treated with metformin exhibit a lower incidence of age-related macular degeneration (AMD) compared to non-users, suggesting potential protective effects in the retina (Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Huang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2020). Furthermore, preclinical studies investigating metformin in vitro have shown that it protects human RPE cells against oxidative stress-induced damage, largely mediated by stimulation of the AMPK pathway (Zhao et al., 2020). In addition, in vivo models employing metformin have provided evidence of its ability to mitigate light-induced photoreceptor loss and retinal degeneration, supporting the hypothesis that its molecular targets in the RPE could be leveraged against dry AMD (Dang et al., 2021; Miyagishima et al., 2021). Although metformin has not been established as a conventional treatment for AMD, its proven use in modulating metabolic pathways, along with demonstrable effects on autophagy, mitochondrial function, and inflammation in retinal cells, provides the rationale for repurposing it in the context of dry AMD (Vessey et al., 2022; Buonfiglio et al., 2024).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, metformin exerts its primary pharmacological effects by inhibiting mitochondrial electron transport chain complex I, which leads to a reduction in ATP production and a concomitant increase in the AMP/ATP ratio. This energetic shift results in the activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), a master regulator of cellular metabolism and autophagy (Zhao et al., 2020). Activated AMPK phosphorylates several downstream targets that collectively restore energy homeostasis; among these targets are key regulators of autophagy, including the inhibition of mTOR and the direct phosphorylation of ULK1, which initiates autophagosome formation (Zhao et al., 2020; Dang et al., 2021). One important biochemical hallmark of this autophagy induction is the conversion of cytosolic LC3-I to its lipidated, membrane-bound form LC3-II, which has been used extensively as a marker to assess autophagy flux in RPE cell studies (Zhao et al., 2020). Additionally, metformin’s activation of AMPK influences mitochondrial biogenesis through upregulation of transcription co-activators such as PGC-1α, thereby promoting the generation of new, functionally active mitochondria (Dang et al., 2021; Sekar et al., 2023). These mechanisms are particularly relevant in the context of dry AMD, where RPE metabolic dysfunction is a key pathological feature. Moreover, metformin has been shown to modulate lipid metabolism, notably by enhancing cholesterol efflux via the AMPK/LXR pathway, which can reduce intracellular lipid accumulation—a contributor to drusen formation in AMD (Dang et al., 2021). Additional anti-inflammatory effects are mediated through suppression of NLRP3 inflammasome activation and modulation of VEGF signaling, further contributing to the overall cytoprotective profile of metformin in the retina (Dang et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2020).

Expected Effect:  
Based on the proposed hypothesis and existing evidence, it is expected that metformin will activate AMPK in RPE cells, thereby promoting autophagy and mitochondrial biogenesis. In practical terms, treatment with metformin is anticipated to lead to a measurable increase in LC3-II formation, a reduction in intracellular lipid accumulation, and restoration of ATP levels in RPE cells—specifically in models employing CFH Y402H iPSC-RPE cells, which serve as a relevant in vitro model of AMD (Zhao et al., 2020). The enhanced autophagic flux, as indicated by increased LC3-II levels and decreased p62 levels, will facilitate the clearance of damaged organelles, including dysfunctional mitochondria. This process of mitophagy is expected to improve overall mitochondrial homeostasis, thereby restoring the energetic capacity required for efficient phagocytosis and degradation of photoreceptor outer segments (POS). Given that efficient POS degradation by RPE cells is critical for photoreceptor renewal and retinal health, the metformin-induced restoration of energy balance and autophagic function could lead to improved visual function. Importantly, AMPK is expressed in RPE cells and has been shown to be a crucial mediator of autophagy and mitochondrial quality control in these cells, ensuring that metformin’s mechanism of action is well-targeted to the cellular environment affected in dry AMD (Miyagishima et al., 2021; Zhou & Xue, 2024). Furthermore, several in vivo studies support the notion that metformin can rescue mitochondrial function and enhance cellular clearance mechanisms, which are expected outcomes of its use in dry AMD models (Sekar et al., 2023; Zhou & Xue, 2024).

Overall Evaluation:  
In evaluating metformin as a drug candidate for treating dry age-related macular degeneration, there are several notable strengths. First, metformin’s well-established safety profile from decades of clinical use for type 2 diabetes offers an advantageous starting point for repurposing—its known pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties facilitate its potential translation to ophthalmic indications (Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Zhang et al., 2020). Second, robust preclinical data support metformin’s ability to activate AMPK and promote autophagy, two pathways intimately involved in the maintenance of RPE health. Activation of AMPK has been repeatedly shown to enhance LC3-II formation, reduce oxidative stress, restore mitochondrial membrane potential, and upregulate mitochondrial biogenesis, all of which are critical to counteracting the metabolic deficits seen in AMD (Zhao et al., 2020; Dang et al., 2021; Sekar et al., 2023). Third, epidemiological studies have suggested that metformin use correlates with a reduced incidence of AMD, lending clinical relevance to its potential protective effects in the retina (Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Huang et al., 2023). Fourth, the ability of metformin to modulate lipid metabolism, particularly by promoting cholesterol efflux and thereby reducing drusen-associated lipid accumulation, represents another beneficial mechanism targeting a fundamental aspect of AMD pathology (Dang et al., 2021).

However, several weaknesses and challenges must also be considered. Although many preclinical and retrospective clinical studies point toward a beneficial role of metformin in retinal health, direct prospective evidence in dry AMD patients remains limited. Some of the data originate from studies published in journals of variable quality, and there exists heterogeneity in the responses observed across different model systems, including patient-derived RPE cells where metformin’s effects may vary (Augustin & Atorf, 2023). Moreover, while metformin is effective in activating AMPK systemically at standard doses used for diabetes, the optimal dosing regimen, route of administration, and tissue-targeting strategies necessary to achieve effective concentrations in the retinal tissue remain to be determined. The possibility that metformin’s efficacy may depend on specific genetic or metabolic backgrounds of patients—such as differences in CFH status or other AMD-related risk factors—also raises concerns about patient heterogeneity in clinical trials (Miyagishima et al., 2021; Augustin & Atorf, 2023). Additionally, while the expected restoration of autophagy and mitochondrial biogenesis is promising, the precise quantitative changes in LC3-II, ATP levels, lipid accumulation, and POS degradation need rigorous validation under controlled experimental conditions before definitive conclusions can be drawn (Zhao et al., 2020; Zhou & Xue, 2024). Finally, potential systemic side effects such as gastrointestinal disturbances, rare occurrences of lactic acidosis, and vitamin B12 deficiency, albeit infrequent, may need consideration when repurposing metformin for chronic ocular use, especially in non-diabetic populations (Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Brown et al., 2019).

Overall, metformin represents a promising therapeutic candidate for dry AMD due to its multifaceted effects on enhancing autophagy, improving mitochondrial biogenesis, and restoring cellular energy metabolism in RPE cells. Its safety profile and broad availability further support its potential for rapid clinical translation. Nonetheless, the candidate’s ultimate success will depend on careful dose optimization, robust clinical trial designs that account for inter-patient variability, and further mechanistic studies to precisely delineate the quantitative biochemical improvements—such as LC3-II increase, lipid clearance, ATP restoration, and enhanced POS degradation—in preclinical AMD models (Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Zhao et al., 2020). The convergence of epidemiological, biochemical, and preclinical data thus supports proceeding with further validation studies, while also highlighting the need to address remaining uncertainties regarding tissue-specific drug delivery and long-term outcomes in the context of dry AMD (Dang et al., 2021; Vessey et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2020).
