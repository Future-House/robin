Proposal for Hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (HPβCD; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. C0926)

Below is a detailed evaluation of hydroxypropyl‐β‐cyclodextrin (HPβCD) as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry age-related macular degeneration (dry AMD). The report is organized into several sections that describe the candidate’s origin, therapeutic history, molecular mechanism, expected effects in the context of RPE (retinal pigment epithelium) pathology, and an overall evaluation of its potential utility in dry AMD.

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Hydroxypropyl‐β‐cyclodextrin (HPβCD) is a chemically modified derivative of β-cyclodextrin, a cyclic oligosaccharide originally discovered as a naturally occurring compound that forms toroidal structures with a hydrophilic exterior and a hydrophobic interior. The modification with hydroxypropyl groups improves its aqueous solubility and reduces toxicity, making it more suitable for biomedical applications. HPβCD belongs to the cyclodextrin family, which has been widely used as drug carriers and excipients due to their ability to form inclusion complexes with a range of lipophilic molecules. The development of HPβCD was driven by the need to improve the solubility and stability of lipophilic drugs, and its ability to modulate cholesterol homeostasis has led to its investigation in several disorders characterized by lipid accumulation. Notably, HPβCD has been studied in the context of cholesterol‐storage diseases such as Niemann–Pick type C disease by virtue of its capacity to extract cholesterol from intracellular membranes. Its synthesis involves partial substitution of hydroxyl groups on the parent β-cyclodextrin with hydroxypropyl moieties, thus expanding its inclusion complexation repertoire and enabling it to interact with lipophilic substrates such as cholesterol and bisretinoids. This candidate’s prior use in ocular studies, especially those addressing cholesterol dyshomeostasis and lipofuscin clearance, provides a strong rationale for its repurposing in dry AMD, where abnormal lipid accumulation in the RPE is a core pathology (Nociari, Kiss, & Rodriguez-Boulan, 2017).

Therapeutic History:  
HPβCD, as well as its parent cyclodextrin compounds, have been used extensively in both biomedical research and preclinical studies focused on lipid storage disorders. In Niemann–Pick type C models, for example, cyclodextrins have demonstrated efficacy in reducing cholesterol accumulation in lysosomes by sequestering cholesterol and promoting its efflux from cells. This cholesterol‐removal capability has led to its evaluation in animal models that exhibit retinal cholesterol dyshomeostasis. Several studies have provided evidence for HPβCD’s beneficial effects on lysosomal function in various cell types; in fibroblasts from Niemann–Pick patients, for instance, HPβCD treatment restored autophagy and reduced intracellular lipid buildup (Moruno-Manchon et al., 2016; Nixon, 2016). Moreover, in a mouse model of retinal cholesterol dyshomeostasis, HPβCD treatment produced significant reductions in retinal cholesterol content and reduced pathological deposits at critical interfaces such as the photoreceptor outer segment (POS)–RPE boundary (El-Darzi, Mast, Petrov, & Pikuleva, 2021; El-Darzi et al., 2023).  
In retinal models more directly related to AMD pathogenesis, cyclodextrins have been shown to decrease A2E, a major bisretinoid that accumulates in the RPE with age, by directly forming inclusion complexes with lipofuscin components (Nociari et al., 2017). Additionally, in cultured human cells and in animal studies, cyclodextrins have demonstrated a capacity to modulate lysosomal acidity and enhance the clearance of aggregated or indigestible material. Some reports, however, caution that cyclodextrins may act predominantly through cholesterol depletion rather than by stimulating lysosomal biogenesis via transcription factor EB (TFEB) (Gaspar, Mathieu, & Alvarez, 2017). Furthermore, studies using patient‐derived iPSC‐RPE cells, particularly in the context of Bietti’s crystalline dystrophy, have shown that cyclodextrin treatment rescues lysosomal pH defects and reinstates autophagic flux, which is directly relevant to dry AMD pathophysiology (Hata et al., 2018). Although HPβCD has not been formally trialed in dry AMD patients to date, its cumulative use in preclinical ocular models suggests that its repurposing for AMD is both a viable and promising strategy (El-Darzi et al., 2021).

Mechanism of Action:  
The molecular basis for HPβCD’s therapeutic potential in retinal diseases rests on its multifaceted ability to interact with lipid and retinoid components within the RPE’s lysosomal compartment. One of the key pathological features of dry AMD is the accumulation of toxic bisretinoids such as A2E within lysosomes, which contributes to impaired phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments and eventual cellular dysfunction (Nociari et al., 2017). HPβCD is thought to ameliorate this situation via several interrelated mechanisms:  

1. Direct Extraction and Complexation of Lipofuscin Precursors:  
HPβCD’s hydrophobic cavity allows it to form inclusion complexes with lipophilic compounds. Several reports have provided computational and experimental evidence that β-cyclodextrins form complexes with A2E and other bisretinoids, effectively “solubilizing” these compounds within their cavity. This extraction mechanism leads to a reduction of lipofuscin accumulation in the lysosomes, which is a critical step since lipofuscin impairs lysosomal enzyme activation such as that of cathepsin D (Nociari et al., 2017). The formation of a 2:1 cyclodextrin–A2E complex has been predicted by in silico models, suggesting that each cyclodextrin can accommodate an arm of the A2E molecule, thereby reducing lipofuscin toxicity (Nociari et al., 2017).  

2. Cholesterol Depletion and Modulation of Lysosomal Lipid Homeostasis:  
HPβCD is well known for its ability to extract free and esterified cholesterol from cellular membranes. In the context of dry AMD, cholesterol accumulation contributes to lysosomal dysfunction by perturbing the activity of the vacuolar-type H⁺-ATPase (v-ATPase), leading to lysosomal alkalinization which in turn reduces cathepsin D activity. By depleting excess cholesterol from the POS and RPE, HPβCD can facilitate the restoration of lysosomal acidity. This cholesterol depletion has been documented in several animal models in which HPβCD treatment led to significant reductions in retinal cholesterol levels and improved ultrastructural characteristics of the RPE (El-Darzi et al., 2021). Additionally, this depletion is likely to relieve the inhibition of v-ATPase activity and promote effective autophagosome–lysosome fusion, thereby enhancing overall lysosomal function (Hata et al., 2018; Gaspar et al., 2017).

3. Activation of Autophagy and TFEB-Mediated Lysosomal Biogenesis:  
In many contexts of lysosomal storage and lipid overload, cellular homeostasis can be restored by triggering autophagy. HPβCD has been shown to induce autophagy by upregulating TFEB, a master transcriptional regulator that governs lysosomal biogenesis and autophagic pathways. Activation of TFEB leads to increased expression of genes involved in lysosome formation, acidification, and function. In experimental models, HPβCD has been reported to promote TFEB translocation to the nucleus, thereby enhancing lysosome-mediated degradation of accumulated substrates such as lipofuscin components (Moruno-Manchon et al., 2016; Nociari et al., 2017). It should be noted that not all studies find TFEB upregulation in all cell types; for instance, some human fibroblast studies have highlighted a cholesterol-mediated mechanism with no direct evidence of TFEB activation (Gaspar et al., 2017). Thus, the net effect of HPβCD may stem from a combination of direct removal of toxic aggregates and improvements in autophagic flux via restored lysosomal pH, rather than relying solely on TFEB-mediated biogenesis.

4. Enhancement of Lysosomal Exocytosis:  
A less widely discussed but complementary mechanism involves the induction of calcium-dependent lysosomal exocytosis. HPβCD has been shown in some studies to promote the exocytosis of lysosomal contents, thereby facilitating the clearance of undegraded material from the cell. This process may help eliminate toxic lipofuscin and other aggregated compounds that accumulate in RPE cells over time (Nociari et al., 2017).  

5. Restoration of Cathepsin D Activity and POS Degradation:  
Cathepsin D is a critical lysosomal protease involved in the degradation of photoreceptor outer segment (POS) material within RPE cells. Accumulated lipofuscin and cholesterol can raise lysosomal pH and directly interfere with cathepsin D maturation and activity. By extracting excess cholesterol and lipofuscin precursors, HPβCD is expected to normalize lysosomal pH and thereby restore cathepsin D function. This restoration is key to improving POS degradation efficiency, which is compromised in dry AMD and contributes to the progressive degeneration of RPE cells (Gaspar et al., 2017; Nixon, 2016). Although direct measurements of enhanced cathepsin D activity following HPβCD treatment in AMD models are not extensively reported in the current literature, the underlying biochemical interactions support the hypothesis that a more acidic lysosomal environment will favor proper cathepsin D activation (El-Darzi et al., 2023; Nociari et al., 2017).

Expected Effect:  
Based on the integrated mechanisms described above, the expected therapeutic effect of HPβCD in dry AMD models is multifold. First, by forming inclusion complexes with A2E and related lipofuscin precursors, HPβCD is expected to lower the intracellular burden of these toxic compounds in RPE lysosomes. This direct extraction would lead to a decrease in the accumulation of lipofuscin, which is known to exacerbate oxidative stress and inflammation in the RPE (Nociari et al., 2017). Second, through its cholesterol-extracting properties, HPβCD should mitigate lipid overload within the RPE cell membranes and lysosomes. This reduction in cholesterol content is predicted to relieve the inhibition on the lysosomal v-ATPase, thereby restoring the acidic pH necessary for optimal lysosomal enzyme function. Consequently, cathepsin D activity should be reinstated, enhancing the capacity of RPE cells to degrade phagocytosed POS material (El-Darzi et al., 2021; Hata et al., 2018). Third, if HPβCD additionally promotes autophagy via TFEB nuclear translocation and increased lysosome biogenesis, there would be an overall improvement in the turnover of cellular debris and a reinforcement of the cell’s degradative machinery. In turn, these improvements should lead to enhanced clearance of damaged proteins, lipids, and photoreceptor debris, thereby reducing RPE cell stress and dysfunction—a primary drive behind dry AMD pathology (Moruno-Manchon et al., 2016; Nociari et al., 2017).  
Furthermore, because lipofuscin accumulation is directly linked to impaired clearance of POS and chronic inflammation, reducing its burden should indirectly improve the visual cycle. Improvements in RPE function would likely translate to better photoreceptor support and potentially slow the progression of macular degeneration, which is critically important in managing dry AMD. In sum, the expected effect of HPβCD is a synergistic improvement in lysosomal function mediated by both direct chemical extraction of toxic substances and indirect enhancement of cellular clearance pathways (Gaspar et al., 2017; Hata et al., 2018).

Overall Evaluation:  
Hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin presents several compelling strengths as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD. Its well-documented ability to extract cholesterol and lipophilic toxins from cells has been validated in several preclinical models, and its potential for restoring lysosomal pH and, consequently, lysosomal enzyme activity is particularly attractive in the context of AMD’s impairment of RPE degradative functions (El-Darzi et al., 2021; Nociari et al., 2017). The multifaceted mechanism of action—ranging from direct complexation with A2E to cholesterol depletion and potential activation of autophagy via TFEB—addresses several interconnected pathological processes that drive dry AMD progression. Moreover, cyclodextrins’ extensive history of use in other lipid storage disorders provides a valuable precedent for their clinical repurposing and supports the feasibility of translating these findings into an ocular application (Hata et al., 2018).  

However, several weaknesses and challenges must be considered. There is some inconsistency in the literature regarding the ability of HPβCD to activate TFEB, with certain studies reporting a predominantly cholesterol-mediated mechanism without marked transcriptional upregulation of lysosomal biogenesis (Gaspar et al., 2017). This indicates that the beneficial effects may depend on the specific cellular context and disease model. Additionally, while preclinical evidence in models of retinal cholesterol dyshomeostasis and lipofuscin accumulation is promising (El-Darzi et al., 2021; El-Darzi et al., 2023), there remains a need for more direct studies in AMD models—preferably in vitro studies using primary RPE cells or in vivo studies in well-established dry AMD animal models such as the Abca4−/− or other relevant transgenic lines. The dosing issue is also a consideration; in some studies, effective lipofuscin clearance required higher concentrations of HPβCD, which in turn may introduce off-target effects or toxicity concerns that have been noted in other cyclodextrin applications, such as ototoxicity in systemic administration (Gaspar et al., 2017). Moreover, while its ability to cross blood–retina barriers has been demonstrated in some contexts (El-Darzi et al., 2021; El-Darzi et al., 2023), ensuring sufficient local concentration in the RPE to achieve the desired lysosomal effects without eliciting adverse systemic effects remains an important translational challenge.

In conclusion, the cumulative evidence from biochemical, cellular, and preclinical studies supports the hypothesis that HPβCD has considerable potential as a repurposed drug candidate for dry AMD. Its dual action in directly binding and extracting toxic lipofuscin precursors and in correcting cholesterol-mediated lysosomal dysfunction addresses two key pathogenic pathways in dry AMD. While additional research is needed to optimize dosing, verify the mechanism in AMD-specific models, and fully assess safety profiles in the ocular context, the strengths of HPβCD—derived from its proven track record in modulating lipid metabolism and lysosomal function—make it a promising candidate for further development (El-Darzi et al., 2021; Gaspar et al., 2017). The proposed clinical strategy aiming to lower A2E levels, restore lysosomal pH and cathepsin D activity, and enhance POS degradation is well supported by the current literature, and thus HPβCD merits strong consideration for inclusion in the dry AMD therapeutic pipeline.

Overall, while some mechanistic nuances remain to be elucidated, particularly regarding the relative contributions of TFEB activation versus cholesterol extraction, the convergence of preclinical findings and the candidate’s established utility in lysosomal storage disorders provide a solid foundation for advancing HPβCD toward clinical evaluation in dry AMD. Continued investigation into its pharmacokinetics, local retinal bioavailability, and long-term effects on RPE function, along with careful dose optimization in relevant AMD animal models, will be essential next steps in validating its therapeutic value (El-Darzi et al., 2021; Gaspar et al., 2017).

This comprehensive review indicates that HPβCD stands as a promising repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD with several strengths that are directly aligned with the pathophysiological challenges of the disease, while also highlighting areas that require further investigation to ensure a safe and effective translation from preclinical models to clinical application (Hata et al., 2018; Nociari et al., 2017).
