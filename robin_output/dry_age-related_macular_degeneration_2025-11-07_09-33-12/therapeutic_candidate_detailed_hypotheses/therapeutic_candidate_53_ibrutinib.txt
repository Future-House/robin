Proposal for Ibrutinib (BTK inhibitor; SelleckChem Cat. No. S2680)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Ibrutinib is a fully synthetic small‐molecule inhibitor originally developed as an irreversible inhibitor of Bruton's tyrosine kinase (BTK). It achieves inhibition by covalently binding to the conserved Cys481 residue located within the ATP‐binding pocket of BTK, a key member of the TEC family of kinases. This irreversible binding results in permanent inactivation of BTK, thereby interrupting B‐cell receptor signaling as well as other downstream pathways that are critical for cell survival and proliferation (Xie et al., 2021). As a member of the broader class of tyrosine kinase inhibitors, ibrutinib is designed through refined organic synthesis strategies that optimize its selectivity and potency. Compounds of this chemical class have been extensively utilized in oncology, particularly for the treatment of B‐cell malignancies, where they serve to disrupt aberrant oncogenic signaling pathways. Ibrutinib is available commercially (e.g., SelleckChem Cat. No. S2680) and has been subject to rigorous clinical evaluation, ensuring that its chemical properties, including its irreversible binding mechanism and cell penetration capabilities, are well established in the literature (Xie et al., 2021).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, ibrutinib was developed and advanced clinically for hematologic cancers such as chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), mantle cell lymphoma (MCL), Waldenström’s macroglobulinemia (WM), and other B‐cell malignancies. Its success in these domains is attributed to its effective inhibition of BTK, a kinase that is critical for B‐cell receptor signaling and survival (Xie et al., 2021). Once approved by the U.S. FDA in 2013, it rapidly became a standard of care in oncology. Outside of its oncologic use, ibrutinib has been recognized for its immunomodulatory effects that extend to innate immune cells such as macrophages, dendritic cells, and, notably, microglia. Although the primary application of ibrutinib is in cancer therapy, there is emerging evidence and growing scientific interest suggesting that BTK plays a role in innate immune signaling pathways that contribute to chronic inflammatory states, including those implicated in age-related macular degeneration (AMD) (Brandli et al., 2024). In pharmacovigilance studies, ocular adverse events—including uveitis, cataracts, and retinal disorders—have been identified among patients treated with ibrutinib, which indicates that the molecule penetrates ocular compartments and exerts biological effects there (Allouchery et al., 2021). However, despite these observations, ibrutinib has not been previously developed or marketed for the treatment of dry AMD or similar degenerative retinal diseases. Instead, its potential repurposing arises from the hypothesis that by modulating BTK-dependent inflammatory pathways in microglia and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells, ibrutinib might reduce deleterious inflammasome priming and cytokine production that contribute to the pathogenesis of dry AMD (Brandli et al., 2024; Korhonen, 2024).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, ibrutinib exerts its primary action by binding irreversibly to BTK, thereby preventing autophosphorylation and subsequent transmission of intracellular signals that lead to inflammation and cell survival. BTK is not only a critical mediator of B‐cell receptor signaling but is also expressed in cells of the innate immune system including microglia and RPE cells. Its activation is linked to the regulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome—a multiprotein complex that senses cellular stress, leading to the activation of caspase-1 and the maturation and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18 (Brandli et al., 2024). There is accumulating evidence that BTK facilitates the priming of the NLRP3 inflammasome in response to various stimuli, including oxidative stress and the accumulation of deleterious protein aggregates, both of which are implicated in dry AMD pathogenesis. Experimental data suggest that inhibition of BTK by ibrutinib can lead to a reduction in IL-1β release, which in turn may lower downstream pro-inflammatory signals such as tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) (Far et al., 2022; Allouchery et al., 2021). Moreover, the preservation of lysosomal cathepsin activity observed in ARPE-19 cells under inflammasome-stimulating conditions implies that BTK inhibition may also support the maintenance of RPE lysosomal integrity and phagocytic function. Such actions are critical for the clearance of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) and prevention of drusen accumulation—a hallmark of dry AMD (Brandli et al., 2024; Allouchery et al., 2021). In summary, by covalently binding to BTK, ibrutinib not only halts aberrant immune cell signaling but also indirectly modulates inflammasome activity and associated inflammatory cytokine release in retinal cells (Xie et al., 2021; Brandli et al., 2024).

Expected Effect:  
The central hypothesis for the repurposing of ibrutinib in dry AMD revolves around its ability to inhibit BTK activity within microglia and RPE cells, thereby suppressing inflammasome priming and limiting the production of inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β and TNFα. In the context of an in vitro assay using ARPE-19 cells, it is anticipated that the application of ibrutinib (IC₅₀ ~0.5 nM) will result in a marked attenuation of inflammasome activation, as evidenced by reduced secretion of IL-1β and preserved lysosomal cathepsin activity (Brandli et al., 2024; Allouchery et al., 2021). The proposed mechanism by which ibrutinib exerts its therapeutic benefit in dry AMD is multifaceted. First, by inhibiting BTK, the compound is expected to downregulate the overactive NLRP3 inflammasome that plays a central role in the inflammatory cascade within the retinal pigment epithelium (Brandli et al., 2024; Korhonen, 2024). Second, by reducing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines—especially TNFα—which have been shown to negatively impact the phagocytic ability of RPE cells, ibrutinib may help to maintain the clearance of photoreceptor outer segment debris. This is particularly important because impaired clearance mechanisms contribute to the accumulation of drusen and subsequent RPE degeneration in dry AMD (Brandli et al., 2024; Korhonen, 2024). Moreover, animal and clinical pharmacovigilance studies have provided evidence that ibrutinib is capable of crossing the blood-brain and blood-retinal barriers, suggesting that it can reach retinal tissues at therapeutically relevant concentrations. Although there is documented evidence of ocular adverse events in patients treated with ibrutinib for hematologic malignancies—including instances of uveitis and cystoid macular edema—such effects have been largely reported as unintended consequences rather than engineered therapeutic outcomes. In the current repurposing framework, the premise is that controlled intravitreal or local administration, possibly as a modified formulation, could harness the anti-inflammatory properties of ibrutinib while minimizing systemic exposure and the risk of ocular adverse events (Allouchery et al., 2021; Far et al., 2022). Ultimately, the expected effect is a preserved RPE function with enhanced ability to clear POS debris, thereby mitigating the degenerative progression typically observed in dry AMD (Korhonen, 2024; Brandli et al., 2024).

Overall Evaluation:  
Ibrutinib presents as a promising candidate for repurposing in the treatment of dry age-related macular degeneration due to its well-characterized molecular mechanism and its established clinical profile in oncology. One of the major strengths of ibrutinib lies in its potent and selective irreversible inhibition of BTK, a kinase implicated not only in B-cell malignancies but also in the pathogenesis of inflammatory retinal diseases through its role in NLRP3 inflammasome priming (Xie et al., 2021). The scientific rationale for its repurposing is supported by preclinical observations indicating that BTK inhibition leads to reduced IL-1β secretion and preservation of lysosomal function in RPE cells, which are essential for maintaining photoreceptor homeostasis and preventing drusen accumulation in dry AMD (Brandli et al., 2024; Allouchery et al., 2021). Furthermore, the demonstration that ibrutinib crosses the blood-retinal barrier underscores its potential utility in directly targeting retinal tissues, especially when administered via routes that ensure localized high concentrations with limited systemic exposure (Xie et al., 2021; Far et al., 2022).

However, there are several challenges and weaknesses associated with this therapeutic candidate. First, the ocular safety profile of ibrutinib is a matter of concern, as pharmacovigilance data have linked ibrutinib to a spectrum of adverse ocular events including cataracts, uveitis, and retinal disorders (Allouchery et al., 2021; Far et al., 2022). While these effects have so far been observed in the context of systemic cancer therapy, they raise important safety questions when considering chronic administration for a disease such as dry AMD that often requires long-term treatment. Second, the dosages and formulation required to achieve an anti-inflammatory effect in the retina, while concurrently minimizing cytotoxicity and other off-target effects, remain uncertain. Ibrutinib’s off-target inhibitory effects, such as its activity against VEGFR2, may impart vascular side effects that could further complicate its use in the delicate retinal environment (Allouchery et al., 2021). Third, the repurposing strategy relies heavily on the extrapolation of cellular and animal model data—such as the observed reduction of inflammasome activation and preservation of cathepsin activity in ARPE-19 cells—to the complex pathophysiology of human dry AMD. The heterogeneity of dry AMD pathogenesis, driven by a confluence of genetic, environmental, and age-related factors, means that successful translation from preclinical models to effective clinical outcomes is not guaranteed (Korhonen, 2024; Brandli et al., 2024). Additionally, while there is a robust theoretical framework underpinning BTK’s involvement in inflamed retinal microenvironments, direct evidence of ibrutinib’s efficacy in modulating microglial or RPE function specifically in the setting of dry AMD remains limited.

Despite these challenges, the mechanistic rationale for using a BTK inhibitor in dry AMD is compelling. The interplay between NLRP3 inflammasome activation, chronic low-grade inflammation, and RPE dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a pivotal driver of dry AMD progression (Brandli et al., 2024). If ibrutinib can effectively attenuate these inflammatory processes without incurring prohibitive adverse events, it may offer a novel therapeutic avenue for a disease area currently lacking effective treatment options. However, this necessitates a series of rigorous preclinical studies to characterize the dose-response relationship, evaluate retinal safety, and define the pharmacokinetic profile of ibrutinib when delivered via ocular routes. Moreover, any proposed clinical trial must incorporate comprehensive ophthalmologic monitoring given the known spectrum of ibrutinib-induced ocular side effects (Allouchery et al., 2021; Far et al., 2022).

In summary, while ibrutinib is primarily an approved oncologic agent, its underlying mechanism of inhibiting BTK—the kinase that modulates inflammasome priming in microglia and RPE cells—provides a strong scientific basis for its repurposing in dry AMD. The expected effects of reduced IL-1β and TNFα production, coupled with preserved lysosomal cathepsin activity and improved phagocytic function, align well with the therapeutic goals of mitigating chronic inflammation and preserving retinal integrity in dry AMD. Nevertheless, the potential ocular adverse effects and the challenges related to achieving a favorable therapeutic index in the retinal environment are significant concerns that must be addressed through further rigorous preclinical and clinical evaluations (Brandli et al., 2024; Allouchery et al., 2021). Overall, ibrutinib represents an intriguing candidate with a solid mechanistic rationale for dry AMD treatment; however, careful attention to formulation, dosing, and safety testing is warranted before advancing this repurposing strategy to later-stage clinical trials (Xie et al., 2021; Brandli et al., 2024; Korhonen, 2024).
