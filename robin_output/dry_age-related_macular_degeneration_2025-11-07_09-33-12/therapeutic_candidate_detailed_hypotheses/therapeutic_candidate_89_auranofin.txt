Proposal for Auranofin (TrxR inhibitor; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. A6733)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Auranofin is an orally bioavailable gold(I) complex that was first synthesized as a candidate to treat rheumatoid arthritis and is historically considered the first orally administered gold drug. Chemically, it is composed of a water‐soluble aurothioglucose moiety linked to a lipophilic triethylphosphine ligand, and, upon metabolism, it yields active gold species such as a triethylphosphine–gold(I) cation and gold–thioglucose derivatives (Abdalbari, 2025). Auranofin belongs to the class of organogold compounds, which have long been recognized for their anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties. In general, this class of compounds has been used primarily in the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis due to their ability to modulate immune cell activity, interfere with inflammatory cytokine production, and target redox-sensitive enzymes. Although originally developed and clinically validated for rheumatic diseases, research over the past decades has repurposed auranofin for anticancer, antiparasitic, antimicrobial, and more recently, for potential neuroprotective indications (Madeira, 2012; Abdalbari & Telleria, 2021a).  

Therapeutic History:  
Auranofin has an established therapeutic history as an anti-rheumatic agent with a clinical safety profile characterized over several decades of use. In the 1980s it was approved by regulatory agencies for the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis where it demonstrated comparable efficacy with fewer severe side effects relative to parenteral gold salts, while its oral delivery provided a significant improvement in patient compliance (Abdalbari, 2025). In addition to its use in rheumatology, auranofin has been studied for its anticancer properties, with investigations demonstrating its ability to induce reactive oxygen species (ROS), inhibit cell proliferation, and trigger apoptosis in various cancer models including ovarian, lung, and mesothelioma cells (Abdalbari & Telleria, 2021a; Mertens et al., 2023). Despite its broad repurposing interest in oncology and infectious diseases, auranofin has not been directly used for treating dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) or other retinal degenerations. However, the cellular mechanisms implicated in its pharmacological actions—particularly its effects on redox homeostasis and anti-inflammatory pathways—provide a compelling rationale for exploring its potential in dry AMD, a disease known to involve chronic oxidative stress, lysosomal dysfunction, and inflammation in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) (Abdalbari & Telleria, 2021b; Chen et al., 2023).  

Mechanism of Action:  
The primary molecular target of auranofin is thioredoxin reductase (TrxR), a critical selenoenzyme involved in maintaining cellular redox balance. Auranofin interacts with TrxR by binding preferentially to its redox-active selenocysteine residue, thereby irreversibly inhibiting its NADPH-dependent catalytic activity (Abdalbari, 2025). Inhibition of TrxR disrupts the thioredoxin (Trx) system leading to an accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxidative stress within the cell. This increase in ROS, while potentially cytotoxic at high levels, can also serve as a signal to activate the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway, a master regulator of antioxidant responses. Under conditions of elevated oxidative stress, Nrf2 translocates to the nucleus and binds to antioxidant response elements (ARE) in the regulatory regions of target genes, inducing the expression of several cytoprotective enzymes, including heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) and enzymes involved in glutathione synthesis and regeneration (Chen et al., 2016; Abdalbari & Telleria, 2021a).  

In addition to its redox-modulatory function, auranofin has been reported to affect lysosomal pathways. Preclinical studies in RPE-derived cells (ARPE-19) have shown that auranofin can attenuate lysosomal alkalinization and thereby improve lysosomal function, an effect that may translate into enhanced degradation of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) under oxidative stress conditions (Chen et al., 2016). This effect on lysosomal preservation is particularly relevant in the context of dry AMD, where the accumulation of undigested POS debris contributes to the formation of drusen and lipofuscin, leading to RPE dysfunction over time. Auranofin’s dual impact—disrupting redox homeostasis via TrxR inhibition while simultaneously triggering a compensatory Nrf2-mediated antioxidant response and preserving lysosomal function—constitutes an attractive mechanistic rationale for its repurposing into ocular indications (Abdalbari, 2025).  

Expected Effect:  
Based on the hypothesized mechanism, when applied to RPE cells, auranofin is expected to inhibit TrxR activity, thus initially promoting an increase in ROS production. This controlled elevation in ROS is anticipated to serve as a secondary signal for the activation of Nrf2, which in turn drives the transcription of antioxidant genes such as HO-1 and genes involved in glutathione synthesis, thereby restoring intracellular redox balance (Madeira, 2012; Abdalbari, 2025). In ARPE-19 cell assays, this cascade should lead to a measurable increase in cellular antioxidant capacity, as well as a reduction in oxidative damage markers. Furthermore, by preserving or even restoring lysosomal acidity and function, auranofin is expected to boost the degradation of POS, particularly in cells challenged with lipofuscin components such as A2E that are known to disrupt lysosomal function (Chen et al., 2016). The enhanced clearance of POS should, in theory, mitigate the accumulation of toxic byproducts that contribute to RPE senescence and the progression of dry AMD (Chen et al., 2023; Brodzka et al., 2024). Moreover, the improvement in lysosomal function may also translate into better maintenance of the RPE’s barrier function and phagocytic capacity, both of which are vital for retinal homeostasis. The oral safety profile established in rheumatoid arthritis supports the notion that, with proper dosing and perhaps targeted ocular delivery strategies, auranofin could be repurposed safely for ocular use, though detailed pharmacokinetic studies in ocular tissues would be required to confirm this (Abdalbari, 2025).  

Overall Evaluation:  
Auranofin presents several compelling strengths as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD. Its well-documented history as an orally administered anti-rheumatic agent with an established human safety profile allows for rapid progression into clinical testing if preliminary ocular efficacy is demonstrated. The mechanistic rationale is robust: by inhibiting TrxR, auranofin triggers an increase in ROS that activates the Nrf2 pathway, culminating in the upregulation of cytoprotective antioxidant genes, restoration of glutathione levels, and the preservation of lysosomal function—all of which are key factors in countering the pathogenic processes in dry AMD (Abdalbari & Telleria, 2021a; Mertens et al., 2023). In addition, preclinical evidence in RPE models indicates that auranofin can modulate signaling pathways such as EGFR/MAPK and that its effects are ROS-dependent, as demonstrated by the mitigation of toxicity with the antioxidant N-acetyl cysteine (Chen et al., 2016).  

Nevertheless, several weaknesses and challenges remain. Although the mechanistic data derived from cancer and non-ocular studies are promising, direct in vivo evidence of auranofin’s efficacy in AMD or related RPE dysfunction models is currently lacking. The cytotoxic nature of auranofin at higher concentrations observed in ARPE-19 cells necessitates careful determination of a dosing window that maximizes the beneficial activation of Nrf2 and lysosomal preservation without exacerbating oxidative damage or inducing cell death (Chen et al., 2016; Madeira, 2012). Furthermore, while the systemic safety of auranofin in rheumatic diseases is relatively well established, the specific requirements for ocular drug delivery—such as achieving sufficient retinal bioavailability while minimizing off-target effects—pose additional challenges that must be addressed through formulation innovation and rigorous preclinical testing (Abdalbari, 2025). Finally, because the pathogenesis of dry AMD is multifactorial, involving not only oxidative stress and lysosomal dysfunction but also inflammatory and complement-mediated processes, it is possible that auranofin alone may not be sufficiently efficacious in a clinical setting without combination with other therapeutic modalities designed to address these complementary pathways (Abdalbari & Telleria, 2021a; Chen et al., 2016).  

In summary, auranofin holds potential as a repurposed therapeutic for dry AMD due to its ability to modulate critical redox pathways via TrxR inhibition and subsequent Nrf2 activation, along with its potential to restore lysosomal function and improve the clearance of POS-related debris. Its established safety record in rheumatoid arthritis, combined with promising mechanistic underpinnings observed in both non-ocular and limited ocular cell models, supports further investigation into its use for dry AMD. However, given the current gaps in direct ocular evidence and the challenges of ocular drug delivery, additional preclinical studies specifically in AMD-relevant models (including detailed ARPE-19 studies, in vivo AMD models, and pharmacokinetic assessments in retinal tissue) are warranted to fully validate its therapeutic potential and safety for eventual clinical application (Chen et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2016; Madeira, 2012; Abdalbari, 2025).
