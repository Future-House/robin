Proposal for Oncostatin M (Cytokine; R&D Systems Cat. No. 287-OM)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Oncostatin M (OSM) is a cytokine originally identified as a member of the interleukin‐6 (IL-6) family and is naturally produced by various immune cells. Recombinant OSM, as provided by R&D Systems (Cat. No. 287-OM), is synthesized using recombinant DNA technology and purified to ensure bioactivity. As a protein therapeutic, OSM belongs to the class of cytokine-based drugs, which have historically been harnessed for their abilities to modulate inflammatory responses, regulate cell differentiation, and promote tissue repair. The recombinant formulation offers high purity and biological consistency, which makes it an attractive candidate for repurposing in ocular diseases. Prior studies have shown that cytokines from the IL-6 family, including OSM, CNTF, and LIF, are capable of inducing cellular protective mechanisms in the retina and may influence inflammatory and repair pathways (Brown, 2015; Pacione, 2004).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, OSM has been investigated in various models of retinal degeneration where its administration was correlated with protection of retinal ganglion cells and photoreceptors. In a rat model of retinal degeneration, OSM injection led to a reduction in photoreceptor loss, suggesting its neuroprotective potential in ocular pathologies (Brown, 2015; Xia et al., 2011). Beyond the retina, evidence indicates that OSM can influence epithelial repair and barrier integrity in tissues affected by inflammation, as seen in studies of airway epithelial cells in diseases such as asthma and allergic rhinitis (Pothoven & Schleimer, 2017). In terms of specific use against dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD), there is no direct clinical history of OSM application; however, the body of work on cytokine-based interventions in ocular diseases is promising and supports repurposing efforts. Notably, the success in using CNTF—a cytokine with mechanistic similarities to OSM—in clinical studies to preserve photoreceptors and improve retinal function underscores the potential of this class of agents for treating degenerative retinal disorders (Xia et al., 2011).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, the mechanism of action of OSM is initiated by its binding to the OSM receptor (OSMR), which forms a heterodimeric complex with the signal-transducing receptor subunit gp130. This receptor engagement prompts the recruitment of Janus kinases (JAKs), including Jak2 and Tyk2, leading to the phosphorylation of specific tyrosine residues on the receptor and subsequent activation of the Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (STAT3) (Pacione, 2004; Richards, 2013). Once phosphorylated, STAT3 forms homodimers that translocate to the nucleus and bind to STAT-responsive elements in the promoter regions of target genes. Among the genes regulated by STAT3 are those implicated in the acute phase response, such as A2m, as well as genes like Cebpd, which are involved in immune and pro-inflammatory responses (Pacione, 2004).

In retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells—critical for maintaining photoreceptor health—the activation of STAT3 may have multiple beneficial effects. Studies have shown that OSM not only induces STAT3 phosphorylation in RPE cells, but also phosphorylates ERK1/2 MAP kinase, indicating a broad spectrum of signal transduction modulation that influences cell survival, differentiation, and cytoskeletal organization (Li et al., 2011). Given that cytoskeletal reorganization is essential for the formation of phagocytic cups and subsequent photoreceptor outer segment (POS) internalization, OSM-mediated STAT3 activation is hypothesized to contribute directly to these processes. It is important to note that similar effects have been observed with related IL-6 family cytokines in various epithelial models, supporting the notion that STAT3 signaling plays a pivotal role in establishing and maintaining epithelial barrier integrity (Richards, 2013; Pacione, 2004; Tiffen et al., 2008).

Expected Effect:  
The central hypothesis proposed for repurposing OSM in dry AMD is that when administered at concentrations around the EC₅₀ (~0.1 nM), OSM will robustly activate STAT3 in RPE cells. This activation is expected to drive the transcription of genes that encode tight junction proteins and cytoskeletal elements critical for the morphological and functional integrity of the RPE layer. Enhanced expression of these genes would, in theory, facilitate reorganization of the cytoskeleton, thereby enabling the efficient formation of phagocytic cups. As phagocytosis of shed POS is a central function of the RPE—ensuring the renewal of the photoreceptor layer—improvements in this process may directly translate into enhanced retinal health and slow disease progression in dry AMD (Li et al., 2011; Richards, 2013).

Evidence from the literature suggests that STAT3 activation plays a role in cytoskeletal dynamics and barrier function. For example, in studies with human fetal RPE cells, OSM stimulation led to phosphorylated STAT3 accumulation, which is correlated with modifications in cell shape, inhibition of uncontrolled proliferation, and potentially enhanced phagocytosis (Li et al., 2011). Moreover, the beneficial effects witnessed in similar cytokine treatments, such as with CNTF, reinforce the notion that a shared signaling cascade involving STAT3 not only supports neuroprotection but also augments cellular processes essential for maintaining the RPE’s barrier functions and phagocytic competence (Xia et al., 2011; Mori et al., 2024). However, while the expected induction of tight junction and cytoskeletal gene expression is mechanistically plausible based on the STAT3 pathway, direct evidence linking OSM-induced gene expression specifically to enhanced phagocytic cup formation in RPE cells remains to be conclusively established and would benefit from further in vitro and in vivo validation (Pothoven & Schleimer, 2017; Pacione, 2004).

Overall Evaluation:  
The repurposing of Oncostatin M for the treatment of dry AMD is supported by a mechanistic rationale that draws on its established role in STAT3-mediated cellular signaling and its previous demonstrations of neuroprotective effects in retinal degeneration models. One of the key strengths of OSM as a candidate lies in its ability to activate STAT3—a transcription factor that governs a spectrum of genes involved in cell survival, cytoskeletal reorganization, and barrier integrity. This is highly relevant in the context of dry AMD, where RPE dysfunction and impaired phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments contribute to disease progression (Brown, 2015; Pacione, 2004). Additionally, the availability of a high-quality recombinant form, combined with prior research in ocular models showing evidence of photoreceptor protection and potential enhancement of retinal function, renders OSM an attractive prospect for repurposing (Xia et al., 2011; Mori et al., 2024).

Despite these strengths, several challenges and potential weaknesses warrant consideration. First, although OSM-induced STAT3 activation is well documented, its downstream effects on tight junction proteins and the cytoskeletal apparatus within RPE cells have not been conclusively demonstrated in the context of dry AMD. There is some concern that while low to moderate doses of OSM may promote cellular repair and barrier stabilization, higher concentrations—or chronic overexpression—might induce epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and compromise barrier integrity, as seen in other epithelial tissues (Pothoven & Schleimer, 2017). This dual nature of OSM’s action necessitates careful dosing and thorough characterization of its effects on RPE function to avoid adverse outcomes such as enhanced inflammation or unwanted EMT that could exacerbate retinal degeneration.

Furthermore, specific studies in human RPE cells indicate that while OSM robustly activates STAT3 and ERK1/2 pathways, it can also lead to inhibitory effects on cell proliferation and survival, suggesting that its impact on cell dynamics is highly context-dependent (Li et al., 2011; Tiffen et al., 2008). This raises questions regarding the optimal balance between achieving sufficient STAT3 activation to drive cytoskeletal and tight junction gene expression and avoiding potential cytostatic or cytotoxic effects that may impair overall RPE health. There is also a need to determine whether the upregulation of target genes such as A2m and Cebpd—documented in other cellular contexts—translates into functional improvements in POS internalization and maintenance of the RPE barrier in the specific setting of dry AMD (Pacione, 2004).

Moreover, while studies in rat models of retinal degeneration have shown that OSM can reduce photoreceptor loss and promote the regeneration of outer segments (Järgen et al., 2017), these effects have not been directly linked to changes in RPE phagocytic activity or tight junction integrity. Given that dry AMD involves chronic, low-grade inflammation and oxidative stress, it is essential to ascertain whether OSM’s systemic inflammatory modulation would have beneficial or deleterious effects over the longer term. The balance between its regenerative potential and the possibility of promoting inflammatory cascades or pathological angiogenesis (despite some reports of reduced pathological neovascularization) must be rigorously tested in preclinical models that faithfully recapitulate the multifactorial nature of dry AMD pathology (Pothoven & Schleimer, 2017; Mori et al., 2024).

Another aspect to consider is the cross-talk between OSM-mediated STAT3 signaling and other cellular pathways that regulate cytoskeletal dynamics and cellular adhesion in the RPE. For instance, the cytokine’s ability to modulate both the ERK1/2 MAP kinase pathway and STAT family proteins suggests that it operates at a nodal point that could influence diverse cellular functions. This complexity, while offering an opportunity to address multiple pathological processes simultaneously, also raises the possibility of off-target effects that could undermine the therapeutic goal. Detailed dose-response studies and time-course experiments will be imperative to delineate the precise window in which OSM functions as a promoter of barrier integrity and efficient phagocytosis, rather than triggering pathological changes detrimental to RPE function (Li et al., 2011; Tiffen et al., 2008).

In terms of translational potential, the strategy of repurposing an existing recombinant cytokine such as OSM offers significant advantages, including the possibility of leveraging existing manufacturing, formulation, and regulatory data. This could expedite the transition from preclinical studies to early-phase clinical trials in patients with dry AMD. Nonetheless, the preclinical evidence specific to dry AMD remains preliminary, and further research is needed to systematically assess OSM’s effects on RPE cell morphology, tight junction formation, cytoskeletal organization, and phagocytic efficiency. It would be beneficial to conduct detailed in vitro experiments using human RPE cell lines or primary RPE cultures, evaluating not only short-term signaling events (e.g., STAT3 phosphorylation kinetics) but also long-term outcomes such as maintenance of the barrier phenotype and resistance to oxidative or inflammatory stress. Such studies could be complemented by in vivo investigations in animal models that mimic the key features of dry AMD, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of the balance between beneficial and potentially harmful effects of OSM in the ocular environment (Li et al., 2011; Pacione, 2004).

Overall, Oncostatin M presents as an innovative and mechanistically plausible candidate for the treatment of dry AMD. Its ability to activate STAT3 is intrinsically linked to cytoskeletal reorganization and epithelial barrier maintenance, processes that are critical for proper RPE function and the clearance of shed POS. The preclinical data from retinal degeneration models, along with evidence from related cytokines, lend credence to the hypothesis that OSM could enhance POS internalization and overall retinal health when applied at carefully optimized concentrations (Brown, 2015; Xia et al., 2011). However, the candidate’s dualistic role in modulating epithelial barrier function—including the risk of inducing EMT and compromising cellular integrity at inappropriate doses—necessitates a cautious and methodical approach to further development (Pothoven & Schleimer, 2017).

In conclusion, while the repurposing of Oncostatin M for dry AMD is underpinned by compelling mechanistic insights and supportive data from alternative ocular applications, significant gaps remain in our understanding of its specific effects on RPE cells. Detailed investigations into the optimal dosing regimen, the kinetics of STAT3 activation, and the downstream transcriptional events governing tight junction and cytoskeletal gene expression are crucial. Only through rigorous preclinical evaluation—encompassing both in vitro mechanism-based assays and in vivo efficacy studies—can we validate whether OSM truly possesses the capacity to enhance phagocytic cup formation and POS internalization as hypothesized. The balance between therapeutic benefit and potential adverse effects must be carefully managed to harness the full potential of OSM as a treatment for dry age-related macular degeneration (Pacione, 2004; Li et al., 2011; Richards, 2013).
