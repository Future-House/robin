Proposal for DPRK1 inhibitor KYP-2047 (Prolyl oligopeptidase inhibitor; MedKoo Cat. No. 394609)

Below is a comprehensive evaluation of the prolyl oligopeptidase inhibitor KYP-2047 as a potential repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD). This report is organized into several sections: an overview of the candidate, its therapeutic history, an in‐depth discussion of its mechanism of action, the expected effects in the proposed assay, and an overall evaluation that weighs its strengths and weaknesses.

─────────────────────────────  
Overview of Therapeutic Candidate

KYP-2047 is a synthetic small molecule belonging to the peptidomimetic class of compounds that target the enzyme prolyl oligopeptidase (PREP), also known as prolyl endopeptidase (POP). Chemically designated as 4-phenylbutanoyl-L-prolyl-2(S)-cyanopyrrolidine, this compound was synthesized following methods established by Dr. Elina Jarho at the University of Eastern Finland (Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 44–47). Its design represents an evolution from early substrate-like inhibitors, with modifications at specific positions – including a three-carbon aliphatic chain at the P3 position instead of the more commonly used carbamate groups seen in predecessors like ZPP – thereby improving the compound’s overall potency, selectivity, and drug-like properties (Guardiola et al., 2018, pp. 3–4).

Belonging to a well-known class of PREP inhibitors, KYP-2047 shares common chemical and biophysical properties with analogous peptidomimetic agents that have been primarily designed to interfere with the catalytic activity of a serine protease that cleaves proline-containing peptides. Given its low water solubility, KYP-2047 is typically formulated in saline containing 5% Tween® 80 for experimental studies, which is consistent with formulation challenges observed with many peptidomimetic drug candidates (Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 44–47). In recent years, the broader class of PREP inhibitors has been explored for a variety of indications, most notably in neurological conditions as well as in certain cancer models. The research impetus behind this class arises from the enzyme’s presumed role in peptide metabolism, where the inhibition of PREP could alter levels of bioactive peptides that participate in cellular homeostasis. Although these agents were originally developed as anti-amnesic candidates, their biological actions have been found to extend to anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective effects (Toppila et al., 2023, pp. 12–13).

─────────────────────────────  
Therapeutic History

The therapeutic exploration of PREP inhibitors dates back to the 1970s when the enzyme was first implicated in the regulation of proline-containing peptides associated with memory formation and synaptic plasticity (Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 18–22). Numerous compounds in this class—including S-17092, JTP-4819, and others—were advanced into preclinical studies and even early-phase clinical trials for cognitive enhancement in neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease and senile dementia (Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 39–41, 70–72). However, despite initial encouraging in vitro and animal data, these agents failed to progress successfully past phase II clinical trials in humans, most likely due to challenges with efficacy, blood-brain barrier penetration, or the complexity of the enzyme’s in vivo function (Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 18–22, 70–72).

In parallel, PREP inhibitors have been evaluated for other indications. For instance, in a study involving oral squamous cell carcinoma, KYP-2047 demonstrated potent PREP inhibition in the mouse brain along with promising anti-tumor effects, highlighting the compound’s potential utility beyond central nervous system disorders (Scuderi, 2023, pp. 133–135). Furthermore, recent work has begun to explore the ocular applications of PREP inhibition. There is a suggestion in the literature that inhibition of prolyl oligopeptidase may represent “a promising pathway to prevent the progression of age-related macular degeneration” (Toppila et al., 2023, pp. 12–13). Notably, recent in vitro studies using human retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells with defective proteasomal clearance have shown that KYP-2047 exhibits cytoprotective and anti-inflammatory properties, thereby indirectly supporting its potential use in dry AMD, even though no clinical trials or prior direct therapeutic applications in retinal diseases have yet been reported (Toppila et al., 2023, pp. 4–6, 9–11).

While the primary historical focus of PREP inhibitors has been on neurological and cognitive applications, the body of literature also contains several exploratory studies in inflammatory and cancer contexts (Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 18–22; Toppila et al., 2023, pp. 13–14). Thus, even though the bulk of previous applications have not directly targeted dry AMD, the emerging evidence supporting cytoprotection and modulation of proteolytic and autophagy pathways in RPE cells provides a rationale for repurposing KYP-2047 in ocular disease states.

─────────────────────────────  
Mechanism of Action

The biological target of KYP-2047 is the serine protease prolyl oligopeptidase (PREP), which is responsible for cleaving peptide bonds at the carboxyl side of internal proline residues in small peptides (typically less than ~30 amino acids) (Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 22–24, 44–47). PREP is widely expressed in the central nervous system and peripheral tissues and has been implicated in multiple physiological processes including neuropeptide regulation and cell signaling. At the molecular level, the mechanism of action involves the binding of KYP-2047 to the enzyme’s active site, more specifically interacting with the catalytic serine residue. Many PREP inhibitors, including those in the class to which KYP-2047 belongs, are designed as substrate-like pseudopeptides that exploit the structure of the enzyme’s binding pockets (S1, S2, and S3) to achieve selectivity and high-affinity binding (Guardiola et al., 2018, pp. 3–4; Toppila et al., 2023, pp. 1–2).

The inhibition by KYP-2047 is characterized as “tight-binding” with slow dissociation, meaning that once the inhibitor occupies the active site of PREP, it remains bound for a significant duration, thereby effectively reducing the enzyme’s catalytic turnover (Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 70–72). In neurological models, inhibition of PREP has been associated with enhanced clearance of toxic protein aggregates such as alpha-synuclein, improved autophagic flux, and reduction in oxidative stress (Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 85–88; Toppila et al., 2023, pp. 9–11). The current therapeutic hypothesis in the context of dry AMD extrapolates these findings to retinal pigment epithelial cells.

In particular, the proposed mechanism posits that elevated PREP activity in RPE cells contributes to an accumulation of proline-rich peptides that, in turn, inhibit the activity of key lysosomal enzymes such as cathepsin D and lysosomal cathepsin L. These cathepsins are critical for the degradation of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) and turnover of cellular debris. In dry AMD, defective protein clearance mechanisms, including impaired proteasomal and lysosomal function, result in the buildup of toxic deposits, such as A2E, and contribute to RPE dysfunction. By blocking PREP activity with KYP-2047 (which has an IC₅₀ in the low nanomolar range, reported to be approximately 30 nM), the proposed effect is to reduce the formation or accumulation of inhibitory peptides, thereby relieving the suppression on cathepsin activity. In addition, KYP-2047-mediated PREP inhibition may trigger downstream intracellular events such as autophagy activation and modulation of MAPK signaling pathways—including p38 and ERK1/2—which have been observed in other cell types and are known to reduce oxidative stress and inflammation (Toppila et al., 2023, pp. 4–6; Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 85–88).

At the biochemical level, these molecular interactions involve the inhibitor “locking in” the PREP enzyme in a conformational state that either prevents substrate access or alters the dynamics of peptide release once cleavage has occurred. Although the effects on neuropeptide levels (e.g., substance P, neurotensin) have been studied in central nervous system models—with mixed and sometimes counterintuitive outcomes (such as decreased acetylcholine levels) (Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 8–16, 70–72)—the rationale for ocular repurposing rests on the idea that the same enzymatic inhibition can counteract the accumulation of cathepsin-inhibiting peptides in RPE cells. Notably, some studies have suggested that PREP inhibition can positively modulate autophagy, a process that is critical for cellular clearance mechanisms. Given that impaired autophagy is a central feature in the pathogenesis of dry AMD, a compound like KYP-2047 that both reduces toxic peptide accumulation and enhances proteolytic pathways presents an attractive, multitargeted strategy (Toppila et al., 2023, pp. 12–13).

─────────────────────────────  
Expected Effect in Dry AMD

The core hypothesis driving the repurposing of KYP-2047 for dry AMD is that inhibition of PREP will reduce the buildup of proline-rich peptides that otherwise impair lysosomal cathepsin activity. In RPE cells, efficient degradation of POS via phagocytosis and lysosomal function is essential for maintaining retinal homeostasis. In the setting of dry AMD, decreased cathepsin D and L activities are implicated in the inefficient clearance of cellular debris, leading to the accumulation of lipofuscin and A2E, ultimately causing RPE dysfunction.

In the proposed assay, RPE cells—typically the ARPE-19 cell line—will be challenged in an environment mimicking AMD pathology; for instance, by loading them with A2E and subjecting the cells to proteasomal stress. Under these conditions, the activity of lysosomal enzymes such as cathepsin D and L is compromised, and POS degradation is impaired. The application of KYP-2047 is expected to inhibit PREP activity with high potency, which theoretically will lead to a reduction in the concentration of peptide substrates that interfere with cathepsin enzymes (Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 44–47; Toppila et al., 2023, pp. 4–6).

The anticipated cascade of events is as follows:  
1. KYP-2047, at concentrations close to its low nanomolar IC₅₀, will effectively bind to and inhibit PREP in RPE cells.  
2. This inhibition will prevent the excessive cleavage of proline-containing peptides, thereby reducing the pool of inhibitory peptides that could otherwise bind to and decrease cathepsin D and L activities.  
3. As a consequence, the restoration of cathepsin activity is expected to improve the degradation of POS components as well as the clearance of other toxic debris, which is a hallmark of dry AMD pathology.  
4. Additionally, the compound’s reported anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective effects, demonstrated in ARPE-19 cells with defective proteasomal clearance, may further contribute to preserving RPE cell health by curtailing inflammatory cytokine production and oxidative stress (Toppila et al., 2023, pp. 1–2, 4–6).

It is also noteworthy that the mechanistic framework is further supported by the observation that PREP inhibition in other models has led to enhanced autophagy and improved clearance of aggregated proteins. In the context of AMD, effective POS degradation is not only dependent on cathepsin function but also on efficient autophagic flux. By indirectly modulating these pathways, KYP-2047 could offer dual benefits: restoration of lysosomal enzyme activity and bolstering of intracellular clearance mechanisms. Thus, the expected in vitro endpoints would likely include increased cathepsin D/L activity, elevated POS degradation (as detected by uptake assays), reduced levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-6, IL-8, IL-1β), and diminished oxidative stress markers (Toppila et al., 2023, pp. 2–4, 4–6).

Moreover, the rationale includes supporting evidence from neurological and cancer research where PREP inhibition by compounds like KYP-2047 improved cell viability and reduced stress responses. Although previous clinical trials in central nervous system indications did not achieve long-term success, the safety data accrued from these studies may lower the translational barrier for ocular applications, provided that proper formulation and localized delivery (or systemic approaches with ocular distribution) can be achieved (Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 66–68; Guardiola et al., 2018, pp. 4–5).

─────────────────────────────  
Overall Evaluation

The evaluation of KYP-2047 as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD involves weighing both its promising aspects and its uncertainties. On the positive side, KYP-2047 is a well-defined, highly potent inhibitor of PREP with an established chemical profile and a strong foundation in preclinical studies. Its synthetic origin as a peptidomimetic compound designed to bind the active site of PREP is well documented (Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 44–47; Guardiola et al., 2018, pp. 3–4), and the compound’s low nanomolar potency provides confidence that even modest drug exposures may achieve effective enzyme inhibition.

The repurposing strategy capitalizes on emerging evidence that PREP inhibitors can exert cytoprotective and anti-inflammatory effects in RPE cells. In vitro studies conducted in ARPE-19 cells with impaired proteasomal clearance have demonstrated that KYP-2047 can reduce inflammatory cytokine production and promote cell viability (Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 18–22; Toppila et al., 2023, pp. 4–6). Given the central role of inflammation and defective lysosomal clearance in dry AMD, these findings provide a mechanistic rationale for testing KYP-2047 in this indication.

Furthermore, the proposed mechanism—whereby inhibition of PREP reduces the formation of inhibitory proline-rich peptides that suppress cathepsin activity—is mechanistically attractive. Restoring the function of key lysosomal enzymes such as cathepsin D and L could feasibly improve the degradation of POS, a process known to be compromised in dry AMD. This dual-targeted approach (improving both enzyme activity and reducing cell stress) is particularly compelling in the context of a multifactorial disease such as AMD (Toppila et al., 2023, pp. 12–13).

Despite these strengths, several potential weaknesses and challenges must be acknowledged. First, while extensive preclinical work has been carried out in neurological models—and there exists some early in vitro evidence of benefit in RPE cells—the direct connection between PREP activity and the inhibition of cathepsin function in the context of RPE biology remains largely hypothetical. The literature currently lacks robust in vivo studies in ocular models of dry AMD demonstrating that PREP inhibition translates into measurable improvements in retinal function and morphology. Historical clinical trials of PREP inhibitors for cognitive applications have encountered difficulties due to inconsistent efficacy, which raises concerns about the translation of in vitro findings to clinical benefits (Scuderi, 2023, pp. 133–135; Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 39–41).

Second, the formulation challenges associated with KYP-2047 cannot be overlooked. Its low water solubility necessitates specialized carriers (such as saline with 5% Tween® 80), which may complicate delivery to the eye, especially when considering the need for localized administration to achieve therapeutic concentrations in the RPE. Appropriate ocular delivery systems (e.g., intravitreal injections or sustained-release implants) would need to be developed and optimized, which adds a layer of complexity to the overall drug development program (Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 44–47).

Another area of concern is the historical inconsistency of PREP inhibitor effects in clinical studies. Although there is evidence of neuroprotective benefits, the fact that several PREP inhibitors failed to advance through the clinical development pipeline for neurological indications suggests that the enzyme’s true physiological role remains incompletely defined. In the absence of a clear and reproducible biomarker for PREP inhibition in vivo, there is a risk that the biochemical benefits seen in cell culture studies may not be recapitulated in the complex environment of the human retina (Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 18–22, 70–72).

Moreover, because PREP is a ubiquitous enzyme expressed in many tissues, systemic administration of KYP-2047 might lead to off-target effects that could limit its therapeutic window. Although the safety profile observed in early neurological trials is encouraging, targeted delivery to the eye (to minimize systemic exposure) will be crucial if KYP-2047 is to be repurposed for dry AMD (Toppila et al., 2023, pp. 12–13; Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 66–68).

Finally, while the proposed mechanism that links elevated PREP activity with the inhibition of lysosomal cathepsins is intriguing, it remains to be validated by direct experimental data specifically in RPE cells. The current literature, including the recent study by Toppila and colleagues, primarily focuses on the cytoprotective and anti-inflammatory actions of KYP-2047 in ARPE-19 cells, but detailed biochemical analyses linking PREP inhibition mechanistically to improved cathepsin-mediated degradation of POS are still needed (Toppila et al., 2023, pp. 2–4, 4–6).

─────────────────────────────  
Conclusion

In summary, KYP-2047 is an established prolyl oligopeptidase inhibitor with a well-characterized chemical and pharmacological profile, offering promising features as a candidate for repurposing in the treatment of dry age-related macular degeneration. Its ability to inhibit PREP at low nanomolar concentrations, coupled with demonstrated cytoprotective and anti-inflammatory effects in preclinical RPE models, aligns well with the therapeutic need to restore lysosomal cathepsin activity and improve photoreceptor outer segment degradation in dry AMD (Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 44–47; Toppila et al., 2023, pp. 4–6, 12–13).

The strategy to mitigate the accumulation of inhibitory proline-rich peptides and thereby reinstate effective lysosomal function is mechanistically plausible and supported by related studies in neural tissues. Nevertheless, the exact role of PREP in the modulation of cathepsin activity in RPE cells remains to be fully elucidated. In addition, the challenges of achieving appropriate ocular drug delivery and the historical precedents of inconsistent efficacy in PREP inhibitor clinical trials represent significant hurdles that must be addressed in future studies (Guardiola et al., 2018, pp. 3–4; Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 39–41).

Overall, while KYP-2047 brings strengths in terms of potency and a partially validated cytoprotective profile in in vitro models, its repurposing for dry AMD would require further in-depth preclinical studies. These studies should include in vivo efficacy assessments in relevant animal models of AMD, comprehensive pharmacokinetic and ocular distribution evaluations, and mechanistic experiments that clearly link PREP inhibition with improved cathepsin and lysosomal function in the RPE. Should these studies confirm the initial promising results, the favorable safety profile observed in prior neurological trials may indeed support the rapid translation of KYP-2047 into early-phase clinical testing for dry AMD (Toppila et al., 2023, pp. 1–2, 12–13; Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 66–68).

In conclusion, KYP-2047 represents a promising, albeit still exploratory, approach to targeting a novel biochemical pathway in the context of dry AMD. The rationale is supported by a solid foundation of preclinical data in other disease models, yet its success in ocular repurposing will depend on overcoming formulation challenges and further clarifying the mechanistic underpinnings of its action in retinal pigment epithelial cells.

─────────────────────────────  
Strengths and Weaknesses Summary

Strengths:  
•  KYP-2047 is a potent, well-characterized PREP inhibitor with a strong synthetic and chemical development history (Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 44–47; Guardiola et al., 2018, pp. 3–4).  
•  Its low nanomolar IC₅₀ provides confidence in its biochemical efficacy, and available preclinical data indicate cytoprotective and anti-inflammatory effects in RPE cell models (Toppila et al., 2023, pp. 4–6; Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 18–22).  
•  The drug class has been extensively studied in neurological and cancer contexts, offering insights into its safety profile and potential mechanisms such as autophagy activation and reduced oxidative stress (Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 70–72, 85–88).  
•  The hypothesis that PREP activity leads to the accumulation of cathepsin-inhibiting peptides is mechanistically plausible, which could lead to restoration of lysosomal enzyme activity in RPE cells (Toppila et al., 2023, pp. 12–13).  

Weaknesses:  
•  Historically, PREP inhibitors have shown inconsistent efficacy in clinical trials for neurological applications; it remains uncertain whether the benefits will translate to the ocular setting (Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 18–22, 39–41).  
•  Direct evidence linking PREP inhibition with improvements in cathepsin activity and POS degradation in RPE cells is still limited, and further mechanistic studies are required (Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 44–47; Toppila et al., 2023, pp. 4–6).  
•  The low aqueous solubility of KYP-2047 necessitates complex formulations, which might complicate ocular drug delivery and could require novel delivery systems to achieve therapeutic concentrations in the retina (Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 44–47).  
•  Systemic safety and potential off-target effects remain a concern, given that PREP is widely expressed and the compound’s systemic clearance and ocular bioavailability have yet to be thoroughly characterized in vivo (Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 66–68).

─────────────────────────────  
Final Remarks

Based on the current literature and the data available, KYP-2047 exhibits several attractive features for repurposing in dry AMD, especially regarding its potential to restore lysosomal function in the RPE by reducing the inhibitory load on cathepsins. However, while the biochemical rationale is compelling and early in vitro data are promising, significant challenges remain. Translational steps such as in vivo efficacy verification in AMD models, optimization of ocular formulation, and further elucidation of the mechanistic pathway are essential before proceeding to clinical development. Thus, KYP-2047 warrants further preclinical evaluation with a specific focus on its ocular pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetics, which will ultimately determine its viability as a novel therapeutic candidate against dry age-related macular degeneration (Toppila et al., 2023, pp. 4–6, 12–13; Jalkanen, 2012, pp. 66–68).

This comprehensive review supports the notion that repurposing KYP-2047 for dry AMD is a promising strategy, but caution must be exercised given the prior challenges encountered with the PREP inhibitor class in other indications. Future studies should be designed to address these gaps and de-risk the development plan before large-scale clinical trials are considered.
