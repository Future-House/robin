Proposal for Probenecid (OAT inhibitor; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. P8761)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Probenecid is an old, small‐molecule drug originally developed in the mid‐20th century as a uricosuric agent for the treatment of gout. It is a benzoic acid derivative that gained clinical utility by inhibiting renal tubular organic anion transporters (OATs), thereby reducing the excretion of uric acid and concomitantly increasing the plasma levels of antibiotics when co‐administered. As a member of the OAT inhibitor class, probenecid has the unique property of modulating membrane transport processes by binding to and inhibiting transport proteins that mediate the efflux of diverse anionic compounds. Its chemical properties, including high lipophilicity and significant plasma protein binding, have led to its usage not only in gout and antibiotic “boosting” regimens but also in experimental settings within the central nervous system and the ocular compartment, where it modulates the activity of transporters critical for drug bioavailability. The candidate proposed for repurposing in dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) is envisioned to leverage the drug’s capacity to inhibit organic anion transporters expressed in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells, thereby modifying intracellular signaling cascades. In particular, by inhibiting OATs and related transporters on the apical RPE membrane—a compartment known to display probenecid‐sensitive transport activities—there is a hypothesis that intracellular cyclic AMP (cAMP) could be augmented, leading to activation of protein kinase A (PKA). This falls in line with the broader therapeutic strategy of repurposing well‐characterized OAT inhibitors for their potential to modulate intracellular signaling and cellular functions beyond their classical renal roles (García-Rodríguez et al., 2023; Schlosshauer & Steuer, 2005).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, probenecid has had a long and well-documented clinical history. Since its advent as a treatment for gout, probenecid has been utilized to inhibit renal excretion of antibiotics and other compounds, capitalizing on its ability to block OATs such as OAT1 and OAT3, as evidenced by its measured inhibitory concentrations (IC₅₀ values in the low micromolar range) (García-Rodríguez et al., 2023). Over the years, its usage has expanded into preclinical studies within the central nervous system, where its inhibition of organic anion transporters and pannexin 1 (Panx1) channels has been explored as a means to modulate neuroinflammation and enhance drug delivery across blood–brain barrier-like structures (García-Rodríguez et al., 2023; Silverman et al., 2008). In the ocular arena, several studies have employed probenecid to probe its capabilities as a transporter modulator—demonstrating effects on ocular drug disposition, retinal electrophysiologic function in light-damage models, and reductions in inflammatory markers in acute animal models (Nor et al., 2021). Despite these encouraging preclinical findings, it is important to note that there are no direct or published clinical studies employing probenecid for the treatment of dry AMD or diseases with similar pathophysiology. Although the literature includes a variety of therapeutic investigations involving retinal cell lines, light-damage models, and studies on ocular transporter expression, none have yet formally evaluated a protocol wherein probenecid is used specifically to enhance photoreceptor outer segment (POS) uptake in the RPE via modulation of intracellular cAMP signaling. Thus, while the drug’s history lends it safety and a rich background of transporter pharmacology, its application in chronic degenerative retinal diseases such as dry AMD remains unvalidated (Mannermaa et al., 2009; Hass et al., 2023).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, probenecid is classically known to inhibit organic anion transporters (OATs) and members of the organic anion transporting polypeptides (OATPs). Experimental studies have demonstrated that probenecid binds to and inhibits OAT1 and OAT3, with IC₅₀ values reported at approximately 12.3 μM for OAT1 and 4.93 μM for OAT3 (García-Rodríguez et al., 2023). Moreover, in retinal cells, evidence indicates that there exists a probenecid-inhibitable organic anion transporter on the apical membrane of RPE cells, implicating its potential to modulate substrate transport in this tissue (Schlosshauer & Steuer, 2005). The candidate hypothesis proposes that inhibition of these transporters will lead to intracellular accumulation of cyclic nucleotides, specifically cAMP, since proteins such as MRP4 and MRP5—which are expressed in ARPE-19 and primary human RPE cells—have been implicated in the homeostasis of cAMP and cGMP (Mannermaa et al., 2009). These transporters, sensitive to probenecid, normally export cyclic nucleotides and other substrates; thus, their inhibition could potentially lead to an elevation in intracellular cAMP levels. Increased cAMP is well known to activate PKA, which in turn phosphorylates a host of downstream targets involved in cytoskeletal dynamics, including actin remodeling factors. Such cytoskeletal remodeling is thought to be critical for the formation of the phagocytic cup during the process of POS uptake by the RPE—a process that is impaired in dry AMD. 

Notably, some literature has documented that organic anion transport inhibitors can suppress certain cAMP-induced responses. For instance, García and Burnside (Hass et al., 2024) reported that suppression of a cAMP-induced pigment granule aggregation in RPE by organic anion transport inhibitors occurs, suggesting that blocking these transporters may not always lead to a straightforward increase in all cAMP-mediated cytoskeletal effects. The intricate balance between cAMP’s multiple downstream effects must therefore be considered. Additionally, probenecid is also known to block pannexin-1 channels, which are involved in ATP release and subsequent inflammatory signaling (Silverman et al., 2008; Yang, 2017). While inhibition of pannexin channels could contribute to reduced inflammation—a desirable outcome in retinal degenerations—the direct link between pannexin blockade and augmented cAMP/PKA signaling in the context of RPE phagocytosis remains speculative. Experimental evidence from ARPE-19 cells also shows that probenecid can inhibit P2X7 receptor/pannexin-1-dependent membrane pore formation induced by oxysterols, though this has been more closely associated with modulation of cell death pathways and inflammatory responses rather than enhancement of phagocytosis (Olivier et al., 2016). Moreover, work demonstrating that probenecid can block the uptake of Firsocostat—a drug that requires an OATP for cellular entry—provides direct evidence of its ability to inhibit transporter-mediated processes in RPE cells (Hass et al., 2023). However, these studies do not directly address the impact on intracellular cAMP levels, PKA activation, actin dynamics, or phagocytic cup formation. In summary, while the mechanistic rationale for utilizing probenecid to modulate RPE cell physiology is grounded in its well-described inhibitory effects on organic anion transporters and its potential to influence cyclic nucleotide homeostasis, the literature does not yet provide a definitive demonstration that these effects translate into enhanced PKA activation or improved phagocytic uptake of POS in the RPE (Mannermaa et al., 2009; Lee & Pelis, 2016; Hass et al., 2023).

Expected Effect:  
The proposed therapeutic hypothesis is that treatment with probenecid will inhibit OATs (and possibly OATPs and MRPs) expressed on the apical surface of RPE cells, resulting in an elevation of intracellular cAMP levels. This, in turn, is expected to augment PKA activity within the cell. Elevated PKA activity is known to drive a range of cellular processes including the modulation of actin cytoskeletal architecture, which is crucial for the formation of phagocytic cups during the process of POS internalization. In the RPE, daily phagocytosis of shed photoreceptor outer segments is essential for photoreceptor maintenance, and dysregulation of this process is implicated in the pathogenesis of dry AMD. Conceptually, probenecid is hypothesized to “boost” the phagocytic capability of RPE cells by pharmacologically inducing conditions that favor actin polymerization and cytoskeletal rearrangements associated with efficient formation of the phagocytic cup. 

Further support for the relevance of cAMP in RPE lysosomal and phagocytic functions comes from studies with other compounds; for example, P2Y12 receptor antagonism (as seen with ticagrelor) leads to lowered lysosomal pH and improved clearance of autofluorescent material in RPE cells from ABCA4−/− models (Lu et al., 2018). Such work underscores the link between cAMP elevation and improved catabolic function in RPE cells, providing a proof-of-concept that activation of cAMP/PKA signaling pathways can be beneficial. However, to date there is no published data directly demonstrating that probenecid increases cAMP levels or PKA activity in ARPE-19 cells, nor is there evidence that treatment with probenecid improves the efficiency of phagocytic cup formation or enhances POS uptake in these cells (Hass et al., 2024; Hass et al., 2023). The expected effect, therefore, remains a theoretical extension of the known pharmacology of probenecid based on its ability to interact with RPE-expressed organic anion transporters. While the anatomical evidence supports the presence of probenecid-sensitive transporters in RPE (Schlosshauer & Steuer, 2005; Mannermaa et al., 2009) and the broader pharmacologic literature confirms probenecid’s ability to modulate transporter-mediated processes (García-Rodríguez et al., 2023; Silverman et al., 2008), there is a gap in direct experimental research linking these events to enhanced cAMP-driven cytoskeletal remodeling and increased PR phagocytosis. As such, while the expected effect is mechanistically attractive, it currently relies on an extrapolation from fundamental transporter biology and indirect studies of cyclic nucleotide regulation rather than on direct proof-of-concept experiments in RPE phagocytic assays.

Overall Evaluation:  
In evaluating probenecid as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD, there are several strengths and weaknesses that emerge from a comprehensive literature review. On the strengths side, probenecid is a well-characterized, clinically approved drug with an extensive safety history. Its ability to inhibit organic anion transporters, including OATs, OATPs, and even certain multidrug resistance-associated proteins like MRP4 and MRP5, is well documented (García-Rodríguez et al., 2023; Schlosshauer & Steuer, 2005; Mannermaa et al., 2009). The documented presence of probenecid-sensitive transporters in the RPE supports the notion that the drug could modulate RPE intracellular homeostasis. Furthermore, preclinical studies in ocular models—including light-damage and oxygen-induced retinopathy models—suggest that probenecid can exert neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects in the retina, with improvements in retinal architecture and electrophysiological parameters (Nor et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2021). Such findings bolster the case that probenecid, via modulation of transporter activity and potentially via additional actions on pannexin channels, can yield beneficial biological responses in retinal tissues, which may be relevant for the treatment of degenerative conditions like dry AMD.

However, the candidate’s weaknesses are significant from the perspective of the specific therapeutic hypothesis under consideration. The central hypothesis posits that probenecid will increase intracellular cAMP and thereby activate PKA to promote cytoskeletal rearrangements that enhance the phagocytic uptake of POS by RPE cells. The literature does provide evidence for probenecid’s inhibition of organic anion transporters in ocular tissues (Hass et al., 2023; Schlosshauer & Steuer, 2005), but there is no direct experimental data linking probenecid treatment to increased intracellular cAMP levels in RPE cells or to enhanced PKA activity. In fact, one study suggests that organic anion transport inhibitors may suppress a cAMP-dependent response (e.g., pigment granule aggregation in RPE) (Hass et al., 2024), which could be interpreted as counter to the proposed mechanism. Additionally, while other compounds such as ticagrelor have been shown to influence lysosomal pH and phagocytic processing via cAMP/PKA pathways (Lu et al., 2018), these effects have not been recapitulated with probenecid in any published work. This leaves a critical gap: the central premise—that inhibiting organic anion transport in RPE will necessarily elevate cAMP and induce the desired cytoskeletal and phagocytic changes—remains unproven in the context of probenecid.

Further complicating the picture is probenecid’s known activity as a pannexin-1 channel inhibitor (Silverman et al., 2008; Yang, 2017). While inhibition of Panx1 can mitigate pathological ATP release and reduce inflammatory signaling—effects that could be beneficial in a neurodegenerative retinal setting—this mechanism is distinct from the modulation of cAMP/PKA signaling that underpins the current therapeutic hypothesis. The potential for off-target effects also raises concerns: if probenecid primarily acts by blocking pannexin channels or by interfering with other transporters not directly related to cAMP homeostasis, its administration could lead to side effects or fail to deliver the anticipated enhancement in phagocytosis. Moreover, the literature does not define the precise dosing regimens or pharmacokinetic profiles in RPE cells that would be required to achieve the proposed biochemical changes. For example, experiments showing inhibition of transporter-mediated uptake in RPE have employed concentrations on the order of 100 μM to 1 mM (Hass et al., 2023; Olivier et al., 2016), raising questions about the feasibility of translating such concentrations in vivo without off-target toxicity or interference with other critical cellular processes.

In terms of translational potential, while probenecid’s long clinical history is a favorable aspect, its application in a chronic disease such as dry AMD requires clear demonstration of sustained modulation of RPE function over time. The evidence from acute light-damage or oxygen-induced retinopathy models (Nor et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2021) suggests that probenecid may improve retinal integrity and reduce neuroinflammation; however, these models do not replicate the slow degenerative progression and complex pathophysiology of dry AMD, where chronic dysfunction in POS phagocytosis and RPE metabolism play central roles. Without explicit studies showing that probenecid can enhance phagocytic cup formation and POS uptake in RPE cells—and thereby ameliorate dry AMD-like pathology in animal models—the candidate remains only a theoretical possibility for this indication.

Thus, while the concept of repurposing probenecid for dry AMD is innovative and leverages a drug with robust safety credentials and a well-understood mechanism as an OAT inhibitor, the current state of the literature presents significant gaps. There is compelling evidence for the presence of probenecid-sensitive organic anion transporters in RPE and for the drug’s efficacy in modulating ocular transporter activity (Schlosshauer & Steuer, 2005; Hass et al., 2023), yet critical downstream assays linking this modulation to increased intracellular cAMP, PKA activation, actin cytoskeletal remodeling, and enhanced POS phagocytosis are lacking. Given that some studies even indicate potential suppression of cAMP-dependent processes by organic anion transport inhibitors (Hass et al., 2024), it is imperative to conduct focused biochemical and cellular experiments in ARPE-19 or primary RPE models that assay cAMP levels, PKA activity, and cytoskeletal changes under probenecid treatment. Additionally, in vivo studies in animal models that more closely mimic the chronic pathology of dry AMD would be necessary to determine whether these in vitro effects translate into meaningful therapeutic benefits.

Overall, the repurposing strategy for probenecid aimed at enhancing phagocytic signaling in RPE cells by modulating organic anion transporter activity is mechanistically plausible yet remains unproven. Its established inhibitory action on OATs and related transporters provides a strong foundation, and its additional anti-inflammatory effects via pannexin blockade further underscore its potential in ocular applications. However, the absence of direct evidence linking probenecid to increased cAMP and PKA activation in RPE, coupled with conflicting indications from studies on cAMP-mediated responses, represents a major weakness in the current proposal. Before proceeding further along the translational pathway, it is critical to perform targeted preclinical studies that (i) quantify changes in intracellular cAMP and PKA activity in RPE following probenecid administration, (ii) assess the resultant impact on actin cytoskeleton dynamics and phagocytic cup formation, and (iii) evaluate whether these cellular alterations lead to improved POS uptake and reduced features of dry AMD in appropriate animal models (Mannermaa et al., 2009; Hass et al., 2023; Hass et al., 2024).

In summary, while probenecid offers several attractive features—including clinical familiarity, a well-characterized pharmacological profile, and documented inhibitory effects on ocular transporters—the current body of evidence does not sufficiently demonstrate that its mechanism of action will result in the intracellular signaling changes necessary to enhance RPE phagocytosis in the context of dry AMD. The hypothesis that increasing intracellular cAMP via OAT inhibition will drive PKA-mediated cytoskeletal remodeling and improved POS clearance in RPE remains speculative until validated by further biomechanical, cellular, and in vivo studies. The overall evaluation of probenecid as a candidate for dry AMD is therefore cautiously optimistic but underscores the need for additional targeted research to bridge the gap between transporter inhibition and the desired modulation of phagocytic signaling (García-Rodríguez et al., 2023; Silverman et al., 2008; Olivier et al., 2016).
