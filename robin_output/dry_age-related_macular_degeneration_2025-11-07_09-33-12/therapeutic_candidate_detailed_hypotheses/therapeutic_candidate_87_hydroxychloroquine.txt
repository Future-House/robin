Proposal for Hydroxychloroquine (Chloroquine analog; Sigma-Aldrich Cat. No. H0915)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) is a fully synthetic small molecule in the 4-aminoquinoline (4AQ) class, developed in the mid-20th century as a hydroxylated analogue of chloroquine (CQ) to improve tolerability; HCQ differs from CQ by a terminal hydroxyl group on the side chain (HCQ: C18H26ClN3O, 336 g/mol; CQ: C18H26ClN3, 320 g/mol) (Browning, 2014a, 2014b). Industrially, HCQ can be prepared by condensing N1-N-ethyl-N1-β-hydroxyethyl-1,4-pentanediamine with 4,7-dichloroquinoline, paralleling the CQ route from the same quinoline core with an altered aminoalkyl side chain (Rainsford et al., 2015). As a class, HCQ/CQ are alkylated 4-aminoquinolines: weak, amphiphilic bases bearing a flat quinoline nucleus and a basic side chain that traverse membranes in unprotonated form and undergo protonation-dependent sequestration in acidic intracellular compartments (lysosomes, endosomes, Golgi) — a defining pharmacologic property (Schrezenmeier & Dörner, 2020). Chloroquine’s two basic centers (pKa ≈ 10.2 and 8.1) illustrate the protonation behavior that drives lysosomal trapping; at lysosomal pH (≈ 4–5) these agents become diprotonated and accumulate, with lysosome:plasma distribution ratios measured on the order of ≈ 160 000 and intralysosomal concentrations up to ≈ 2 × 10⁻² M reported for CQ, elevating lysosomal pH toward ≈ 6 (Browning, 2014b, 2014a). Pharmacokinetically, HCQ exhibits high oral bioavailability (≈ 0.7–0.8), an extremely large apparent volume of distribution (> 40 000 L), and a long terminal elimination half-life (≈ 40–60 days), with blood concentrations exceeding plasma due to extensive intracellular partitioning and tissue sequestration (Browning, 2014b; Schrezenmeier & Dörner, 2020). Melanin-containing ocular tissues are prominent depots; with chronic ingestion, uveal and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) concentrations can reach up to ≈ 10 000× plasma, reflecting lysosomal trapping and melanin binding (Browning, 2014a). Enantiomer-specific disposition has been observed for HCQ, with stereoselective accumulation noted in blood compartments, underscoring complex distribution kinetics (Schrezenmeier & Dörner, 2020). Clinically, HCQ is commonly administered as the sulfate salt, and CQ as the phosphate salt (Neto et al., 2020).

Therapeutic History  
HCQ and CQ were developed as antimalarials and subsequently repurposed as disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) for systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and rheumatoid arthritis (RA), with additional uses across dermatology and other immune-mediated conditions; HCQ is generally preferred over CQ for chronic therapy due to a better safety profile while maintaining comparable immunomodulatory efficacy (Rainsford et al., 2015; Neto et al., 2020). Broad clinical applications documented for HCQ/CQ include SLE, RA, primary Sjögren’s syndrome, discoid/cutaneous lupus erythematosus, polymorphous light eruption, solar urticaria, porphyria cutanea tarda, and other inflammatory dermatoses, reflecting lysosomotropic and immune-modulating actions (Rainsford et al., 2015; Rapparini et al., 2025). Mechanistically motivated repurposing has extended to autophagy inhibition in oncology trials, typically at daily doses far exceeding rheumatologic regimens, although HCQ is not approved as an anticancer adjuvant (Yusuf et al., 2023). Over recent decades HCQ prescriptions have increased substantially due to its relative affordability and systemic safety advantages, expanding the exposed population and the need for ocular safety surveillance (Yusuf et al., 2023).

With respect to retinal disease, the literature emphasizes retinotoxic risk and mechanistic links to RPE lysosomal dysfunction rather than therapeutic benefit in degenerative macular disease; authoritative pharmacology and ophthalmology sources do not provide evidence that HCQ/CQ have been used as treatments for dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD), and instead catalogue retinal toxicity mechanisms and monitoring practices associated with long-term systemic use (Browning, 2014b; Browning, 2014a). Multiple summaries note that available data do not support therapeutic use of 4AQs for dry AMD or similar retinal degenerations, while documenting ocular accumulation, lysosomal alkalinization, and RPE stress as central safety concerns (Browning, 2014a). Retinopathy, although uncommon at recommended doses, is a recognized dose- and duration-related adverse effect that can be irreversible and may progress after drug cessation, motivating dose caps and structured ophthalmic screening (Rainsford et al., 2015; Ding et al., 2016).

Mechanism of Action  
Lysosomotropism and lysosomal alkalinization  
HCQ/CQ are canonical lysosomotropic weak bases that accumulate massively within acidic intracellular organelles and elevate their pH, directly impairing acidity-dependent lysosomal hydrolases, receptor recycling, and degradative pathways including autophagy (Browning, 2014a; Browning, 2014b). In vitro and cell-system studies report that chloroquine exposure increases lysosomal pH from ≈ 4–5 toward ≈ 6, a change sufficient to inhibit cathepsin activities and other acid hydrolases; pharmacologic blockade of the vacuolar H⁺-ATPase with bafilomycin A₁ abrogates CQ’s lysosomotropism and toxicity, pinpointing disruption of lysosomal acidification as the proximal driver (Browning, 2014a; Browning, 2014b). In RPE cells specifically, the lysosomotropic alkalinization by 4AQs inhibits lysosomal enzyme activity and autophagic flux, leading to accumulation of undegraded material; experimental inhibition of vacuolar acidification with bafilomycin phenocopies these degradative defects, indicating that pH elevation per se is sufficient to impair RPE lysosomal function (Guha et al., 2014).

Immune modulation and nucleic acid sensing  
Elevation of endolysosomal pH by 4AQs inhibits MHC class II antigen processing and presentation and blocks activation of endosomal Toll-like receptors (TLR-3/7/9), thereby reducing MyD88-dependent interferon production and dampening pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion from mononuclear cells and dendritic cells (IL-1, IL-6, TNF, IFN-α/γ, CCL4) (Browning, 2014b; Schrezenmeier & Dörner, 2020). The class also interferes with cGAS–STING pathway activation through inhibition of cGAMP synthase and reduces T-cell co-stimulatory signaling (e.g., CD154), a mechanistic constellation that explains DMARD efficacy without significant increases in opportunistic infections (Schrezenmeier & Dörner, 2020; Browning, 2014b). Additional molecular interactions reported for 4AQs include DNA binding/intercalation, kinase pathway modulation (e.g., ERK/MAPK), and diverse enzyme interactions (e.g., cholinesterase), reflecting broad pleiotropy at pharmacologic and suprapharmacologic exposures (Browning, 2014a).

RPE-relevant degradative biology: cathepsins and POS processing  
The RPE depends critically on acidic lysosomes and cathepsin activity to clear the daily phagocytic load of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) and maintain autophagic flux; failure of this system yields lipofuscin accumulation and contributes to AMD pathogenesis (Sharif, 2015). Multiple lysosomal proteases are expressed by RPE, including cysteine cathepsins B, L, and S as well as aspartyl cathepsin D; cathepsins are synthesized as pre-proenzymes, processed via the secretory pathway, and activated in the acidic lumen of late endosomes/lysosomes (Sharif, 2015). In human RPE/choroid and RPE cell lines, both inactive and active forms of cathepsin B, L, and S have been detected; functional studies show that broad cysteine protease inhibition or cathepsin S knockdown impairs POS degradation and increases lipofuscin-like inclusions, underscoring their role in RPE proteostasis (Sharif, 2015). Cathepsin D, a major lysosomal aspartyl protease in RPE, is essential for POS processing; its maturation and catalytic efficiency are strongly pH-dependent and occur within the acidic lysosomal lumen (Cardoso et al., 2023; Sharif, 2015).

CQ/HCQ disrupt lysosomal proteolysis and lysosome dynamics in RPE  
In RPE models, CQ induces a rapid lysosomal pH increase followed by partial pH recovery, yet produces persistent lysosomal enlargement, accumulation of undegraded endocytic, phagocytic, and autophagic cargos, and a progressive reduction in accessibility of enlarged lysosomes to the endocytic pathway — a phenotype consistent with impaired autolysosome reformation and failure to regenerate a pool of degradatively competent lysosomes (Cardoso et al., 2023). A salient molecular signature of CQ exposure is accumulation of pro-cathepsin D and the lysosomal membrane protein LAMP1 without corresponding mRNA increases, indicating impaired processing/turnover rather than transcriptional upregulation; protease inhibitor treatment phenocopies this pro-cathepsin D accumulation, linking CQ’s effect to reduced proteolytic capacity (Cardoso et al., 2023). Although lysosomotropic agents can activate TFEB-dependent lysosomal gene transcription at high concentrations, the inhibitory effects on cathepsin maturation and lysosome reformation dominate in RPE models at low doses and precede any compensatory changes in gene expression (Cardoso et al., 2023).

HCQ further impairs RPE lysosomes by perturbing cholesterol homeostasis and promoting lysosomal membrane permeability (LMP), with cytosolic release of cathepsin B  
Beyond general autophagy/lysosome inhibition, HCQ suppresses mTORC1-dependent cholesterol biosynthesis, a change that increases lysosomal membrane permeability and allows lysosomal cathepsin B to translocate into the cytosol, where it can activate caspase-dependent apoptosis and drive caspase-independent phosphatidylserine externalization; these effects are mitigated by neutral pH-selective cathepsin B inhibition and exacerbated by pharmacologic cholesterol lowering (simvastatin) or depletion (methyl-β-cyclodextrin) (Wicks et al., 2022). These data in ARPE-19 RPE cells indicate that HCQ-induced lysosomal dysfunction and LMP sensitize RPE to additional stressors (e.g., UV), compounding vulnerability in aging retinal tissue (Wicks et al., 2022).

RPE/retinal stress signatures and storage phenotypes with 4AQ exposure  
Cell and animal studies document that 4AQ exposure produces RPE and retinal stress consistent with impaired lysosomal degradation: multilamellar myeloid bodies in RPE, photoreceptors, and ganglion cells; inhibition of oxidative enzymes in photoreceptors; decreased retinal glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase and glutathione with increased lipid peroxidation; disruption of the blood–retina barrier; and inhibition of protein synthesis in RPE culture (Browning, 2014a). In RPE cell culture, high concentrations of CQ perturb fusion of autophagic vesicles, increase LC3-II and p62, and can drive cell death, while intravitreal bafilomycin reproduces opsin-loaded phagolysosome accumulation, affirming that alkalinization is sufficient to cause degradative backlog (Guha et al., 2014). Chronic CQ treatment in mice increases mRNA expression of v-H⁺-ATPase and TFEB, consistent with compensatory attempts to reacidify lysosomes and enhance biogenesis, but does not normalize degradative function during exposure (Guha et al., 2014).

Absence of evidence for “low-dose stabilization” of lysosomal pH in RPE  
Across mechanistic summaries and primary RPE studies, there is no evidence that low-dose HCQ stabilizes lysosomal pH at an “optimal” value, preserves cathepsin activity, or enhances POS degradation; rather, the consistent effect is lysosomal alkalinization with inhibition of lysosomal enzymes (including cathepsin B) and blockade of autophagic/lysosomal degradation (Browning, 2014a). In fact, low-dose CQ already yields accumulation of pro-cathepsin D and cargo buildup in RPE, indicating that even modest lysosomotropic perturbation degrades proteolytic competence, counter to the hypothesis (Cardoso et al., 2023).

Pharmacokinetic and ocular deposition constraints  
The extreme tissue distribution and ocular melanin binding of 4AQs undermine precise intraocular dose control: uveal/RPE drug levels can markedly exceed plasma during chronic dosing, and the terminal half-life is long, with months-long detectability after discontinuation due to depot release (Browning, 2014b; Browning, 2014a). These properties make “titration” to a narrow, hypothetical lysosomal pH window impractical and raise the risk of sustained lysosomal alkalinization even with ostensibly low systemic doses (Browning, 2014b; Browning, 2014a).

Expected Effect  
The working hypothesis proposes that submicromolar HCQ will “fine-tune” lysosomal pH in RPE to an optimal range for cathepsin activity, thereby preserving cathepsin function and enhancing POS degradation without blocking autophagy. The aggregate evidence contradicts this at mechanistic, cellular, and pharmacokinetic levels.

- Cathepsins in RPE require an acidic lumen for zymogen processing and catalytic function, and multiple cathepsins (B, L, S, D) are expressed and active in RPE lysosomes where they regulate POS degradation and autophagy; broad cysteine protease inhibition or specific cathepsin S knockdown impairs POS clearance and increases lipofuscin-like inclusions, demonstrating the acid-dependent protease requirement (Sharif, 2015). In this context, 4AQs act as lysosomal alkalinizers that inhibit acid hydrolases; CQ/HCQ inhibit cathepsin B and disrupt cathepsin D processing, with CQ causing pro-cathepsin D accumulation and reduced maturation into the active enzyme, a phenotype directly linked to impaired lysosomal proteolysis and cargo clearance (Browning, 2014a; Cardoso et al., 2023). Therefore, the expected effect of submicromolar HCQ is not preservation but reduction of cathepsin function in RPE lysosomes, with consequent impairment of POS proteolysis (Browning, 2014a; Cardoso et al., 2023).

- In RPE cells, CQ/HCQ exposure impairs autophagic flux and lysosome reformation, producing enlarged, cargo-laden lysosomes that are progressively less accessible to endocytic inputs; these dynamics deplete the pool of degradatively competent lysosomes needed for daily POS processing, an effect observed even at “low-dose” CQ and preceding transcriptional responses (Cardoso et al., 2023). Thus, in assays measuring POS degradation and lysosomal flux, HCQ is predicted to slow, not enhance, turnover (Cardoso et al., 2023).

- HCQ further increases lysosomal membrane permeability and releases cathepsin B into the cytosol by impairing mTORC1-dependent cholesterol biosynthesis, activating pro-death signaling and phosphatidylserine externalization; pharmacologic cholesterol lowering/depletion magnify these effects, suggesting that HCQ’s action sensitizes RPE to metabolic and environmental stress rather than stabilizing proteolysis (Wicks et al., 2022).

- Beyond lysosomal pH, 4AQs disrupt other RPE functions essential for photoreceptor support: CQ inhibits rod outer segment phagocytosis in human/pig RPE cell lines, and HCQ/CQ inhibit OATP1A2 in human RPE, potentially perturbing all-trans-retinol uptake and the visual cycle — liabilities expected to worsen POS handling and photoreceptor maintenance in AMD models (Yusuf et al., 2023).

- The claim that “over-acidification” in RPE lysosomes needs moderation lacks evidentiary support in this literature corpus; the dominant pathologic signal in RPE aging and AMD models is lysosomal under-acidification/alkalinization and degradative impairment, which 4AQs exacerbate rather than correct (Guha et al., 2014).

- Pharmacokinetically, due to massive ocular accumulation and prolonged tissue persistence, “low systemic dose” does not guarantee low intra-RPE exposure; achieving and maintaining a narrow “optimal pH” window is improbable and unsafe for chronic indications with slowly reversible tissue exposure (Browning, 2014b; Browning, 2014a).

In sum, in assays of RPE lysosomal function and POS degradation, the most probable outcomes of HCQ at submicromolar concentrations are lysosomal pH elevation, inhibited cathepsin activity/maturation (including cathepsin B inhibition and pro-cathepsin D accumulation), impaired autophagic flux and lysosome reformation, increased LMP with cytosolic cathepsin B release, inhibition of POS phagocytosis, and potential disruption of retinoid transport — all expected to reduce, not enhance, POS degradation (Browning, 2014a; Cardoso et al., 2023; Wicks et al., 2022; Yusuf et al., 2023).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths for development feasibility  
- Extensive clinical pharmacology and systemic experience: HCQ has well-characterized oral pharmacokinetics, metabolism, and drug–drug interactions, with validated immunomodulatory mechanisms (endolysosomal pH elevation, TLR/cGAS–STING inhibition, cytokine suppression) across rheumatology and dermatology; supply chains and clinical dosing paradigms are established, enabling rapid translational work if warranted (Browning, 2014b; Schrezenmeier & Dörner, 2020; Rainsford et al., 2015). The compound’s canonical lysosomotropic action makes it a useful positive control for modeling lysosomal alkalinization and degradative impairment in RPE assays benchmarking alternative, mechanistically restorative strategies (Browning, 2014b).

- Lower ocular toxicity relative to CQ at equipotency: HCQ is generally less retinotoxic than CQ, a difference attributed in part to greater polarity and lower lipophilicity; however, the class risk remains dose- and duration-dependent and clinically significant (Browning, 2014a; Rainsford et al., 2015).

Critical weaknesses for dry AMD  
- Mechanistic conflict with therapeutic goal: RPE catabolism of POS requires acidic lysosomes and intact cathepsin maturation/activity; 4AQs elevate lysosomal pH, inhibit cathepsin activity (including cathepsin B) and maturation (cathepsin D), impair autophagic flux and lysosome reformation, and induce cargo accumulation — a set of changes that predict impaired POS processing and enhanced lipofuscin burden rather than restoration of lysosomal proteolysis (Browning, 2014a; Cardoso et al., 2023). No evidence in RPE/AMD models supports the hypothesis that “low-dose” HCQ stabilizes lysosomal pH to optimize cathepsin function; the reported outcomes consistently indicate degradative inhibition (Browning, 2014a).

- Ocular tropism and prolonged tissue persistence: The combination of melanin binding and lysosomal trapping produces extreme ocular accumulation and very slow washout, making precise intraocular dose control infeasible and raising the risk of prolonged lysosomal alkalinization even after dose reduction or cessation (Browning, 2014a; Browning, 2014b).

- Added liabilities in RPE and retina: HCQ promotes lysosomal membrane permeability and cytosolic cathepsin B release in RPE, sensitizing cells to UV and cholesterol-lowering stresses; CQ/HCQ also inhibit POS phagocytosis and OATP1A2 in RPE, potentially disrupting retinoid uptake and the visual cycle, and they induce retinal stress signatures (oxidative enzyme inhibition, antioxidant depletion, barrier disruption) — all detrimental in the aging macula (Wicks et al., 2022; Yusuf et al., 2023; Browning, 2014a).

- Absence of supportive precedent in AMD: Comprehensive pharmacologic and ophthalmic reviews provide no evidence that HCQ/CQ have been successfully used to treat dry AMD or analogous retinal degenerations; rather, they underscore retinotoxic risk and mechanisms consistent with RPE lysosomal dysfunction (Browning, 2014b; Browning, 2014a).

Strategic implications and recommendation  
The central hypothesis — that low-dose HCQ can fine-tune RPE lysosomal pH to preserve cathepsin activity and enhance POS degradation — is at odds with the mechanistic canon and RPE-focused data for 4AQs, which consistently demonstrate lysosomal alkalinization, cathepsin inhibition or maturation block, impaired lysosome reformation and autophagic flux, and accumulation of undegraded cargo (Browning, 2014a; Cardoso et al., 2023). No cited evidence shows that HCQ stabilizes lysosomal pH or improves POS degradation in RPE or AMD models; on the contrary, RPE studies with CQ/HCQ and mechanistic summaries emphasize inhibited lysosomal enzymes and autophagy as core actions (Browning, 2014a; Guha et al., 2014). Given extreme ocular accumulation and prolonged tissue retention, exploratory dosing aimed at a narrow “optimal” intralysosomal pH is neither controllable nor safe in a chronic neurodegenerative indication; adverse lysosomal effects could persist despite dose adjustments due to depot release (Browning, 2014b; Browning, 2014a). If the program goal is to restore RPE lysosomal proteolysis and POS turnover in dry AMD, agents that acidify lysosomes, facilitate cathepsin maturation/activity, or pharmacologically promote lysosome reformation (as suggested by pathways implicated in CQ-induced defects) align mechanistically, whereas HCQ/CQ, as archetypal alkalinizers and autophagy inhibitors, do not (Cardoso et al., 2023).

Conclusion: HCQ (Sigma-Aldrich H0915) is not a suitable therapeutic candidate for dry AMD under the proposed mechanism. The weight of biochemical, cellular, and pharmacokinetic evidence indicates HCQ will alkalinize RPE lysosomes, inhibit cathepsin function and maturation, impair autophagic flux and POS processing, and increase RPE vulnerability to stress, while ocular accumulation and long half-life amplify risk; no supportive evidence exists for benefit in dry AMD (Browning, 2014a; Cardoso et al., 2023; Wicks et al., 2022; Browning, 2014b). I recommend against advancing HCQ for AMD and suggest redirecting efforts toward mechanisms that restore lysosomal acidity, cathepsin activity, and lysosome reformation in RPE (Cardoso et al., 2023).
