Assay Overview  
The proposed assay models two central and intertwined pathogenic mechanisms in dry AMD—oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction—in human iPSC-derived retinal pigment epithelium (iPSC-RPE) monolayers. Briefly, confluent iPSC-RPE cultures are challenged with either:  
 • A pulse of H₂O₂ (100–500 µM, 1–2 h) to mimic acute oxidative insult, or  
 • Pre-loaded A2E (10–20 µM) followed by controlled blue-light exposure (430–490 nm, 6–12 J/cm²) to recapitulate chronic photooxidative stress.  
Immediately after insult (within 24 h), cells are assayed for:  
1. Total cellular ROS via CellROX™ fluorescence.  
2. Mitochondrial superoxide via MitoSOX™ staining.  
3. Mitochondrial functional parameters—basal respiration, ATP-linked respiration, maximal respiration, spare respiratory capacity—using a Seahorse XF Analyzer.  
Test compounds are added either prophylactically or post-insult, and their ability to reduce ROS signals and restore respiratory parameters is quantified.  

Cells and materials:  
 • Human iPSC-RPE lines from healthy donors and, where available, AMD patients (Golestaneh et al., Stem Cells 2017).  
 • Standard RPE culture supplements (nicotinamide, taurine, triiodothyronine).  
 • Commercial fluorescent probes (CellROX™, MitoSOX™), XF cell mito stress kit.  

Biomedical Evidence  
1. Oxidative stress in RPE is a hallmark of dry AMD. RPE cells are exposed lifelong to high oxygen tension, photo-oxidative byproducts (lipofuscin/A2E), and phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments—cumulatively generating ROS (Jarrett & Boulton, Mol Neurobiol 2012; Beatty et al., Surv Ophthalmol 2000).  
2. Mitochondrial dysfunction is prominent in AMD RPE. Post-mortem AMD donor RPE show decreased complex I activity, mtDNA damage, and reduced mitochondrial mass (Ferrington et al., Invest Ophthalmol Vis Sci 2017; Golestaneh et al., Stem Cells 2017).  
3. Clinical studies (AREDS/AREDS2) demonstrated that augmentation of antioxidant defenses slows progression of geographic atrophy (AMD Research Group, Arch Ophthalmol 2001), underscoring the therapeutic relevance of targeting oxidative stress.  
4. Impaired spare respiratory capacity in RPE predicts vulnerability to secondary sub-lethal insults (Kaur et al., Redox Biol 2018). Restoration of spare capacity correlates with cell survival under chronic stress.  

Collectively, both preclinical and clinical data place RPE oxidative injury and mitochondrial bioenergetic failure at the core of dry AMD pathogenesis.  

Previous Use  
• Ferrington and colleagues (IOVS 2005, 2017) first quantified mitochondrial complex activities and respiration in RPE from human donors, establishing the translational relevance of Seahorse-based bioenergetic profiling.  
• Golestaneh et al. (Stem Cells 2017) used patient-derived iPSC-RPE to show that AMD RPE have elevated basal ROS and defective mitochondrial dynamics, and that coenzyme Q10 partially rescued these phenotypes.  
• Several groups have employed A2E plus blue-light to induce RPE cell death and screened for photoprotective agents in 96-well plate formats (Roche et al., Invest Ophthalmol Vis Sci 2014).  
• High-content imaging of ROS (CellROX) and mitochondrial potential (TMRM) has been used to validate antioxidant hits in RPE cells (Richter et al., Redox Biol 2020).  

Key discoveries include:  
– Identification of mitochondrial protective compounds (e.g., SS-31 peptide) that normalize spare respiratory capacity and reduce lipofuscin-induced ROS.  
– Demonstration that enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis (via PGC-1α activation) confers resistance to photooxidative stress.  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
• Disease relevance: Directly interrogates two mechanistic drivers of RPE degeneration in dry AMD.  
• Multiplexed endpoints: Simultaneous measurement of total ROS, mitochondrial ROS, and multiple respiration parameters allows discrimination between general antioxidant effects and true mitochondrial rescue.  
• Throughput: Adaptable to 96-well format; endpoints can be read within 24 h, supporting moderate-throughput screening.  
• Human cells: Use of iPSC-RPE—especially from AMD donors—captures patient-specific susceptibilities and genetic background.  

Weaknesses  
• Acute vs. chronic stress: H₂O₂ or A2E/blue-light pulses model acute insults and may not fully recapitulate decades-long, low-grade oxidative damage.  
• iPSC-RPE maturity: In vitro RPE often lack full age-related phenotypes (e.g., Bruch’s membrane alterations, lipoprotein accumulation) unless extensively matured.  
• Light toxicity parameters: Blue-light doses and A2E concentrations vary across labs; standardization is needed to ensure physiological relevance.  
• Seahorse limitations: While informative, XF assays require specialized equipment, have limited well throughput (~24 wells/run), and can be sensitive to cell seeding density.  
• Lack of multicellular context: AMD involves choroidal endothelium, immune cells, and photoreceptors; RPE-only assays do not capture complement activation or drusen formation.  

Conclusion  
This oxidative stress/mitochondrial dysfunction assay stands on solid mechanistic and translational grounds for evaluating potential therapeutics in dry AMD. It offers a balanced combination of disease relevance, multiplexed readouts, and reasonably high throughput. However, results should be complemented with longer-term, multicellular models (e.g., RPE-choroid organoids, co-cultures) to fully predict therapeutic efficacy in the complex in vivo environment of the aging macula.
