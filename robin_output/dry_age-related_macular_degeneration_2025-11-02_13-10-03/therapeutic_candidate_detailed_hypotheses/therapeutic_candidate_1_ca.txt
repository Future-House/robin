Proposal for CA-074Me (cathepsin B inhibitor; Sigma-Aldrich catalog C3692)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
CA-074Me is the methyl-ester “pro-inhibitor” derived from the epoxysuccinyl peptide CA-074, a highly selective and irreversible inhibitor of the lysosomal cysteine protease cathepsin B (CatB) developed from the E-64 scaffold that broadly targets papain-family cysteine proteases but not caspases or other protease classes (Yoon et al., 2022, pp. 1–3, 13–14; Hook et al., 2015, pp. 17–18). CA-074’s molecular pharmacology has been resolved structurally and biochemically: it covalently blocks CatB with strong pH dependence—subnanomolar-to-nanomolar potency at acidic lysosomal pH is driven by ionic interactions between its free C-terminal carboxylate and CatB occluding-loop histidines (His110/His111), whereas potency drops more than 100-fold at neutral pH, consistent with the enzyme’s conformational and electrostatic changes outside the lysosome (Yoon et al., 2022, pp. 1–3, 6–8). Methylation of the C-terminal carboxylate yields CA-074Me, which abrogates the pH-dependent binding mechanism and substantially decreases biochemical potency against CatB across pH 4.6–7.2 (IC₅₀ ∼ 7–14 μM; Kᵢ ∼ 29–56 μM), in sharp contrast to CA-074’s low-nanomolar-to-submicromolar potency at these pHs (Yoon et al., 2022, pp. 3–6, 15–21). In biochemical selectivity panels, CA-074 retains high specificity for CatB over other cysteine cathepsins, whereas CA-074Me shows weak inhibition of CatB and measurable micromolar inhibition of Cathepsin S while otherwise exhibiting limited inhibition at the concentrations tested; in cellular contexts, slow prodrug conversion can broaden CA-074Me’s apparent target spectrum to multiple cathepsins before de-esterification to CA-074 (Yoon et al., 2022, pp. 1–3, 5–6, 21–25; Ishrat & Nasoohi, 2018, pp. 31–34).

The methyl ester was introduced to improve cell permeability and enable intracellular CatB inhibition; CA-074 is commonly considered cell-impermeant, while CA-074Me is cell-permeant and is converted intracellularly by esterases to CA-074, which then engages CatB within acidic compartments (lysosomes/late endosomes) (Oberstein et al., 2021, pp. 7–10; Yoon et al., 2022, pp. 1–3, 13–14). Across in vivo CNS disease models, CA-074Me or CA-074 have been widely used as pharmacological tools targeting CatB, with reported neuroprotection and reductions in CatB activity and downstream apoptosis/autophagy/inflammatory markers in traumatic brain injury and ischemia models; however, these studies did not interrogate retinal disease indications (Hook et al., 2015, pp. 13–14, 15–17; Zuo et al., 2016, pp. 7–9; Yoon et al., 2022, pp. 1–3). One ophthalmic report from 2008 suggests CA-074Me can suppress retinal neovascularization, consistent with CatB’s known role in matrix remodeling and angiogenesis, but there is no provided preclinical evidence for CA-074Me in dry AMD per se (Rudzińska et al., 2019, pp. 19–21).

The drug class—irreversible epoxysuccinyl inhibitors of papain-like cathepsins—has a long history as target-validation tools and as in vivo pharmacologic probes in neurodegeneration and inflammation, with CA-074 being CatB-selective and CA-074Me serving as the intracellularly active derivative for cell and animal studies (Hook et al., 2015, pp. 13–14, 17–18; Yoon et al., 2022, pp. 1–3). In the retina, CatB is a constituent lysosomal protease of RPE that participates in photoreceptor outer segment (POS) clearance and autophagy; RPE/ocular biology thus provides both a rationale (CatB’s participation in lysosomal damage–inflammasome coupling) and a caution (CatB’s role in proteostasis) for therapeutic strategies targeting CatB (Sharif, 2015, pp. 60–64, 189–192).

Therapeutic History  
CA-074 and CA-074Me have been extensively applied in CNS injury and neurodegeneration models. In a non-human primate ischemia model, intravenous CA-074 inhibited hippocampal CA1 CatB activity by ∼ 75 % and preserved ∼ 67 % of neurons compared with controls, whereas the broader cysteine protease inhibitor E64c achieved ∼ 84 % neuronal survival, consistent with additional benefit from inhibiting CatL/calpains beyond CatB (Hook et al., 2015, pp. 15–17). In rodent traumatic brain injury models, intracerebroventricular CA-074Me reduced CatB activity in cortex and improved behavioral readouts in certain injured cohorts; these studies illustrate in vivo target engagement but also highlight variable functional effects across injury paradigms (Hernandez, 2023, pp. 97–101, 111–114, 136–139, 139–144). Across CNS models surveyed in aggregate, CA-074Me treatments by icv/ip/it routes are associated with reductions in CatB activity, decreases in IL-1β/IL-18 and autophagy markers, and improvements in neurobehavioral outcomes, though these data are not quantitative pharmacokinetics and lack comprehensive selectivity profiling in vivo (Hook et al., 2015, pp. 13–14).

In cell systems, CA-074Me has been used at 25–50 μM to inhibit measurable CatB activity and perturb Aβ peptide pools in astrocytes and H4 APP₇₅₁-overexpressing cells; genetic CTSB deletion abolished CA-074Me’s effects on Aβ variants, supporting on-target action in that model (Oberstein et al., 2021, pp. 4–8). CA-074Me exposure also altered lysosomal morphology even in CTSB⁻/⁻ cells, indicating CatB-independent effects on lysosomes at these concentrations in some systems (Oberstein et al., 2021, pp. 8–10). In okadaic acid–treated primary cortical neurons, CA-074Me (50–100 μM) suppressed CatB activity, reduced APP accumulation and tau phosphorylation, and decreased calpain-specific α-spectrin cleavage; the latter underscores potential off-targets or network-level effects (Cho et al., 2013, pp. 2–6).

With respect to ocular applications, the provided literature contains one cited study title indicating CA-074Me suppressed retinal neovascularization, which is a neovascular context rather than geographic atrophy; no direct in vivo studies of CA-074Me in dry AMD models, or in drusen/complement-driven retinal degeneration, were provided in the excerpts (Rudzińska et al., 2019, pp. 19–21). By contrast, in vitro RPE inflammasome pathway studies that used cathepsin inhibitors to suppress NLRP3 activation in AMD-relevant contexts employed CA-074 (active-site CatB inhibitor) and a CatB/L inhibitor (Z-FF-FMK), not CA-074Me, and reported reductions in pyroptosis and IL-1β/IL-18 release (Brandstetter, 2016, pp. 47–51, 80–85, 92–95, 99–104; Tseng et al., 2013, pp. 9–10).

Mechanism of Action  
Biochemical mechanism versus CatB. CA-074 is an epoxysuccinyl inhibitor that covalently and irreversibly inhibits CatB, showing strong pH dependence: nanomolar IC₅₀ and Kᵢ at lysosomal pH 4.6, ∼10-fold weaker at pH 5.5, and > 100-fold weaker at neutral pH 7.2; this arises from ionic interactions of the free C-terminal carboxylate of CA-074 with His110/His111 within the S2′ subsite of CatB’s occluding loop, favoring the acidic conformation (Yoon et al., 2022, pp. 1–3, 6–8). In functional and docking experiments, CA-074 shows high specificity for CatB among cysteine cathepsins in both acidic and neutral conditions (Yoon et al., 2022, pp. 1–3). CA-074Me, by contrast, is an irreversible but weak CatB inhibitor; methylation of the C-terminal carboxylate eliminates the pH-dependent affinity enhancement and reduces biochemical potency by 10–>1000× (IC₅₀ 7–14 μM; Kᵢ 29–56 μM), and it shows measurable activity against CatS in the micromolar range in selectivity panels (Yoon et al., 2022, pp. 3–6, 21–25). In living cells, CA-074Me is widely used as a cell-permeant prodrug that can be converted to CA-074 by esterases within lysosomes; however, at least one report concluded that CA-074 (not CA-074Me) maintains selectivity for CatB within cells, suggesting that CA-074Me’s slow conversion affords time to inhibit multiple cathepsins and produce CatB-independent lysosomal effects (Hook et al., 2015, pp. 25–26; Ishrat & Nasoohi, 2018, pp. 31–34; Oberstein et al., 2021, pp. 8–10). Typical in vitro concentrations required for robust intracellular CatB inhibition by CA-074Me are in the 10–50 μM range, consistent with its weak biochemical potency and the need to achieve sufficient intracellular exposure (Yoon et al., 2022, pp. 6–8; Oberstein et al., 2021, pp. 6–7).

Cellular and disease-mechanistic context in RPE. RPE cells robustly express active CatB (as well as CatL and CatS), which is integral to lysosomal POS processing and autophagy; perturbation of cathepsin activity in RPE leads to accumulation of undegraded material and can precipitate RPE cell dysfunction/death, with implications for age-associated lipofuscin accumulation and AMD pathogenesis (Sharif, 2015, pp. 60–64, 189–192). Multiple AMD-relevant stimuli—including drusen components (e.g., amyloid-β), oxidized lipids (ox-LDL), lipofuscin constituents (e.g., A2E), and complement activation products (e.g., C1q, C3a, C5a, sublytic MAC)—disrupt phagolysosomal integrity and/or prime NF-κB signaling, converging on NLRP3 inflammasome activation in RPE (Campbell & Doyle, 2013, pp. 5–7; Korhonen, 2024, pp. 76–79; Yerramothu, 2018, pp. 67–72). Mechanistically, lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP) permits cytosolic leakage of lysosomal enzymes, and cytosolic cathepsin activity is a required upstream signal for NLRP3 assembly in RPE and in macrophages; in ARPE-19 and primary RPE, pharmacologic inhibition of cathepsins B and L blocks LMP-triggered NLRP3 activation, caspase-1 activation, IL-1β/IL-18 secretion, and pyroptotic cell death (Brandstetter, 2016, pp. 47–51; Tseng et al., 2013, pp. 9–10). In a lipofuscin phototoxicity model (HNE-modified POS + blue light), cathepsin B/L inhibitors suppressed NLRP3 activation, and, when RPE were primed by IL-1α or C5a, cathepsin B/L inhibition and caspase-1 inhibition both reduced pyroptosis and shifted death kinetics back toward apoptosis-like profiles; importantly, CA-074 (active-site CatB inhibitor) was one of the agents used in these RPE experiments (Brandstetter, 2016, pp. 80–85, 92–95). Complement and IL-1 pathways provide priming (signal 1): C5a and IL-1α upregulate pro-IL-1β via NF-κB, and conditioned media from pyroptotic RPE further amplifies susceptibility via an IL-1R–dependent loop; pharmacologic blockade of C5a/C5aR and IL-1R attenuates inflammasome activation and RPE death, and specific NLRP3 inhibitors are proposed therapeutic strategies (Brandstetter, 2016, pp. 80–85, 102–104). Drusen-induced IL-1β can be suppressed by blocking lysosomal acidification or CatB activity, further reinforcing the cathepsin requirement upstream of NLRP3 in RPE (Campbell & Doyle, 2013, pp. 5–7).

Thus, at the pathway level: lysosomal damage → cathepsin leakage (including CatB/L) → NLRP3 assembly → caspase-1 activation → IL-1β/IL-18 maturation → pyroptosis; and priming via complement/IL-1α/C5a is required for robust cytokine maturation (Brandstetter, 2016, pp. 47–51, 80–85, 102–104; Tseng et al., 2013, pp. 9–10). Cathepsin B/L inhibition attenuates this sequence in vitro in human RPE models (Brandstetter, 2016, pp. 92–95; Campbell & Doyle, 2013, pp. 5–7; Tseng et al., 2013, pp. 9–10).

Pharmacology considerations for cytosolic versus lysosomal CatB. CatB released to the cytosol experiences a higher pH, where CatB’s activity state and inhibitor sensitivity differ from the lysosome; CA-074’s potency decreases ∼ 90-fold between pH 4.6 and 7.2, and CA-074Me, which is weaker at all pHs, lacks the advantageous pH dependence entirely (Yoon et al., 2022, pp. 1–3, 6–8, 21–25). In this respect, inhibiting cytosolic CatB (post-LMP) with CA-074/CA-074Me will be less efficient than inhibiting lysosomal CatB, unless very high intracellular concentrations are achieved; Yoon et al. suggest using CA-074 (acid-preferring) together with a neutral-pH–preferring CatB inhibitor (e.g., Z-Arg-Lys-AOMK) to dissect and potentially treat lysosomal versus cytosolic CatB contributions (Yoon et al., 2022, pp. 6–8). In living cells, CA-074Me’s slow conversion to CA-074 may lead to a window where multiple cathepsins are inhibited, and CA-074Me can cause CatB-independent lysosomal alterations, complicating mechanistic interpretation and selectivity (Hook et al., 2015, pp. 25–26; Ishrat & Nasoohi, 2018, pp. 31–34; Oberstein et al., 2021, pp. 8–10).

Expected Effect  
In dry AMD-relevant in vitro assays where RPE are primed (e.g., IL-1α/C5a) and then challenged with drusen-like cargo or lipofuscin photooxidative stress, pharmacologic inhibition of CatB/L reduces NLRP3 activation, decreases caspase-1 activity, lowers IL-1β/IL-18 release, and mitigates pyroptotic lysis, often shifting death kinetics toward apoptosis-like profiles; this has been shown with CA-074 (CatB) and broad CatB/L inhibitors (Z-FF-FMK) (Brandstetter, 2016, pp. 47–51, 92–95; Campbell & Doyle, 2013, pp. 5–7; Tseng et al., 2013, pp. 9–10). Given CA-074Me’s intracellular activity as a CatB-directed agent at tens of micromolar concentrations, one would predict similar suppression of NLRP3 readouts in RPE if sufficient intracellular exposure is achieved; however, CA-074Me’s weak biochemical potency, altered pH profile, and broader cathepsin engagement at high concentrations introduce uncertainty in both the magnitude of effect and mechanism selectivity (Yoon et al., 2022, pp. 5–6, 21–25; Ishrat & Nasoohi, 2018, pp. 31–34; Oberstein et al., 2021, pp. 8–10). The predicted on-pathway effects would include:

- Reduction of IL-1β and IL-18 maturation/secretion, with a predominantly apical decrease in cytokine flux toward neuroretina; this could secondarily reduce microglial chemoattraction and associated neuroinflammation, consistent with apically polarized secretion from activated RPE (Brandstetter, 2016, pp. 47–51, 102–104).  
- Decreased caspase-1 activation and less gasdermin-driven membrane lysis (pyroptosis), translating to lower LDH release and increased RPE survival under phototoxic or drusen-like stress (Brandstetter, 2016, pp. 92–95; Tseng et al., 2013, pp. 9–10).  
- Context-dependent effects on VEGF: NLRP3 activation can reduce constitutive VEGF secretion by RPE and condition anti-angiogenic media; dampening inflammasome activation could normalize these changes, though net effects on VEGF in dry AMD are complex and model-dependent (Brandstetter, 2016, pp. 102–104).

Critical risks include interference with lysosomal proteostasis: CatB (with CatL, CatS, CatD) is central to POS processing and autophagy; prolonged or strong inhibition can lead to accumulation of undegraded cargo and lysosomal residual bodies, phenocopying aspects of degenerative RPE biology (Sharif, 2015, pp. 60–64, 189–192). At the concentrations typically required for CA-074Me efficacy (10–50 μM), off-target inhibition of other cysteine cathepsins or effects on lysosomal morphology and calpain readouts have been observed in non-RPE cells, warranting careful titration and orthogonal target validation (e.g., genetic CTSB perturbation) in RPE systems (Cho et al., 2013, pp. 2–6; Oberstein et al., 2021, pp. 6–10). Finally, because CatB’s inhibitory pharmacology is pH-sensitive and cytosolic CatB may drive NLRP3 activation post-LMP, CA-074Me may be more effective at reducing lysosomal CatB activity (and thus the amount available to leak) than at directly inhibiting CatB that has translocated to neutral pH cytosol; this distinction should be addressed experimentally, potentially by pairing CA-074/CA-074Me with a neutral-pH CatB inhibitor to ensure cytosolic coverage (Yoon et al., 2022, pp. 1–3, 6–8).

Expression evidence supporting the target in the cell type. Human RPE (cell lines ARPE-19 and D407; ex vivo RPE/choroid) express both inactive and active forms of CatB (and other cathepsins), consistent with robust lysosomal protease capacity in RPE; inhibition of cathepsins in RPE disrupts POS degradation and autophagy, aligning with the biological role of these enzymes in retinal homeostasis (Sharif, 2015, pp. 60–64, 189–192). In AMD-relevant RPE models, LMP-mediated activation of NLRP3 is cathepsin-dependent, and CatB/L inhibition prevents IL-1β/IL-18 release and pyroptosis, establishing functional target validation in RPE (Brandstetter, 2016, pp. 47–51, 92–95; Campbell & Doyle, 2013, pp. 5–7; Tseng et al., 2013, pp. 9–10).
