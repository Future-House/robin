Proposal for Tubastatin A (Sigma-Aldrich catalog #SML0044), a selective HDAC6 inhibitor.

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate: Tubastatin A is a fully synthetic, selective inhibitor of histone deacetylase 6 (HDAC6) that belongs to the hydroxamate-based class of HDAC inhibitors and is also classified among diarylamine-based derivatives. Originally inspired by the structure of tubacin, Tubastatin A was conceived through rational, structure-based drug design employing modern medicinal chemistry approaches that take advantage of the unique dimensions and catalytic zinc‐dependent active site of HDAC6 (Simões-Pires, Bertrand, & Cuendet, 2017; Wang, Wan, & Liu, 2018). Its chemical synthesis is characterized by straightforward methodologies involving less than five linear steps, which not only streamlines the production process but also facilitates the rapid derivation of analog series for further preclinical development (Simões-Pires, Bertrand, & Cuendet, 2017; Batchu, Brijmohan, & Advani, 2016). In addition to the common hydroxamate zinc-binding motif, structural modifications such as the incorporation of a rigid, selectively tuned carbazole cap have contributed to Tubastatin A’s high selectivity for HDAC6 over other isoforms (Butler et al., 2010; Shen & Kozikowski, 2020a). As a member of a growing portfolio of HDAC6 inhibitors that originated from oncology and neurodegenerative research, Tubastatin A offers a well-documented chemical scaffold that has been optimized for both potency—with an IC₅₀ of approximately 15 nM—and favorable pharmacological properties including significant selectivity and in vivo tolerability (Wang, Wan, & Liu, 2018; Butler et al., 2010). Its synthetic origin and evolution from earlier molecules exemplify current trends in epigenetic drug discovery, where the focus is on generating compounds with high specificity toward individual HDAC family members to mitigate off-target effects while maintaining anticancer or neuroprotective efficacy (Batchu, Brijmohan, & Advani, 2016; Cosenza & Pozzi, 2018).

Therapeutic History: Tubastatin A has been extensively evaluated in preclinical studies within oncology and neurodegenerative disease frameworks, where HDAC6 has been implicated as a pivotal regulatory enzyme in both cancer cell survival and neuronal homeostasis (Simões-Pires, Bertrand, & Cuendet, 2017; Kalin & Bergman, 2013). In cancer research, Tubastatin A and its diarylamine- and hydroxamate-based analogs have been leveraged for their ability to selectively inhibit HDAC6-mediated deacetylation, thereby restoring acetylation of α-tubulin and counteracting aberrant cellular processes that are characteristic of malignant transformation. For instance, studies in multiple myeloma models have shown that selective inhibition of HDAC6 can create a therapeutic window that minimizes the collateral effects frequently observed with broad-spectrum HDAC inhibitors (Simões-Pires, Bertrand, & Cuendet, 2017). Parallel to its oncology applications, Tubastatin A has been assessed in a number of neuroprotective paradigms, where its action in increasing tubulin acetylation has been linked to the restoration of axonal transport mechanisms, mitochondrial dynamics, and overall neuronal integrity (Wang, Wan, & Liu, 2018; Butler et al., 2010). In rodent models of tauopathy and other neurodegenerative conditions, inhibition of HDAC6 with Tubastatin A has resulted in improvements in behavioral outcomes and the reduction of toxic protein aggregates, thereby lending credence to its molecular mechanism of action and efficacy in improving cellular autophagy (Kalin & Bergman, 2013; Selenica et al., 2014). Despite these promising preclinical results, there is as yet limited direct evidence of Tubastatin A being applied in models of dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) or in vivo studies specifically addressing retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cell dysfunction; however, several reports have highlighted the potential of HDAC6 inhibitors to modulate cellular processes, such as autophagosome–lysosome trafficking, that are directly relevant to the pathophysiology of dry AMD (Kalin & Bergman, 2013; Buniello et al., 2025). Moreover, the favorable ADME profiles and established tolerability demonstrated by Tubastatin A in central nervous system (CNS) models suggest that repurposing this drug for ocular applications, while innovative, would build on a robust preclinical framework documented in other therapeutic areas (Shen et al., 2020b; Cosenza & Pozzi, 2018). Thus, from a therapeutic history perspective, Tubastatin A’s well-characterized profile in non-ocular systems and its mechanistic implications on autophagy present a compelling rationale for its evaluation as a candidate for dry AMD, while also noting that direct studies in retinal models remain a gap to be addressed in future investigations (Kalin & Bergman, 2013; Selenica et al., 2014).

Mechanism of Action: At the molecular level, Tubastatin A exerts its pharmacological effects by selectively inhibiting HDAC6, a cytoplasmic enzyme that is uniquely involved in the deacetylation of non-histone proteins. The principal substrate of HDAC6 is α-tubulin, a major component of the microtubule network, whose acetylation status is critical for maintaining microtubule stability and facilitating intracellular transport events (Wang, Wan, & Liu, 2018; Butler et al., 2010). By binding to the active site of HDAC6 through its hydroxamate zinc-binding group, Tubastatin A chelates the catalytic zinc ion and thereby blocks the deacetylation reaction, leading to an accumulation of acetylated α-tubulin (Butler et al., 2010; Shen & Kozikowski, 2020a). This increase in tubulin acetylation enhances the binding affinity of motor proteins—such as dynein—which are essential for driving the microtubule-dependent movement of vesicles and organelles (Shen & Kozikowski, 2020a; Shen et al., 2020b). In particular, the microtubule network plays a critical role in the movement of autophagosomes towards lysosomes, a process that is necessary for the maturation of autophagic vesicles and the subsequent degradation of cellular waste such as misfolded proteins and toxic lipid aggregates (Curcio et al., 2024; Shen & Kozikowski, 2020a). In neurodegenerative models, enhanced acetylation of microtubules by Tubastatin A has been shown to restore axonal transport as well as improve the clearance of abnormal tau proteins, thereby mitigating neurotoxicity (Kalin & Bergman, 2013; Selenica et al., 2014). This mechanism is of particular interest with respect to retinal pigment epithelium function in dry AMD because RPE cells are known to accumulate lipofuscin and A2E as a result of impaired autophagic flux under oxidative stress conditions (Kalin & Bergman, 2013; Buniello et al., 2025). The working hypothesis posits that HDAC6-mediated deacetylation of α-tubulin and autophagy-related proteins hampers the effective transport and fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes; thus, by inhibiting HDAC6 with Tubastatin A, one can restore the acetylation status of these key proteins, enhance the motility of autophagosomes via improved dynein engagement, and ultimately promote the degradation of accumulated toxic substances (Shen & Kozikowski, 2020a; Olaoye et al., 2021). Furthermore, by selectively targeting HDAC6 without broadly inhibiting other histone deacetylases, Tubastatin A limits potential off-target effects that have historically been associated with non-selective HDAC inhibitors, thereby improving its safety margin and therapeutic index (Wang, Wan, & Liu, 2018; Butler et al., 2010). These molecular interactions collectively provide a plausible biochemical explanation for the observed neuroprotective and autophagy-enhancing effects of Tubastatin A in preclinical studies (Shen & Kozikowski, 2020a; Selenica et al., 2014).

Expected Effect: In the context of the proposed in vitro assays using induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells, it is expected that treatment with Tubastatin A will lead to a measurable increase in the acetylation of α-tubulin and autophagy-related proteins such as LC3. This pharmacodynamic response is anticipated to facilitate improved recruitment and binding of the dynein motor complex to microtubules, thereby enhancing autophagosome transport toward lysosomes (Wang, Wan, & Liu, 2018; Kalin & Bergman, 2013). Under conditions of oxidative stress—a known contributory factor in dry AMD pathogenesis—RPE cells typically exhibit impaired autophagic flux, leading to the accumulation of lipofuscin and A2E, which are cytotoxic by-products of incomplete degradation pathways (Kalin & Bergman, 2013; Buniello et al., 2025). The hypothesis guiding the proposed studies is that by inhibiting HDAC6, Tubastatin A will “rescue” the autophagic pathway; increased microtubule acetylation is expected to enhance the velocity and efficiency of autophagosomal movement across the cytoskeletal network, promoting more efficient fusion with lysosomes and subsequent degradation of cellular debris (Shen & Kozikowski, 2020a). This effect can be quantitatively assessed by monitoring changes in LC3-II levels, the clearance of accumulated lipofuscin and A2E, and overall improvements in RPE cell viability and function in stressed cultures (Olaoye et al., 2021; Curcio et al., 2024). Moreover, given that HDAC6 is expressed in RPE cells, as evidenced by reports linking HDAC6 activity to both neural and epithelial pathologies, a targeted inhibition is hypothesized to directly ameliorate the intracellular trafficking bottlenecks that lead to the pathogenic buildup of waste products (Kalin & Bergman, 2013; Shen et al., 2020b). The expected cellular outcomes include restoration of autophagic flux, increased clearance of detrimental aggregates, reduction in oxidative stress markers, and the preservation or improvement of RPE cellular integrity, all of which are critical for maintaining retinal homeostasis and mitigating progression in dry AMD (Buniello et al., 2025; Shen & Kozikowski, 2020a). The compound’s documented in vivo tolerability and CNS penetration further support the notion that Tubastatin A, if capable of traversing ocular barriers, may exert similar beneficial effects within the retinal microenvironment (Wang, Wan, & Liu, 2018; Cosenza & Pozzi, 2018).

Overall Evaluation: In summary, Tubastatin A emerges as a promising repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry age-related macular degeneration based on its well-established activity as a selective HDAC6 inhibitor, its robust synthetic accessibility, and its capacity to modulate autophagy-related processes. Strengths of Tubastatin A include its high potency (nanomolar IC₅₀), remarkable selectivity against other HDAC isoforms—which minimizes adverse off-target effects—and its documented ability to enhance acetylation of α-tubulin, thereby restoring microtubule stability and facilitating the transport of autophagic vesicles (Wang, Wan, & Liu, 2018; Butler et al., 2010). Its proven efficacy in models of neurodegeneration—in which similar cellular impairments of autophagosome trafficking and protein clearance are observed—adds further weight to the rationale for its application in RPE dysfunction associated with dry AMD (Kalin & Bergman, 2013; Selenica et al., 2014). The mechanistic hypothesis that HDAC6 inhibition can restore autophagosome–lysosome fusion and thereby accelerate the clearance of lipofuscin and A2E under oxidative conditions is supported by analogous findings in neuronal systems and emerging evidence from cellular assays (Kalin & Bergman, 2013; Shen & Kozikowski, 2020a). Additionally, Tubastatin A’s synthetic route, which permits rapid analog generation, its favorable ADME profiles, and the availability of preclinical safety data contribute significantly to its feasibility as a candidate for repurposing in ophthalmic applications (Shen & Kozikowski, 2020a; Cosenza & Pozzi, 2018).

Nevertheless, there remain some concerns and challenges that warrant attention. One of the primary weaknesses lies in the current paucity of direct evidence supporting Tubastatin A’s efficacy in ocular models or its ability to effectively penetrate the blood–retina barrier (Buniello et al., 2025; Kalin & Bergman, 2013). Furthermore, while the mechanistic data from neurodegenerative and oncological studies are compelling, translating these effects to the context of dry AMD will require careful verification in retinal-specific in vitro and in vivo systems. There is also the potential for unforeseen off-target effects in the unique microenvironment of the retina, where chronic oxidative stress and inflammatory cues may alter drug distribution or action (Shen & Kozikowski, 2020a). Additionally, although Tubastatin A’s selectivity minimizes inhibition of other HDAC isoforms, the role of non-HDAC6 deacetylation in maintaining retinal homeostasis is not fully elucidated; thus, comprehensive studies are needed to ensure that selective HDAC6 inhibition does not inadvertently disrupt other critical regulatory pathways within the RPE (Selenica et al., 2014; Butler et al., 2010).

In conclusion, Tubastatin A’s established profile as a potent, selective HDAC6 inhibitor—with its inherent ability to boost α-tubulin acetylation and thereby enhance autophagic trafficking—provides a strong scientific rationale for its further evaluation as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD. The candidate’s chemical and pharmacological attributes, which have been validated in parts through oncology and neurodegenerative research, align well with the proposed mechanism of action whereby impaired autophagosome–lysosome fusion contributes to RPE dysfunction in dry AMD. While pre-existing data primarily focus on its anti-tumor and neuroprotective roles, the mechanistic similarities shared between neuronal autophagic deficits and RPE deficits under oxidative stress conditions support the hypothesis that Tubastatin A may improve autophagic clearance of lipofuscin and A2E, thereby preserving RPE integrity (Kalin & Bergman, 2013; Buniello et al., 2025; Shen & Kozikowski, 2020a).

Moving forward, it will be critical to design rigorous preclinical studies using iPSC-derived RPE cells and appropriate animal models to evaluate the pharmacokinetics, ocular bioavailability, and long-term safety of Tubastatin A in the retinal setting. Such studies should incorporate detailed analyses of autophagy markers, lysosomal function, and the clearance kinetics of toxic aggregates under oxidative conditions. Additionally, investigations addressing potential off-target impacts and the capacity of Tubastatin A to penetrate ocular barriers will be essential to fully validate its repurposing for dry AMD. On balance, despite the current gaps in direct evidence for ocular applications, the strengths—including its targeted mechanism, robust safety profile in non-ocular systems, and ease of synthesis—position Tubastatin A as an attractive candidate for repurposing. It represents a scientifically sound and strategically promising approach to address the autophagic dysfunction underlying dry AMD, thereby meriting further investigation as part of an innovative therapeutic strategy (Wang, Wan, & Liu, 2018; Butler et al., 2010; Shen & Kozikowski, 2020a; Shen et al., 2020b).
