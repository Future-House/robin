Therapeutic Candidate 1:
Candidate: MK-2206 (Selleckchem catalog #S1078), an allosteric pan-AKT inhibitor with high potency against AKT2.
Hypothesis: In dry AMD, hyperactive AKT2 phosphorylates TFEB, preventing its nuclear translocation and downstream CLEAR gene induction. MK-2206 will selectively inhibit AKT2 in RPE cells, de-repress TFEB nuclear localization, restore lysosomal biogenesis and autophagic flux, accelerate lipofuscin/A2E clearance, and thereby protect RPE from oxidative and drusen-associated stress.
Reasoning: Genetic and functional studies (Ghosh et al. Nat Commun 2024) identify AKT2 as a validated node whose hyperactivation in CFH-variant and Akt2-KI mouse RPE leads to lysosomal dysfunction. MK-2206 has advanced into oncology trials (oral bioavailability, known PK/safety), providing a translationally tractable profile. In iPSC-derived RPE assays, AKT2 inhibition restored CLEAR network expression, reduced extracellular waste deposition, and rescued mitochondrial function. MK-2206’s known ADME, dose-response and target engagement (p-AKT2 decrease) profiles facilitate rapid repurposing. Its mechanism is specific and directly addresses the core lysosomal/autophagy defect in dry AMD.

Therapeutic Candidate 2:
Candidate: 3,4-Dimethoxychalcone (DMC; Cayman Chemical catalog #CAY32002), a mTOR-independent TFEB activator.
Hypothesis: DMC induces TFEB dephosphorylation independently of mTORC1, driving its nuclear translocation in RPE cells. This upregulates lysosomal and autophagy genes, enhances autophagic flux, and promotes degradation of photo-oxidized bisretinoids (A2E) and lipofuscin granules, thereby preventing RPE cell death in dry AMD.
Reasoning: DMC was reported to activate TFEB in mammalian cells and C. elegans, enhancing lysosomal clearance of aggregates without inhibiting mTOR (Science Transl Med 2018). It directly engages the calcineurin–TFEB axis, offering mechanistic specificity and avoiding immunosuppressive liabilities of rapalogs. In primary neuronal and epithelial models, DMC increased lysosome number, cathepsin activity, and autophagic flux. As a small, drug-like chalcone with established synthesis routes and favorable solubility, DMC can be tested in human iPSC-RPE A2E-loading assays (Cai et al. Ex Eye Res 2019) and in dry AMD mouse models. Its novelty lies in mTOR-independent TFEB activation, addressing lipofuscin accumulation via a previously underexploited mechanism.

Therapeutic Candidate 3:
Candidate: Tubastatin A (Sigma-Aldrich catalog #SML0044), a selective HDAC6 inhibitor.
Hypothesis: HDAC6-mediated deacetylation of α-tubulin and autophagy-related proteins impairs autophagosome transport and fusion with lysosomes in RPE. Tubastatin A will inhibit HDAC6, increase acetylation of microtubules and LC3/dynein complex, enhance autophagosome–lysosome fusion, accelerate clearance of lipofuscin and A2E under oxidative conditions, and preserve RPE integrity in dry AMD.
Reasoning: HDAC6 is a critical regulator of autophagosome motility and maturation; its inhibition restores autophagic flux in neurodegenerative and epithelial models (J Neurochem 2013). Tubastatin A exhibits nanomolar potency (IC50 ≈ 15 nM) and >100-fold selectivity over other HDACs, with documented in vivo tolerability and CNS penetration. In stressed ARPE-19 cells, boosting tubulin acetylation increases transport of autophagosomes to lysosomes. This mechanistic specificity addresses the trafficking bottleneck contributing to lipofuscin buildup. Tubastatin A’s established ADME/safety and commercial availability support rapid preclinical evaluation in iPSC-derived RPE autophagy/lipofuscin assays.

Therapeutic Candidate 4:
Candidate: MSL-7 (Cayman Chemical catalog #14818), a potent small-molecule TFEB activator via calcium–calcineurin signaling.
Hypothesis: MSL-7 elevates cytosolic calcium in RPE cells, activates calcineurin phosphatase activity, triggers TFEB dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation, upregulates CLEAR network genes, boosts lysosomal capacity and autophagic flux, and thereby enhances removal of lipofuscin/A2E, mitigating RPE atrophy in dry AMD.
Reasoning: MSL-7 was identified through high-content screening as a selective TFEB agonist that functions via Ca2+-dependent calcineurin rather than mTOR inhibition, delivering robust lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy in neuronal and hepatic models (Nat Chem Biol 2019). It achieved clearance of proteotoxic aggregates in vivo with minimal off-target effects. Mechanistic specificity (calcineurin–TFEB axis) avoids broad kinase inhibition. Commercial availability, favorable in vivo PK, and established dosing in murine studies underline its developmental feasibility. Applying MSL-7 to human iPSC-derived RPE models of lipofuscin accumulation will directly test its therapeutic potential in dry AMD.

Therapeutic Candidate 5:
Candidate: Nilotinib (Tasigna®, Novartis), a c-Abl tyrosine kinase inhibitor.
Hypothesis: In RPE cells, c-Abl activation under oxidative and drusen stress phosphorylates and inhibits key autophagy initiators (ULK1, Beclin1), blocking autophagic flux and promoting lipofuscin accumulation. Nilotinib will inhibit c-Abl, release autophagy initiation checkpoints, activate ULK1/Beclin1 complex, restore autophagic flux and lysosomal degradation of A2E, and prevent RPE degeneration in dry AMD.
Reasoning: Nilotinib crosses the blood–retina barrier at low doses, has a well-characterized safety profile, and is FDA-approved for CML. In Parkinson’s models, nilotinib enhanced autophagy, reduced aggregate burden, and improved neuronal survival by inhibiting c-Abl–mediated ULK1 phosphorylation. c-Abl’s role in RPE autophagy blockade underlies lipofuscin build-up (Autophagy 2021). Repurposing nilotinib leverages human PK/safety data; dose-response can be assessed in RPE cultures (A2E challenge) and dry AMD rodent models. The clear molecular mechanism offers high translational value and specificity.
