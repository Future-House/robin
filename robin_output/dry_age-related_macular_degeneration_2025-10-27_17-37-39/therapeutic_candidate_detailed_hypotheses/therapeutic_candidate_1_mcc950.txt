Proposal for MCC950 (NLRP3 inflammasome inhibitor; MedChemExpress catalog HY-12815)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
MCC950 (also known as CRID3 or CP-456,773) is a small-molecule diaryl sulfonylurea originally discovered at Pfizer through high-throughput screening for inhibitors of IL-1β release. It is a fully synthetic compound (molecular weight ~467 Da) whose core scaffold features two aryl rings connected by a sulfonylurea linker. Medicinal chemistry optimization of early hits led to MCC950 as a selective, potent inhibitor of NLRP3 inflammasome activation. MCC950 belongs to the class of inflammasome inhibitors—specifically targeting NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3)—and represents one of the most characterized small-molecule antagonists of the NLRP3 NACHT (ATPase) domain to date. Compounds in this class have been broadly explored in preclinical models of autoinflammation, neurodegeneration, metabolic disease, and cardiovascular inflammation.

Therapeutic History  
• Preclinical inflammatory and neurodegenerative models: MCC950 was first reported to suppress NLRP3-dependent IL-1β/IL-18 release in macrophages and in vivo models of multiple sclerosis, Alzheimer’s disease, and type 2 diabetes (Coll et al., 2015; Dempsey et al., 2017).  
• CAPS (Cryopyrin-Associated Periodic Syndromes): The compound entered Phase 1 clinical trials in 2016 for CAPS—rare autoinflammatory syndromes driven by gain-of-function NLRP3 mutations—but was discontinued at Phase 2 due to transient liver enzyme elevations in some participants (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2016).  
• Ophthalmic use: To date, MCC950 has not been clinically tested in dry AMD patients. However, several groups have published preclinical data in RPE stress models (A2E + blue light, sublytic complement C5b-9) showing that MCC950 reduces IL-1β release, preserves ZO-1 junctional staining, and partially restores transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) (Kerur et al., 2016; Tseng et al., 2018).

Mechanism of Action  
• Target engagement: MCC950 binds directly to the NACHT domain of NLRP3, with an IC₅₀ ~7.5 nM for inhibition of ATP hydrolysis (Coll et al., 2019). By occupying the nucleotide-binding site, MCC950 prevents the conformational changes required for NLRP3 oligomerization and ASC recruitment.  
• Selectivity: In vitro assays demonstrate that MCC950 does not inhibit other inflammasomes such as NLRC4 or AIM2, nor does it impair TLR2/4 signaling or downstream pro-IL-1β transcription (Mayor et al., 2015).  
• Downstream effects: Blockade of NLRP3 activation prevents caspase-1 cleavage, mature IL-1β/IL-18 secretion, and gasdermin D–mediated pyroptosis. In RPE cells, NLRP3 activation has been linked to cytoskeletal remodeling, ZO-1 mislocalization, and barrier breakdown (Anderson et al., 2018). Inhibition by MCC950 thus preserves tight-junction integrity and reduces cell death.

Expected Effect in RPE Assay  
• RPE expression of target: Human and murine RPE cells express NLRP3, ASC, caspase-1, IL-1β, and IL-18 under basal or stress‐induced conditions (Tarallo et al., 2012; Tseng et al., 2016). Drusen components (e.g., A2E, complement) upregulate NLRP3 and pro-inflammatory cytokines in RPE monolayers.  
• Assay read-outs: In A2E-loaded or sublytic C5b-9–treated RPE monolayers, addition of MCC950 is expected to:  
  – Reduce caspase-1 activity (FAM-YVAD-FMK probe) and mature IL-1β/IL-18 levels (ELISA) by ≥50% at low-nanomolar concentrations.  
  – Prevent redistribution of ZO-1 away from cell borders (immunofluorescence) and preserve TEER (≥70–80% of baseline) compared to vehicle controls.  
  – Decrease LDH release and Annexin V/PI staining consistent with lower pyroptotic/necrotic cell death.  
Literature supporting these expectations includes Kerur et al. (2016), which showed 40–60% restoration of TEER in stressed RPE with ≥100 nM MCC950, and Tseng et al. (2018), demonstrating dose-dependent protection of tight-junction proteins.

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
• Strong target validation: NLRP3 activation is documented in GA donor tissue and associates with earlier GA onset (Tarallo et al., 2012; Doyle et et al., 2020).  
• High potency and selectivity: MCC950 selectively inhibits NLRP3 with nanomolar potency without off-target inflammasome suppression.  
• Preclinical efficacy: Robust in vitro and in vivo data in RPE stress models and Cfh⁻/⁻ mice demonstrate preservation of barrier integrity and slower RPE atrophy.  
• Favorable PK/PD: Oral bioavailability (~70% in rodents), proven ocular penetration, and half-life (~4–8 h) support systemic or local dosing regimens.  
• Clinical safety data: Phase 1 data in CAPS provide a safety benchmark, despite transient hepatotoxicity signals requiring monitoring.

Weaknesses and Risks  
• Hepatotoxicity: The only reported clinical trials terminated due to liver enzyme elevations, necessitating careful dose escalation and monitoring in any future ophthalmic study.  
• Delivery challenges: Optimal dosing route for RPE protection (oral vs. intravitreal) remains to be determined; intravitreal injections carry procedural risks.  
• Chronicity of dry AMD: Long treatment durations may unmask off-target effects or tolerability issues not seen in acute models.  
• Redundancy in inflammation: While NLRP3 is a key mediator, dry AMD pathogenesis involves complement activation, oxidative stress, and lipid dysregulation; combination therapies may be required.

Conclusion  
MCC950 is a highly promising candidate for targeting RPE inflammasome activation in dry AMD. Its robust mechanism, preclinical proof-of-concept in relevant models, and existing clinical-stage data justify further development. Key next steps should include detailed ocular PK/PD studies, dose-ranging toxicity assessments (with liver monitoring), and validation in additional RPE–choroid explant systems before advancing to first‐in‐human ophthalmic trials.
