Proposal for S1QEL-1.1 (Cayman Chemical #25658)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
S1QEL-1.1 is a small synthetic molecule identified as a “suppressor of site IQ electron leak” (S1QEL) that has been developed to target mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) production selectively at the quinone‐reaction (IQ) site of Complex I. This compound belongs to a distinct chemical class uncovered through high‐throughput screening efforts of over 635,000 compounds, which yielded two primary structural families: the thiazole‐type S1QEL1 series (of which S1QEL-1.1 is a leading representative) and the piperazine‐type S1QEL2 series (Banba et al., 2019). As a thiazole‐type compound, S1QEL-1.1 was chemically synthesized and subsequently optimized using structure–activity relationship (SAR) studies; derivatives such as S1QEL1.1_D1–D3 were generated to probe the essential chemical features required for function (Banba et al., 2019). In addition, photoaffinity probe derivatives ([125I]S1QEL1.1_PD1 and PD2) have been prepared to map its binding site in Complex I, underscoring both its synthetic tractability and the sophistication of the medicinal chemistry approach used in its development (Banba et al., 2019). As a class, S1QEL compounds have been primarily engineered to modulate mitochondrial ROS by selectively suppressing superoxide production at the IQ site without broad‐spectrum inhibition of electron transfer, a profile that distinguishes them from canonical Complex I inhibitors such as rotenone and piericidin A (Wong et al., 2019). S1QEL-1.1 is commercially available (for example, through Cayman Chemical under catalog number #25658), which not only facilitates its procurement for research but also supports its potential repurposing as a therapeutic candidate—particularly in indications where mitochondrial oxidative stress is regarded as a key pathogenic factor.

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, S1QEL compounds have been extensively characterized in biochemical assays using isolated mitochondria and submitochondrial particles. In these studies, S1QEL-1.1 and other members of the class were shown to potently suppress superoxide and hydrogen peroxide production specifically from the IQ site during conditions that favor reverse electron transfer, while sparing forward electron transport and thus preserving overall mitochondrial electron flow and ATP production (Wong et al., 2019). Preclinical studies in in vivo models have demonstrated the protective effects of S1QEL compounds in varied contexts; for instance, stress-induced stem cell hyperplasia in the Drosophila gut was ameliorated by S1QEL application, and in perfused mouse heart preparations, S1QELs reduced ischemia–reperfusion injury (Banba et al., 2019). Although these examples involve cardiac and developmental models, the central concept—that selective suppression of mitochondrial ROS can confer tissue protection—has broad implications for diseases where redox imbalance is a critical driver. Importantly, while there are numerous reports underscoring the utility of S1QELs in modulating mitochondrial ROS in neurons, cardiomyocytes, and skeletal muscle mitochondria (Banba et al., 2019), a direct application of S1QEL-1.1 to ocular disorders such as dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) has not yet been documented. However, given that dry AMD is characterized by heightened oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), where Complex I activity plays a central role in cellular metabolism and survival, the extension of S1QEL-1.1 into this field is a compelling and logical repurposing avenue (Banba et al., 2019).

Mechanism of Action:  
At a molecular level, S1QEL-1.1 acts by selectively suppressing the production of superoxide at the Complex I IQ site—a major source of mitochondrial ROS under conditions of reverse electron transfer. Mechanistic studies using bovine heart submitochondrial particles have revealed that S1QEL-1.1 achieves its effect by modulating the redox chemistry in the quinone-binding region while leaving the NADH oxidation (and hence overall electron flux) largely intact (Banba et al., 2019; Wong et al., 2019). Detailed photoaffinity labeling studies using radiolabeled derivatives ([125I]S1QEL1.1_PD1 and PD2) have mapped its binding to the ND1 subunit of Complex I, particularly near the matrix-side loop connecting transmembrane helices 5 and 6, which is distinct from the canonical quinone-binding pocket typically occupied by classical inhibitors such as rotenone (Banba et al., 2019). This binding does not fully block electron entry nor does it globally inhibit forward electron transfer, because the inhibitory potency for suppressing ROS generation is approximately 1–2 orders of magnitude lower than that required to inhibit basal respiratory activity (Wong et al., 2019). It has been proposed that the binding of S1QEL-1.1 induces subtle conformational changes in Complex I that reduce the probability of electron leakage to oxygen, thereby diminishing superoxide production without altering the essential catalytic cycle of electron transport. Furthermore, the mechanism is direction-dependent: S1QEL-1.1 exhibits significantly stronger inhibition of reverse electron transfer-associated ROS production than forward electron transfer, a property that has been linked to structural differences in the ND1 subunit that influence the flux of electrons under various bioenergetic states (Banba et al., 2019). Notably, competitive studies have demonstrated that the presence of S1QEL-1.1 reduces the inhibitory potency of established quinone-site inhibitors like piericidin A and rotenone, supporting the concept that S1QEL-1.1 interacts directly with, and alters, the conformation of, the quinone-binding region (Wong et al., 2019). Overall, the mechanism of action of S1QEL-1.1 is a fine modulation of the electron leak in Complex I—targeting the very site responsible for superoxide generation—without compromising the bioenergetic integrity of the mitochondrion (Banba et al., 2019; Wong et al., 2019).

Expected Effect:  
Under the hypothesis proposed by the research team, S1QEL-1.1 is expected to yield significant benefits in the context of dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) by acting on the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) mitochondria. In dry AMD, chronic light exposure, along with inflammatory and complement-mediated stress, leads to persistent mitochondrial dysfunction and escalated ROS production. Since the IQ site of Complex I has been identified as a major source of mitochondrial superoxide production—particularly under conditions of reverse electron transfer—S1QEL-1.1 is anticipated to selectively lower ROS output from this critical site in RPE mitochondria, thereby reducing oxidative damage (Wong et al., 2019).  
In a proposed RPE model of chronic light-induced stress, application of S1QEL-1.1 would be expected to attenuate mitochondrial superoxide formation significantly while preserving forward electron flux, NADH oxidation, and overall mitochondrial ATP synthesis. This preservation of bioenergetic function is crucial because the RPE relies on high levels of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation to support essential cellular functions, including the phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments and the recycling of visual pigments. Precedents for such effects have been documented in cardiomyocyte and neuronal models, where S1QEL-1.1 efficiently reduced ROS without impairing mitochondrial respiration, thereby contributing to tissue protection under stress (Banba et al., 2019).  
At the molecular level, by binding near the matrix side of the ND1 subunit and inducing conformational changes within the quinone-binding region, S1QEL-1.1 is projected to decrease electron leak from Complex I. This reduction in superoxide generation will likely translate into a lower burden of hydrogen peroxide and peroxynitrite formation, thus mitigating oxidative damage to mitochondrial and cellular components. The expected outcome is a protective effect in RPE cells under constant oxidative assault from light exposure and inflammation, ultimately contributing to the slowing or potential halting of dry AMD progression (Banba et al., 2019).  
Given that RPE cells express all key Complex I subunits, including the ND1 subunit that has been implicated as the binding site for S1QEL-1.1, it is reasonable to extrapolate that the expression profile in RPE cells is conducive to the compound’s mechanism of action (Banba et al., 2019). Moreover, because dysfunction of Complex I and excess mitochondrial ROS production are established features of age-related degeneration in the retina, selectively targeting the IQ site with S1QEL-1.1 represents an attractive strategy to restore redox balance and improve cell survival in the RPE (Banba et al., 2019; Wong et al., 2019).

Overall Evaluation:  
S1QEL-1.1 stands out as a highly promising drug candidate for repurposing in the treatment of dry age-related macular degeneration owing to its unique ability to selectively suppress mitochondrial ROS production at the Complex I IQ site without impacting the overall electron transport and ATP production. One of the major strengths of S1QEL-1.1 is its mechanism of action, which has been elegantly elucidated: it binds to a region of the ND1 subunit, distinct from the classical quinone-binding pocket, and modulates the rate constants of electron leak specifically associated with superoxide formation. This allosteric mechanism ensures that while pathological ROS production is curtailed, the critical functions of mitochondria—such as NADH oxidation and ATP synthesis—continue without significant perturbation (Banba et al., 2019; Wong et al., 2019). Such properties are particularly advantageous in the context of dry AMD, where maintaining robust cellular bioenergetics in the RPE is essential to counteract chronic stress from light exposure and inflammation.

Another strength lies in its direction-dependent inhibitory profile. S1QEL-1.1 is capable of exerting a more potent suppression of reverse electron transfer-induced ROS generation compared to forward electron transfer, thereby offering a more refined modulation of mitochondrial activity. This selective action is crucial in tissues such as the RPE that must continuously manage oxidative stress while still performing high levels of metabolic activity (Banba et al., 2019; Wong et al., 2019).

Furthermore, the extensive preclinical evaluation of S1QEL-1.1 in non-ocular tissues—including models of ischemia–reperfusion injury and stress-induced stem cell hyperplasia—provides a robust foundation for its potential repurposing. Although these studies were conducted in cardiac and developmental systems, the underlying principle—that targeted suppression of mitochondrial ROS can mitigate tissue damage—translates well to the pathophysiological mechanisms implicated in dry AMD (Banba et al., 2019).  

Nevertheless, several weaknesses and challenges must be considered. The therapeutic candidate has not yet been evaluated in any ocular or retinal models; therefore, its pharmacokinetics, tissue penetration, and long-term safety within the unique microenvironment of the eye remain to be established. The blood–retina barrier poses a significant hurdle for systemically administered compounds, and specialized delivery methods (such as intravitreal injection or targeted nanoparticle formulations) may be required to achieve therapeutic concentrations in the RPE (Banba et al., 2019). Additionally, although S1QEL-1.1 exhibits a favorable profile in isolated mitochondria and other cell types, chronic inhibition of mitochondrial ROS could potentially disrupt normal redox signaling mechanisms that are vital for cellular adaptation and homeostasis. Thus, determining the optimal dosing range that provides maximal protective effect without interfering with physiological signaling is critical (Wong et al., 2019).

Another potential limitation is the narrow therapeutic window in which S1QEL-1.1 operates. Preclinical data indicate that while low concentrations effectively suppress pathological superoxide production, higher concentrations may begin to inhibit forward electron transport, which could be deleterious in energy-demanding tissues like the RPE (Banba et al., 2019; Wong et al., 2019). This necessitates precise dose optimization and comprehensive toxicity studies prior to advancing into clinical trials.

Finally, despite a strong mechanistic rationale and promising biochemical data from non-ocular models, there is still an absence of direct evidence for efficacy in models of dry AMD. Translational research is required to validate whether the selective ROS suppression observed in vitro will translate into measurable therapeutic benefits in the context of retinal degeneration. In particular, the interplay between reduced mitochondrial ROS and the modulation of complement activation or inflammatory cascades in the RPE must be thoroughly investigated (Banba et al., 2019).

In summary, S1QEL-1.1 is a compelling candidate for repurposing in dry AMD based on its robust and selective mechanism of action in reducing mitochondrial ROS production at the Complex I IQ site while preserving oxidative phosphorylation. The strengths of its biochemical profile, including direction-dependent inhibition and a well-characterized binding site on Complex I, provide a strong scientific rationale for its use in a redox-driven degenerative disease. However, significant gaps remain in translating these findings to the ocular context, including challenges related to delivery across the blood–retina barrier, long-term safety in the retina, and the need for dedicated efficacy studies in dry AMD models. Addressing these issues through further in vitro experimentation using RPE cell lines under chronic light-induced stress, followed by targeted in vivo animal studies, will be essential to determine whether S1QEL-1.1 can fulfill its potential as a novel therapeutic for dry age-related macular degeneration (Banba et al., 2019; Wong et al., 2019).

Overall, while the existing literature provides a solid foundation in favor of the underlying mechanism and general protective effects of S1QEL-1.1 in reducing mitochondrial ROS, the candidate’s transition into an effective therapy for dry AMD will require extensive further research to ensure ocular efficacy, optimal dosing, and safety over long-term administration. These steps are critical to fully validate S1QEL-1.1 as a viable treatment option in the challenging and complex landscape of age-related macular degeneration (Banba et al., 2019; Wong et al., 2019).
