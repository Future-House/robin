Proposal for Olaparib (Selleckchem #S1060)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Olaparib is a small‐molecule, synthetic inhibitor originally discovered and developed for oncology indications, particularly in cancers harboring BRCA mutations. It belongs to the class of PARP (poly[ADP‐ribose] polymerase) inhibitors that function by binding to the catalytic domain of PARP enzymes, competitively inhibiting the binding of NAD+ and thereby blocking poly‐ADP‐ribosylation reactions. Olaparib was discovered through high‐throughput screening and subsequently optimized chemically to yield a compound with high potency and selectivity for PARP1 and PARP2. As a synthetic chemotherapeutic agent, olaparib has been extensively characterized and approved by regulatory agencies for the treatment of various malignancies such as ovarian and breast cancer. Its mechanism involves “trapping” PARP on damaged DNA, although in nononcologic applications the key therapeutic action is related to the inhibition of overactive PARP1 activity in conditions of oxidative DNA damage. In the context of dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD), the hypothesis is that olaparib will inhibit PARP1‐mediated NAD+ consumption in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells, thereby preserving both the cellular energy status and, indirectly, the activity of NAD+‐dependent enzymes such as the mitochondrial sirtuin SIRT3. This candidate falls within a larger class of repurposed drugs that are being re‐evaluated for neurodegenerative and retinal disorders, where dysregulated PARP activity is thought to be a key driver of cellular dysfunction under chronic oxidative stress (Jang et al., 2018).

Therapeutic History:  
Olaparib’s primary therapeutic history is rooted in oncology. It has been used clinically to target cancers that are deficient in homologous recombination repair, where its PARP‐inhibitory action leads to synthetic lethality in malignant cells. Over recent years, growing experimental evidence has implicated hyperactivation of PARP1 in a number of non-neoplastic conditions characterized by oxidative DNA damage and subsequent energetic collapse. Various studies carried out in retinal cell lines such as ARPE-19 under oxidative stress (e.g., H2O2 exposure) have demonstrated that pathological PARP1 activation leads to a depletion of NAD+ and ATP, mitochondrial depolarization, altered mitochondrial morphology, and ultimately, cell death; these studies have employed olaparib to effectively block PARP1 activity and thereby rescue the cells from these metabolic defects (Jang et al., 2018). In animal models that mimic features of dry AMD, such as sodium iodate (SI)-injected mice or rabbits, pharmacologic inhibition of PARP1 with olaparib resulted in preservation of retinal morphology including maintenance of outer nuclear layer thickness and improvements in electrophysiological readouts (Jang et al., 2018). To date, while significant preclinical evaluation has been performed in models of oxidative injury and retinal degeneration, olaparib has not yet been trialed clinically in dry AMD patients. However, the repurposing approach is supported by extensive literature in which PARP inhibitors have been proposed as a therapeutic approach to mitigate oxidative injury in neurodegenerative disorders, with the underlying rationale being the restoration of cellular NAD+ levels as well as improved mitochondrial bioenergetics (Jang et al., 2018).

Mechanism of Action:  
The well-established mechanism of action of olaparib is based on the inhibition of the PARP enzyme family, primarily targeting PARP1. Under conditions of oxidative stress, DNA strand breaks activate PARP1, which catalyzes the transfer of ADP-ribose units from NAD+ to several nuclear and mitochondrial proteins. This process, known as poly-ADP-ribosylation, normally plays a role in DNA repair; however, when overactivated—as is observed in conditions of persistent oxidative damage—PARP1 consumes enormous amounts of cellular NAD+, leading to ATP depletion and an ensuing collapse of cellular energy metabolism. Olaparib binds to the catalytic domain of PARP1, thereby preventing the excessive transfer of ADP-ribose moieties and preserving intracellular NAD+ levels. This preservation of NAD+ is critical because NAD+ is a necessary cofactor not only for PARP activity but also for the function of a range of NAD+-dependent deacetylases, including the sirtuin family.

Within the context of dry AMD, RPE cells subjected to oxidative stress exhibit PARP1 hyperactivation, resulting in NAD+ depletion that compromises cellular bioenergetics and contributes to mitochondrial dysfunction. While much of the literature has focused on the interplay between PARP1 and nuclear sirtuin SIRT1 in the context of autophagy, emerging hypotheses posit that similar mechanisms extend to the mitochondria. SIRT3, a key mitochondrial deacetylase, requires NAD+ to catalyze the deacetylation of multiple mitochondrial proteins. One well-characterized substrate is manganese superoxide dismutase (MnSOD), whose deacetylated, active form is responsible for detoxifying reactive oxygen species (ROS) within the mitochondria. The proposed mechanism is that by inhibiting PARP1, olaparib preserves NAD+ pools in RPE cells, which in turn sustains SIRT3 activity. Active SIRT3 would ensure proper deacetylation of mitochondrial proteins, including MnSOD, thus reducing mitochondrial protein hyperacetylation, curtailing mtROS production, and ultimately mitigating oxidative damage (Jang et al., 2018; Lin & Apte, 2018).

Notably, while multiple studies have documented that olaparib effectively reduces NAD+ depletion, preserves ATP levels, and maintains mitochondrial membrane potential in ARPE-19 cells under H2O2 challenge (Jang et al., 2018), direct assessments of SIRT3 activity and the acetylation state of mitochondrial proteins remain to be specifically reported. Several excerpts unequivocally state that although the biochemical cascade from PARP1 hyperactivation to NAD+ loss is well-documented, explicit data connecting PARP inhibition by olaparib to restored SIRT3-mediated mitochondrial deacetylation have not been presented (Jang et al., 2018). Thus, the mechanistic rationale is largely built on the inference that a well-maintained NAD+ pool under olaparib treatment will naturally support the activity of all NAD+-dependent enzymes, including SIRT3 (Jang et al., 2018).

Expected Effect:  
The expected effect of olaparib in experimental models of dry AMD is multifaceted. First, by inhibiting PARP1, olaparib should significantly reduce the accumulation of poly-ADP-ribose (PAR) polymers that are normally generated in response to oxidative DNA damage. This reduction in PARP1 activity would result in preservation of intracellular NAD+ and ATP levels, thereby preventing the energy collapse characteristic of parthanatos—a form of cell death distinct from classical apoptosis that has been implicated in RPE degeneration (Jang et al., 2018).

In in vitro assays using ARPE-19 cells, olaparib is expected to block H2O2-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, as evidenced by improved mitochondrial membrane potential measurements and normalized rates of ATP production. Functionally, such bioenergetic stabilization should correlate with increased cell survival and improved resistance to oxidative stress. Moreover, given the central role of NAD+ as a substrate for mitochondrial sirtuins, it is anticipated that olaparib treatment will indirectly sustain SIRT3 activity within the mitochondria. Although direct measurements of SIRT3 activity and mitochondrial protein acetylation were not explicitly given in the reviewed studies, the biochemical paradigm suggests that when NAD+ concentrations are conserved, SIRT3 maintains the deacetylated and therefore active state of key proteins. For example, the deacetylation of MnSOD by SIRT3 is crucial for controlling mtROS levels. A reduction in mitochondrial protein hyperacetylation would likely result in lower levels of mitochondrial oxidative stress, improved mitochondrial respiration, and an overall protective effect on both the metabolic and structural integrity of RPE cells (Jang et al., 2018; Lin & Apte, 2018).

In animal models designed to mimic the degenerative environment of dry AMD—such as the sodium iodate (SI) induced retinal degeneration model—olaparib has been shown to preserve retinal morphology. For instance, olaparib-treated animals exhibited maintenance of outer nuclear layer thickness and more robust electroretinogram (ERG) wave amplitudes compared to SI-treated controls (Jang et al., 2018). Thus, the expected effect of olaparib extends from the biochemical (preservation of NAD+ and suppression of PAR polymer formation) to the functional (preservation of retinal structure and function) levels. In assays that measure oxidative damage, assays of mitochondrial function, and endpoint assessments of RPE survival, olaparib treatment is therefore predicted to yield significant improvements relative to untreated, oxidatively stressed controls.

Overall Evaluation:  
The overall evaluation of olaparib as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD proceeds from its well-characterized mechanism of action and a robust body of preclinical evidence, while also acknowledging certain gaps that need to be addressed in future research.

Strengths:  
1. The Mechanism Is Well-Established in Related Contexts: Olaparib’s ability to inhibit PARP1 has been incontrovertibly demonstrated both biochemically and in various disease models. Preclinical studies in ARPE-19 cells have repeatedly shown that olaparib can mitigate PARP1-dependent NAD+ depletion and restore cellular ATP levels under oxidative stress conditions (Jang et al., 2018). This is especially relevant in dry AMD, where RPE loss and mitochondrial dysfunction are driven by oxidative insults and chronic PARP activation.  
2. Preservation of NAD+ Pools: By blocking PARP1 overactivation, olaparib helps preserve the intracellular NAD+ pool, which is critical for maintaining the activity of NAD+-dependent enzymes. Although most studies have focused on the nuclear deacetylase SIRT1, the biochemical logic extends to mitochondrial sirtuins such as SIRT3. Given SIRT3’s essential role in deacetylating enzymes involved in the detoxification of ROS—most notably MnSOD—its preservation is of potential therapeutic importance in the context of dry AMD (Jang et al., 2018).  
3. Preclinical Efficacy in Retinal Models: Several studies have used olaparib in retinal oxidative injury models, demonstrating improvements in mitochondrial potential, cellular bioenergetics, and even in in vivo retinal function as measured by ERG. These data support the notion that PARP inhibition can translate into a meaningful rescue of retinal structure and function (Jang et al., 2018; Akkaya et al., 2025).  
4. Established Clinical Use in Oncology: Olaparib is already approved by major regulatory agencies for several cancer indications, meaning that its pharmacokinetics, safety profile, and manufacturing processes are well characterized. This facilitates the drug repurposing pathway and may allow for a head start in clinical development for dry AMD, provided that formulation and ocular delivery challenges can be overcome (Jang et al., 2018).

Weaknesses and Considerations:  
1. Incomplete Evidence on SIRT3 and Mitochondrial Deacetylation: While the preservation of NAD+ is well documented, direct evidence linking olaparib treatment to sustained SIRT3 activity and the corresponding maintenance of mitochondrial protein deacetylation is sparse in the current literature. Several studies explicitly note that measurements of SIRT3 activity or assessments of mitochondrial protein acetylation status were not performed in the RPE/retinal models reviewed (Jang et al., 2018; Du et al., 2016). Given that the hypothesis specifically posits that olaparib preserves SIRT3 activity—and, by extension, proper mitochondrial deacetylation—this represents a critical gap that must be filled by targeted experimental studies.  
2. DNA Repair and Long-Term Safety: PARP1 plays an important role in normal DNA repair processes. Chronic inhibition in a non‐oncologic setting such as dry AMD raises concerns about potential cumulative genomic instability in retinal cells. Therefore, long-term dosing regimens must be carefully optimized to balance neuroprotection against the risk of impairing physiologic DNA repair mechanisms (Jadeja et al., 2020; Jadeja et al., 2018).  
3. Delivery and Bioavailability Challenges: Although olaparib is effective systemically in oncology, its bioavailability in ocular tissues following systemic administration may be suboptimal. Successful repurposing for dry AMD may require the development of novel ocular delivery formulations, such as topical eye drops or intravitreal injections, to achieve therapeutic concentrations at the level of the RPE without incurring off-target systemic toxicities (Kovacs et al., 2019; Jang et al., 2018).  
4. Translational Gap: Most of the available evidence for olaparib’s efficacy derives from preclinical models, including in vitro experiments in ARPE-19 cells and in vivo studies in animal models of retinal degeneration. There is currently no clinical trial data for olaparib in dry AMD, which increases the uncertainty associated with its translational potential. Furthermore, the pathophysiology of dry AMD is multifactorial and may involve additional pathways that are not fully addressed by PARP inhibition alone (Jang et al., 2018).  
5. Potential Off-Target Effects: While olaparib is selective for PARP enzymes, the extent to which off-target effects may impact retinal homeostasis is not fully understood. Ocular cells might have unique metabolic or repair requirements that differ from those in oncologic tissues, necessitating careful preclinical toxicology studies.

Overall, the repurposing of olaparib for dry AMD is supported by a robust mechanistic rationale—namely, that excessive PARP1 activation under oxidative stress depletes NAD+ reserves, leading to energy collapse and retinal cell death. By inhibiting PARP1, olaparib rescues NAD+ levels and has been shown to restore mitochondrial integrity and preserve retinal function in preclinical models. This fundamental mechanism is highly relevant to dry AMD pathogenesis, where oxidative stress-mediated damage to the RPE is a central feature. Moreover, given that NAD+ is required for the activity of sirtuins (including nuclear SIRT1 and mitochondrial SIRT3), ultimately ensuring proper deacetylation of key enzymes and maintenance of mitochondrial homeostasis, the overall therapeutic cascade proposed by the hypothesis is well grounded in established biochemistry (Jang et al., 2018).

Nevertheless, while there is convincing evidence regarding the ability of olaparib to inhibit PARP1 and rescue bioenergetic failure in RPE under oxidative stress, a crucial aspect of the proposal—that PARP inhibition preserves SIRT3 activity and thus maintains mitochondrial deacetylation—remains largely inferential. Several studies have noted the absence of direct measurements of SIRT3 activity or mitochondrial protein acetylation status after olaparib treatment in RPE models (Jang et al., 2018; Du et al., 2016). It is conceivable that, if NAD+ pools are restored, SIRT3 function will inherently be maintained; however, without direct experimental validation, this remains an unproven aspect of the therapeutic mechanism. Moreover, the potential consequences of long-term PARP inhibition—particularly with respect to DNA repair fidelity—and the challenges of delivering adequate concentrations of the drug to the retina without systemic toxicity cannot be overlooked.

In addition, while preclinical studies using SI-induced retinal degeneration models have shown promising results with olaparib in terms of improving retinal morphology and function (as per improvements in ERG wave amplitudes and preservation of outer nuclear layer thickness), these models do not capture the full complexity of dry AMD in humans. Dry AMD is a chronic, multifactorial disorder with contributions from inflammation, drusen formation, and cumulative RPE dysfunction over years; thus, short-term protective effects observed in animal models, while encouraging, might not directly translate to robust clinical efficacy.

Taking into account both the strengths and the weaknesses, the overall potential for olaparib as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD is promising but not yet definitive. The strengths lie in its well-documented ability to inhibit PARP1, prevent NAD+ depletion, and improve mitochondrial bioenergetics in models of retinal oxidative injury. These effects are directly relevant to the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying dry AMD, where RPE degeneration is driven by chronic oxidative stress. Furthermore, the fact that olaparib is already an approved drug for other indications offers a strategic advantage in terms of repurposing and regulatory approval. However, the lack of direct evidence linking olaparib treatment to the restoration of mitochondrial SIRT3 activity and normalization of mitochondrial protein acetylation is a significant gap in the current literature. Addressing this gap would require focused studies that assess SIRT3 enzyme activity, evaluate the acetylation status of mitochondrial proteins such as MnSOD, and determine whether these molecular endpoints correlate with improved RPE survival and function. Additionally, long-term safety studies that investigate the potential risk of impaired DNA repair in retinal cells under chronic PARP inhibition are warranted. Finally, developing an appropriate ocular delivery system that ensures effective local concentrations of olaparib while minimizing systemic exposure will be critical for translating these preclinical findings into a viable clinical candidate.

In conclusion, olaparib’s repurposing for dry AMD has significant mechanistic and preclinical support. Its ability to inhibit PARP1 offers a means to preserve NAD+ levels in RPE cells, thereby potentially maintaining mitochondrial function and reducing oxidative injury—a cascade that is highly attractive for a disease characterized by chronic oxidative stress. Although the evidence for PARP inhibition in rescuing NAD+ and ATP levels in the RPE is strong (Jang et al., 2018), the specific link between NAD+ preservation and the maintenance of mitochondrial sirtuin SIRT3 activity remains to be directly demonstrated. Addressing this knowledge gap through targeted biochemical assays will be essential before advancing olaparib into clinical trials for dry AMD. Overall, given the compelling preclinical data, existing clinical experience in oncology, and the critical need for novel therapeutics in dry AMD, olaparib represents a promising candidate that merits further investigation. Future studies should focus on elucidating its effects on mitochondrial deacetylation in RPE, optimizing ocular drug delivery, and carefully assessing long-term safety in the context of chronic retinal disease (Jang et al., 2018; Du et al., 2016; Lin & Apte, 2018).
