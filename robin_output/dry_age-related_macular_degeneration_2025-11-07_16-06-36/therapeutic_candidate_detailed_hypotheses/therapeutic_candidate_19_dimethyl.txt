Proposal for Dimethyl fumarate (Tecfidera; Biogen)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Dimethyl fumarate (DMF) is a synthetic derivative of fumaric acid that belongs to the class of fumaric acid esters (FAEs) (Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 1–2). Chemically, DMF is the dimethyl ester of fumaric acid, with a molecular weight of approximately 144.13 g/mol, and is produced by reacting fumaric acid with methanol in the presence of a strong acid catalyst such as sulfuric acid (Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 1–2). Although fumaric acid occurs naturally as an intermediate in the citric acid cycle, DMF itself is fully synthetically manufactured, conferring the advantages of chemical stability and the ability to precisely modulate its structure to enhance pharmacological properties (Foresti, 2014, pp. 102–106). Moreover, DMF is classified as an electrophilic molecule that, due to its α,β-unsaturated ester structure, can covalently modify protein thiols, particularly the cysteine residues within KEAP1, thereby initiating its downstream effects on cellular redox homeostasis (Cuadrado et al., 2019, pp. 21–25; Foresti, 2014, pp. 102–106). Fumaric acid esters as a class have a long-standing history in pharmacotherapy, originally finding application in dermatology, most notably in the treatment of psoriasis, and have since been repurposed for neurological conditions such as multiple sclerosis (MS) (Cuadrado et al., 2019, pp. 21–25; Shimizu et al., 2022, pp. 1–2).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, fumaric acid esters were introduced into clinical practice as antipsoriatic agents in Europe, with formulations such as Fumaderm® entering the market in the 1990s (Shimizu et al., 2022, pp. 1–2; Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 2–3). DMF in particular emerged as a key component in these formulations, and its efficacy in modulating immune responses and suppressing inflammation in skin diseases laid the foundation for its later repurposing in other systemic inflammatory conditions (Cuadrado et al., 2019, pp. 21–25; Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 2–3). Over time, DMF was repurposed and further developed as an oral agent for the treatment of relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS), culminating in its FDA approval in 2013 under the trade name Tecfidera® by Biogen (Foresti, 2014, pp. 102–106; Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 2–3; Promsote, 2015, pp. 157–163). Clinical trials, including large-scale phase 3 studies, have demonstrated its favorable safety profile and efficacy in reducing relapse rates and slowing disability progression in MS patients (Promsote, 2015, pp. 157–163; Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 14–16). Although there is no extensive historical record of DMF being used directly for dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) or other primary ocular diseases, recent preclinical findings have indicated that its potent activation of the nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway may confer significant protective effects on the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) (Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 2–3; Shu et al., 2022, pp. 2–3). This has sparked considerable interest in repurposing DMF for ocular indications such as geographic atrophy, a late stage of dry AMD, by leveraging its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities observed in nonocular settings (Buglak et al., 2018, pp. 11–12; Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 2–3).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, DMF functions primarily as a prodrug; upon oral administration it is rapidly hydrolyzed by esterases within the gastrointestinal tract to yield its active metabolite, monomethyl fumarate (MMF) (Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 1–2; ClinicalTrials.gov, 2022). The pharmacodynamic actions of DMF principally involve the electrophilic modification of specific cysteine residues—most notably cysteine 151—on Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1) (Cuadrado et al., 2019, pp. 21–25). Under physiological conditions, KEAP1 binds to and retains the transcription factor Nrf2 in the cytoplasm, targeting it for ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. However, when DMF covalently modifies KEAP1, it disrupts this complex, allowing Nrf2 to dissociate and translocate into the nucleus (Cuadrado et al., 2019, pp. 21–25; Manai & Amadio, 2022, pp. 1–2). In the nucleus, Nrf2 binds to antioxidant response elements (AREs) in the promoters of numerous genes encoding cytoprotective enzymes, including glutathione S-transferases (GSTs), NAD(P)H quinone dehydrogenase 1 (NQO1), heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), and various enzymes involved in glutathione (GSH) synthesis and regeneration (Cuadrado et al., 2019, pp. 21–25; Foresti, 2014, pp. 102–106; Manai & Amadio, 2022, pp. 1–2). These molecular interactions lead to enhanced antioxidant defenses, improved detoxification of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and overall better maintenance of cellular redox balance (Foresti, 2014, pp. 102–106; Shimizu et al., 2022, pp. 1–2). In addition to its role in promoting antioxidant gene expression via Nrf2 activation, DMF has been shown to have immunomodulatory properties; it can shift cytokine profiles by increasing interleukin-4 (IL-4)–expressing CD4+ T cells and decreasing the production of proinflammatory cytokines such as interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and interleukin-17 (IL-17) (Camelo, 2025, pp. 6–7; Buglak et al., 2018, pp. 20–21). Furthermore, DMF has been reported to inhibit nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) activation in certain cellular contexts, thus contributing to a reduction in inflammatory signaling independently of Nrf2 (Shu et al., 2022, pp. 2–3; Manai & Amadio, 2022, pp. 1–2). Together, these molecular actions result in lowered cellular oxidative stress and an improved inflammatory milieu, which are critical factors implicated in the pathogenesis of chronic degenerative diseases (Foresti, 2014, pp. 102–106; Shimizu et al., 2022, pp. 1–2).

Expected Effect in Dry Age-Related Macular Degeneration:  
Dry AMD, particularly the geographic atrophy form, is characterized by the gradual degeneration of RPE cells, which leads to subsequent photoreceptor loss and irreversible vision impairment. Central to the pathophysiology of dry AMD are oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and chronic low-grade inflammation that progressively compromise RPE viability and function (Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 2–3; Shimizu et al., 2022, pp. 1–2). The hypothesis being explored is that DMF, through its robust activation of the Nrf2 pathway, can enhance the expression of ARE-driven genes that encode for antioxidant and cytoprotective proteins, thereby counteracting the deleterious effects of oxidative stress in the RPE (Marchesi et al., 2024, pp. 19–20; Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 13–14). In particular, the upregulation of enzymes involved in glutathione synthesis is expected to increase intracellular levels of GSH, a critical thiol antioxidant that protects mitochondrial membranes and supports cellular bioenergetics (Shu et al., 2022, pp. 1–2; Buglak et al., 2018, pp. 20–21). Preclinical studies in retinal models have demonstrated that DMF can effectively preserve mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) and reduce the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β), both of which are hallmarks of RPE distress under oxidative challenge (Marchesi et al., 2024, pp. 19–20; Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 2–3). Given that the RPE is highly metabolically active and particularly vulnerable to cumulative oxidative damage, the sustained activation of Nrf2 by DMF following oral dosing is anticipated to provide continuous reinforcement of antioxidant defenses and maintain mitochondrial integrity in these cells (Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 3–5; Shu et al., 2022, pp. 1–2). Moreover, by mitigating inflammatory signaling, DMF may not only protect the RPE but also contribute to a more favorable microenvironment that limits the propagation of cell death signals, thus potentially slowing the progression of geographic atrophy in dry AMD (Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 2–3; Buglak et al., 2018, pp. 11–12). Collectively, these expected cellular effects support the rationale for using DMF as an intervention to preserve retinal structure and function by lowering oxidative damage and supporting bioenergetics in the RPE.

Overall Evaluation:  
The potential of dimethyl fumarate as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD rests on a solid mechanistic rationale combined with an established clinical pedigree in other indications. One of the primary strengths of DMF is its well-characterized pharmacological profile; it is FDA-approved for multiple sclerosis and has been safely administered to thousands of patients over several years, which significantly mitigates the risk and regulatory hurdles typically associated with repurposed drugs (Foresti, 2014, pp. 102–106; Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 2–3). The dual actions of DMF—in particular, its ability to both activate the Nrf2 pathway and attenuate proinflammatory signals—directly target the underlying pathological processes in dry AMD, where oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction are central drivers of RPE degeneration (Cuadrado et al., 2019, pp. 21–25; Manai & Amadio, 2022, pp. 1–2; Shu et al., 2022, pp. 2–3). In vitro studies have provided encouraging evidence that DMF increases intracellular glutathione levels and protects RPE cells from oxidative insults, findings that are directly translatable to the pathobiology of dry AMD (Marchesi et al., 2024, pp. 19–20; Shu et al., 2022, pp. 1–2).

Nonetheless, several challenges remain. Although DMF is known to be effective in MS and psoriasis, the ocular milieu presents unique pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic challenges that may not be fully addressed by the current oral formulations (Camelo, 2025, pp. 11–13; Buonfiglio et al., 2024, pp. 17–18). The rapid hydrolysis of DMF to MMF means that, while the active metabolite does exert therapeutic effects, fine-tuning the dosing regimen to ensure adequate drug levels in the retina while minimizing systemic side effects is necessary (Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 1–2; ClinicalTrials.gov, 2022). In addition, common adverse reactions associated with DMF, such as gastrointestinal discomfort and flushing, though generally mild to moderate, could be more problematic in the typically elderly dry AMD population, necessitating careful monitoring and potentially modified dosing strategies (Camelo, 2025, p. 17; Camelo, 2025, pp. 11–13). There is also the question of whether systemic administration of DMF can generate sufficient concentrations in the retina to achieve a meaningful therapeutic effect. While emerging evidence suggests that DMF can cross the blood-retinal barrier and activate Nrf2 in retinal cells (Shu et al., 2022, pp. 12–13; Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 2–3), further pharmacokinetic and translational studies are warranted to confirm its retinal bioavailability and the optimal dosing regimen for ocular indications.

Another consideration is the complexity of dry AMD itself, which is a slowly progressive, multifactorial disease with contributions from genetic predispositions, environmental exposures, and systemic metabolic disturbances. It remains to be seen whether the monotherapeutic approach of DMF, targeting primarily oxidative stress via Nrf2 activation, will be sufficient to halt or reverse disease progression, or whether combination therapies will ultimately be required (Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 2–3; Buglak et al., 2018, pp. 11–12). Furthermore, while the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory mechanisms of DMF are well supported by in vitro data and animal studies, there is still limited clinical data on its efficacy in ocular disease settings. Early-phase clinical trials aimed at evaluating DMF in geographic atrophy have to rigorously assess both structural outcomes (e.g., changes in the area of atrophy) and functional endpoints (e.g., visual acuity, retinal sensitivity) to establish its therapeutic benefit in dry AMD (Buglak et al., 2018, pp. 11–12; Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 2–3).

Despite these limitations, the strengths of DMF are compelling. The drug’s ability to upregulate a broad spectrum of cytoprotective genes via the Nrf2 pathway makes it a versatile candidate for mitigating the oxidative and inflammatory stress that drives RPE degeneration. The fact that DMF has already cleared extensive regulatory and clinical safety evaluations in nonocular indications is a significant advantage, as it greatly accelerates the translational pathway compared to a novel chemical entity (Foresti, 2014, pp. 102–106; Cuadrado et al., 2019, pp. 21–25). Additionally, the repurposing of an already approved drug leverages existing manufacturing, formulation, and distribution infrastructure, favorably impacting the cost and timeline associated with drug development (Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 2–3; Shimizu et al., 2022, pp. 1–2).

In summary, the comprehensive literature review supports the potential of DMF to serve as a promising therapeutic candidate for dry AMD. Its mechanism of action—centered on Nrf2 activation leading to enhanced antioxidant capacity along with anti-inflammatory effects—directly addresses the key pathophysiological mechanisms implicated in dry AMD. The extensive clinical experience with DMF in MS and psoriasis provides a reliable safety and tolerability framework, which is crucial for rapid repositioning into a new clinical area such as geographic atrophy (Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 2–3; Shimizu et al., 2022, pp. 1–2). However, it is essential to conduct targeted preclinical studies and early-phase clinical trials specifically in ocular disease models to optimize dosing, confirm retinal delivery, and demonstrate clinical efficacy before advancing to larger randomized controlled trials (Buglak et al., 2018, pp. 11–12; Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 2–3). Overall, while challenges remain regarding optimal application in the retinal context and potential side effects in an older patient population, DMF—by virtue of its dual antioxidant and anti-inflammatory mechanisms and robust clinical background—stands as a viable candidate that merits further investigation for the treatment of dry AMD.

Given the current evidence, the following recommendations are proposed for advancing DMF as a candidate for dry AMD:  

1. Conduct detailed pharmacokinetic studies in animal models to assess the retinal bioavailability of DMF and its active metabolite MMF following systemic administration. This should include studies comparing different dosing schedules and formulations to optimize retinal exposure without exacerbating systemic adverse effects (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2022; Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 1–2).  

2. Develop and validate preclinical models of dry AMD that recapitulate the key features of human geographic atrophy, with a focus on evaluating the efficacy of DMF in protecting RPE cells against oxidative stress and preserving mitochondrial function (Marchesi et al., 2024, pp. 19–20; Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 2–3).  

3. Investigate the potential benefits of combining DMF with other therapeutic agents that target complementary pathways in dry AMD. For example, agents that modulate complement activation or improve choroidal blood flow may synergize with the Nrf2-mediated antioxidant mechanism of DMF (Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 2–3; Shu et al., 2022, pp. 2–3).  

4. Initiate early-phase clinical trials in patients with geographic atrophy to establish proof of concept. These studies should include robust biomarker assessments (e.g., measurement of ARE gene expression, retinal imaging for atrophy progression, and functional visual outcomes) to correlate molecular effects with clinical benefit (Buglak et al., 2018, pp. 11–12; Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 2–3).  

5. Carefully monitor the safety profile of DMF in the elderly population, as the incidence of gastrointestinal side effects and lymphopenia may differ compared to the population with MS. Consider the feasibility of modified-release formulations or alternative routes of administration (e.g., topical ocular delivery) to minimize systemic side effects while maximizing retinal drug concentrations (Camelo, 2025, pp. 11–13; Buonfiglio et al., 2024, pp. 17–18).

Overall, the repurposing of dimethyl fumarate for dry age-related macular degeneration is supported by a robust mechanistic framework and a significant body of preclinical and clinical data generated in other indications. Its capacity to activate the Nrf2 pathway, enhance mitochondrial glutathione synthesis, and modulate inflammatory responses in RPE cells directly addresses several pivotal aspects of dry AMD pathogenesis (Cuadrado et al., 2019, pp. 21–25; Foresti, 2014, pp. 102–106; Shu et al., 2022, pp. 1–2). While there are challenges regarding optimal retinal delivery and management of systemic adverse effects, the clinical experience with DMF in MS and psoriasis lends considerable support to its translational potential. Consequently, dimethyl fumarate represents a well‐justified and attractive therapeutic candidate for further exploration in the treatment of dry AMD, warranting additional preclinical optimization and clinical investigation to fully define its role in preserving visual function in patients affected by this progressive degenerative disease (Manai, Govoni, & Amadio, 2022, pp. 2–3; Buglak et al., 2018, pp. 11–12).
