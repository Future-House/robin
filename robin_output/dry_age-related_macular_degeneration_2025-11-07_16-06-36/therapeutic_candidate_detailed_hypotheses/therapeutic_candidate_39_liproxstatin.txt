Proposal for Liproxstatin-1 (Selleckchem S7692)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Liproxstatin-1 is a synthetic small-molecule inhibitor of ferroptosis that was originally discovered using phenotypic high-throughput screening in systems where ferroptosis was induced by either genetic deletion of GPX4 or by glutathione depletion with agents like erastin (Shah, Margison, & Pratt, 2017, pp. 4–7; Shah, 2019, pp. 66–69). Its chemical identity is tied to a unique spiroquinoxalinamine scaffold, which places it in the class of aromatic amine radical-trapping antioxidants (RTAs) – compounds well known in industrial settings to protect hydrocarbon products from autoxidation, as well as in biomedical research to block oxidative lipid chain reactions (Scarpellini, 2023, pp. 41–45; Angeli, Shah, Pratt, & Conrad, 2017, pp. 6–8). In contrast to classical phenolic antioxidants such as α-tocopherol, Liproxstatin-1 is designed to operate in the lipid bilayer, where its aminic (or diarylamine) moieties are less hindered by nonproductive hydrogen-bonding interactions with phospholipid headgroups, thus allowing rapid hydrogen-atom transfer to lipid peroxyl radicals (Conrad & Pratt, 2019, pp. 5–6; Shah, 2019, pp. 207–210). Although detailed synthetic routes are not provided in the available reports, the discovery process clearly identifies Liproxstatin-1 as a lead candidate that emerged from unbiased cell-based screens aimed at identifying compounds that preserve cell viability in the absence of GPX4 function (Shah, Margison, & Pratt, 2017, pp. 4–7; Shah, 2019, pp. 207–210). Its categorization as an RTA is significant because this class of compounds has a long history of being used to inhibit free radical–mediated damage in both industrial and biological contexts (Angeli et al., 2017, pp. 6–8; Conrad & Pratt, 2019, pp. 5–6).

Therapeutic History:  
The preclinical history of Liproxstatin-1 is rich with evidence supporting its role as a potent inhibitor of ferroptosis. Early studies demonstrated that Liproxstatin-1 acts at low nanomolar concentrations to prevent ferroptotic cell death in mouse embryonic fibroblasts as well as in specialized cells rendered vulnerable by GPX4 inactivation (Shah, Margison, & Pratt, 2017, pp. 4–7; Angeli et al., 2014, p. 10). Beyond in vitro cell survival assays, Liproxstatin-1 has been evaluated in vivo where it has shown remarkable protective effects in models of acute tissue injury including genetic GPX4 deficiency, which induces acute renal failure, and in ischaemia/reperfusion models of liver injury (Angeli et al., 2014, pp. 9–10). Although its direct clinical use in dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) has not been established – with most in vivo data coming from non-ocular models – its success in ameliorating tissue damage by inhibiting lipid peroxidation suggests its potential utility in other degenerative pathologies where oxidative membrane damage drives dysfunction, such as in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) degeneration underlying geographic atrophy in dry AMD (Shah, 2019, pp. 66–69; Zhao et al., 2025, pp. 20–21). Moreover, related compounds, notably Ferrostatin-1, have been tested in various retinal cell models and in some in vivo settings pertaining to retinal ischemia–reperfusion injury and oxygen-induced retinopathy, further bolstering the therapeutic concept of using RTA-type ferroptosis inhibitors in ocular settings (Zhao et al., 2025, pp. 21–23; Angeli et al., 2017, pp. 6–8). While the bulk of Liproxstatin-1’s therapeutic history centers on its role as an anti-ferroptotic agent in cancer, neurodegeneration, and acute organ failure, its mechanistic overlap with key pathological features observed in dry AMD – such as iron accumulation, glutathione depletion and elevated lipid peroxidation in the RPE – supports evaluating it as a repurposed candidate in this context (Angeli et al., 2014, pp. 9–10; Shen et al., 2025, pp. 1–2).

Mechanism of Action:  
The molecular mechanism by which Liproxstatin-1 exerts its cytoprotective effect is rooted in its function as a radical-trapping antioxidant. Specifically, Liproxstatin-1 reacts rapidly with lipid peroxyl radicals that propagate the autoxidation chain reaction in cellular membranes. Experimental data have established that Liproxstatin-1 exhibits an intrinsic reaction rate constant (k_inh) in the range of approximately 2.4 × 10^5 M^−1 s^−1 in organic solvents such as chlorobenzene at 37 °C, which is comparable to industrial diarylamine antioxidants (Shah, 2019, pp. 207–210; Zilka et al., 2017, pp. 8–9). Although its intrinsic reactivity in homogeneous organic solutions may appear lower than classical phenolic antioxidants like α-tocopherol, its performance in the lipid bilayer is superior due to reduced hydrogen-bonding interactions with phospholipid headgroups – a feature that enhances its mobility and reactivity in the lipid environment where ferroptosis is executed (Zilka et al., 2017, pp. 8–9; Conrad & Pratt, 2019, pp. 5–6). The compound functions primarily by donating a hydrogen atom from its arylamine (or diarylamine) N–H group, effectively quenching chain-propagating lipid peroxyl radicals before they can perpetuate lipid hydroperoxide formation (Shah, 2019, pp. 207–210; Conrad & Pratt, 2019, pp. 5–6). Kinetic isotope effect studies and computational analyses have confirmed that the N–H bond dissociation enthalpy is sufficiently low in Liproxstatin-1 to enable rapid hydrogen-atom transfer, which is a critical step in its radical-trapping mechanism (Zilka et al., 2017, pp. 2–3, 5–7). Importantly, Liproxstatin-1 does not exert significant inhibitory effects on enzymes such as human 15-lipoxygenase-1, thereby ruling out an enzymatic inhibition mechanism and reinforcing that its protective effect is solely attributable to non-enzymatic scavenging of peroxyl radicals (Shah, Margison, & Pratt, 2017, pp. 4–7; Shah, 2019, pp. 201–210). Recent studies have even suggested that Liproxstatin-1 may form a complex with iron in certain cellular compartments (e.g., the lysosome), which could further diminish iron-catalysed radical generation under acidic conditions; however, the predominant and well-characterized mechanism remains its role in intercepting lipid peroxyl radicals (Rodriguez et al., 2024, pp. 1–5; Zilka et al., 2017, pp. 1–2). On a molecular level, by halting the autoxidation chain reaction, Liproxstatin-1 preserves mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) and prevents the deleterious accumulation of lipid hydroperoxides, events that are central to the execution phase of ferroptosis (Shah, 2019, pp. 207–210; Conrad & Pratt, 2019, pp. 5–6). Thus, by protecting cellular membranes from oxidative deterioration, Liproxstatin-1 interrupts the cascade of events leading to ferroptotic cell death, a process that is implicated in RPE degeneration seen in geographic atrophy (Shah, Margison, & Pratt, 2017, pp. 4–7; Shen et al., 2025, pp. 1–2).

Expected Effect:  
In the context of dry AMD, particularly in geographic atrophy where RPE degeneration is a hallmark, the expected effect of Liproxstatin-1 would be to curtail the chain reaction of lipid peroxidation that underpins ferroptotic cell death. In vitro assays using RPE cell lines such as ARPE-19 or primary human RPE cells have demonstrated that targeting lipid peroxidation – as evidenced by reductions in markers like BODIPY-C11 oxidation and ACSL4 expression – can rescue cells from oxidative stress–mediated death (Zhao et al., 2025, pp. 20–23). Liproxstatin-1 is anticipated to mitigate the accumulation of lipid hydroperoxides, thus preserving the integrity of cellular membranes and mitochondrial function in the RPE (Shah, 2019, pp. 207–210; Zilka et al., 2017, pp. 1–2). Given that geographic atrophy in dry AMD is associated with iron overload and subsequent iron-catalysed lipid peroxidation, the ability of Liproxstatin-1 to trap peroxyl radicals is particularly significant; by reducing lipid ROS, it is expected to prevent the conversion of polyunsaturated fatty acids into cytotoxic lipid hydroperoxides, thereby maintaining cellular viability (Shah, 2019, pp. 207–210; Conrad & Pratt, 2019, pp. 5–6). Moreover, the inhibition of ACSL4-dependent peroxidation pathways – which have been linked to ferroptosis in RPE cells – may also be intercepted by Liproxstatin-1, providing a dual line of defense against ferroptotic signaling (Zhao et al., 2025, pp. 21–23; Angeli et al., 2017, pp. 6–8). On a cellular level, the compound is expected to maintain Δψm and reduce indicators of oxidative stress, as previously demonstrated in models addressing GPX4 inhibition (Angeli et al., 2014, pp. 9–10; Shah, 2019, pp. 207–210). In animal models where ferroptosis contributes to tissue degeneration (such as renal or hepatic ischaemia/reperfusion models), treatment with Liproxstatin-1 resulted in reduced TUNEL-positive cell counts and ameliorated tissue injury; these outcomes provide a rationale for its application in preclinical dry AMD models, where similar pathological processes – including mitochondrial dysfunction, iron dysregulation, and excessive lipid peroxidation – are observed (Angeli et al., 2014, pp. 9–10; Peng et al., 2020, pp. 3–5). In essence, when applied to RPE cells under conditions that mimic dry AMD, Liproxstatin-1 should decrease oxidative markers, prevent cell death, and thereby slow or halt the progression of geographic atrophy by restoring the balance between pro-oxidant and antioxidant pathways (Zhao et al., 2025, pp. 20–23).

Overall Evaluation:  
Liproxstatin-1 stands out as a promising candidate for repurposing as a therapeutic agent against dry AMD, particularly in geographic atrophy, given the mechanistic overlap between ferroptosis and RPE cell death observed in dry AMD. One of its principal strengths lies in its potent radical-trapping antioxidant activity, which is not only effective at low nanomolar concentrations but also uniquely suited to the lipid bilayer environment of the RPE (Shah, Margison, & Pratt, 2017, pp. 4–7; Shah, 2019, pp. 207–210). This specificity is critical because retinal cells are subject to a high burden of polyunsaturated lipid content and are therefore particularly sensitive to oxidative damage. The fact that Liproxstatin-1 has demonstrated efficacy in protecting cells under ferroptotic stress in other organ systems – including kidney and liver models – further supports its potential viability in ocular applications (Angeli et al., 2014, pp. 9–10; Peng et al., 2020, pp. 3–5). Additionally, the compound’s ability to reduce lipid peroxidation, maintain mitochondrial membrane potential, and possibly engage in secondary iron-inactivating mechanisms (as suggested by lysosomal localization studies) offers multiple avenues through which it may address the multifactorial pathogenesis of dry AMD (Rodriguez et al., 2024, pp. 1–5; Zilka et al., 2017, pp. 1–2).

However, several challenges must be considered. Despite the strong preclinical evidence in non-ocular systems, direct evidence of Liproxstatin-1’s efficacy in models of dry AMD or RPE-specific ferroptosis is still limited relative to other compounds such as Ferrostatin-1 or nanoparticle-delivered ferroptosis inhibitors in retinal models (Zhao et al., 2025, pp. 20–23). This gap in ocular preclinical data implies that further dedicated studies using RPE cell models and in vivo dry AMD animal models are necessary to validate its protective effects in the eye. In addition, drug delivery to the retina poses its own set of challenges – the blood-retinal barrier and the unique pharmacokinetic profile required for intravitreal stability must be thoroughly optimized (Shah, 2019, pp. 66–69; Hao, Xu, Zhang, & Xue, 2024, pp. 6–7). Moreover, dry AMD is a multifactorial disease involving not only ferroptosis but also apoptosis, inflammation, and other forms of regulated cell death; therefore, while inhibiting ferroptosis may be beneficial, it is uncertain whether monotherapy with Liproxstatin-1 would be sufficient to arrest disease progression. There is also the matter of safety and tolerability in the context of the ocular environment, as systemic dosing strategies that work in renal or hepatic models may not directly translate to the delicate milieu of retinal tissues (Angeli et al., 2014, pp. 9–10; Shah, 2019, pp. 207–210). Finally, considerations regarding the long-term stability, potential off-target effects, and metabolic fate of Liproxstatin-1 in the eye will be crucial parameters in the drug development process.

In summary, the collective literature indicates that Liproxstatin-1 has a well-defined mechanism as a radical-trapping antioxidant that effectively blocks lipid peroxidation and, by extension, ferroptosis. Its discovery through unbiased screening in ferroptosis-susceptible models and its demonstration of efficacy in multiple organ systems provide a strong foundation for its repurposing in dry AMD. The evidence supports that Liproxstatin-1 could counteract the iron-driven ROS amplification and lipid peroxidation that contribute to RPE cell death in geographic atrophy. Nonetheless, while its mechanistic rationale and preclinical promise are substantial, further research – particularly within retinal models – is necessary to fully evaluate its therapeutic potential and address challenges related to ocular delivery and multi-pathway disease components. Taken together, Liproxstatin-1 represents a compelling candidate for further investigation as a targeted inhibitor of ferroptosis in the treatment of dry AMD (Shah, Margison, & Pratt, 2017, pp. 4–7; Shah, 2019, pp. 66–69, 207–210; Angeli et al., 2014, pp. 9–10; Zhao et al., 2025, pp. 21–23).
