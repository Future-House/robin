Proposal for Beta-hydroxybutyrate (BHB; Sigma-Aldrich 389410)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Beta-hydroxybutyrate (BHB) is an endogenous ketone body produced by mitochondrial ketogenesis from fatty-acid–derived acetyl-CoA, classically in hepatocytes but also in extrahepatic cell types including the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and several neuronal lineages (enzymatic pathway: ACAT, HMGCS2, HMGCL, and BDH1) (Wang et al., 2021; Gough, 2021). In physiological and nutritional ketosis, circulating BHB rises from baseline (~0.05–0.1 mM) to levels >0.5 mM and up to 5–7 mM with prolonged fasting, providing an alternative oxidative substrate for extrahepatic mitochondria when carbohydrate availability is limited (Wang et al., 2021; Cooper et al., 2023). BHB and acetoacetate are transported into and out of cells via proton-linked monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs) such as MCT1 and MCT2 that are expressed at the blood–retinal barrier and in retinal cells, enabling substrate entry into the retina (Yang, 2021; Tribble et al., 2021; Gough, 2021). RPEs robustly express mitochondrial HMGCS2 and can ketogenize palmitate to BHB, secreting BHB via MCT1; retinal cells can take up and metabolize BHB into Krebs-cycle intermediates and amino acids, indicating functional capacity to use BHB as mitochondrial fuel in the outer retina (Yang, 2021). More recent mass spectrometry–based reassessment shows that RPE secretion of BHB is substantially lower than previously estimated by enzymatic kits, that RPE exports BHB to both apical and basal compartments, and that RPE both produces and consumes BHB depending on nutrient state—important considerations for experimental design and therapeutic targeting (Gulette et al., 2024).  

BHB belongs to the class of endogenous metabolic intermediates that function dually as oxidative fuels (via oxidation to acetyl-CoA and entry into TCA cycle) and signaling molecules with paracrine/autocrine receptor and epigenetic actions (e.g., agonism at HCAR2/GPR109A and inhibition of class I/IIa histone deacetylases) (Wang et al., 2021; Yang, 2021; Kovács et al., 2021). As a therapeutic modality, BHB has been delivered exogenously as salts/esters or induced endogenously through ketogenic diets, fasting, or exercise to support mitochondrial ATP production and alter cellular stress responses in the CNS, heart, and skeletal muscle (Cooper et al., 2023; Kovács et al., 2021; Parker et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2022). In retinal disease models, ketogenic interventions or exogenous BHB have reduced oxidative/inflammatory injury and improved functional measures, though direct application to dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) has not yet been clinically tested (Gough, 2021; Tribble et al., 2021).  

Therapeutic History  
Ketone biology has a long history in neurology and cardiometabolic research, where BHB has been shown to preserve mitochondrial function, increase ATP production, and blunt oxidative injury in ischemia, neurodegeneration models, and skeletal muscle stress paradigms (Kovács et al., 2021; Mierziak et al., 2021; Parker et al., 2018). In neural tissues, ketogenic interventions and exogenous ketones prevent synaptic dysfunction induced by respiratory complex inhibitors and increase the brain NAD⁺/NADH ratio, correlating with decreased mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation (Morris et al., 2020). In rat skeletal muscle cells, 5 mM BHB for 24–48 h increased respiratory control, ATP-linked respiration, P:O efficiency, lowered H₂O₂ production, and shifted mitochondrial dynamics toward fusion via DRP1 suppression—consistent with enhanced bioenergetics and decreased oxidative stress (Parker et al., 2018). In primary neurons, BHB maintained oxidative phosphorylation under glutamate workload even when cytosolic NADH transfer via the malate–aspartate shuttle was inhibited, demonstrating that BHB can sustain respiration under conditions that impair glycolytic NADH coupling to mitochondria (Llorente-Folch et al., 2023).  

In the retina and optic nerve, multiple preclinical models show that ketogenic exposures or direct ketone administration reduce oxidative stress and inflammation, preserve function, and limit neuronal death. Intraperitoneal BHB and acetoacetate reduced NMDA-induced retinal ganglion cell (RGC) death in rats, and ketogenic diet attenuated NMDA-induced RGC injury in juvenile rats (Gough, 2021). In ocular blast or optic neuropathy paradigms, ketogenic diet prevented increases in superoxide and IL-1α, preserved visual evoked potentials, and reduced astrocytic hypertrophy and optic nerve death (Gough, 2021). In glaucoma models, ketogenic diet improved RGC soma/axon survival, axonal transport, and reduced gliosis, with increases in NAD and NAD:NADH ratio and upregulation of MCT1/MCT2 in optic nerve (Tribble et al., 2021). AAV-mediated MCT2 overexpression (increasing uptake of lactate/pyruvate/ketones) preserved RGCs and optic nerve axons in ocular hypertension and glaucoma models and elevated markers of mitochondrial biogenesis and function (PGC1α, COX, SDH) and antioxidant defense (SOD2) (Gough, 2021). However, in at least one RGC injury model, daily intraperitoneal BHB or acetoacetate did not replicate ketogenic diet neuroprotection, underscoring differences between exogenous ketone dosing and the systemic/multifactorial effects of ketogenic diets (Tribble et al., 2021).  

Within the RPE compartment specifically, two lines of evidence are relevant: (1) BHB protects ARPE-19 cells from high-glucose–induced injury, improving viability, alleviating cell cycle arrest, and modulating inflammatory mediators (IL-8, IL-17α, MCP-1) at millimolar concentrations achievable with nutritional ketosis or exogenous supplementation (Argentino et al., 2025). (2) HCAR2/GPR109A, a BHB receptor, is robustly expressed by the RPE; BHB agonism reduced retinal leukocyte infiltration in LPS-challenged mice in an HCAR2-dependent manner, and loss of HCAR2 in RPE led to barrier dysfunction (ZO-1 defects) and increased immune cell infiltrates, indicating that BHB–HCAR2 signaling can preserve an immunoinhibitory retinal environment (Powell et al., 2021). In diabetic retinopathy models, systemic BHB activation of HCA2 reduced ER stress and NLRP3 inflammasome signaling and ameliorated retinal pathology, establishing anti-inflammatory and ER stress–limiting actions in retina (Trotta et al., 2019).  

In inherited retinal degeneration models, ketogenic interventions delayed photoreceptor degeneration and improved electroretinogram (ERG) amplitudes and outer nuclear layer (ONL) thickness in rd10 and Pde6a mice, and a soluble ketogenic formulation partially restored vision and survival in PDH-deficient noir zebrafish—consistent with bypassing PDH to supply acetyl-CoA and support neurotransmitter synthesis (Gough, 2021). Notwithstanding these encouraging signals, the literature consistently notes the absence of clinical trials evaluating ketogenic interventions or BHB in retinitis pigmentosa or AMD patients, and reports potentially adverse effects of ketogenic diets in wild-type mice (decreased ERG amplitudes, altered albumin/weight), highlighting translational uncertainties and the need to distinguish effects of BHB from the broader systemic consequences of diet (Gough, 2021).  

Mechanism of Action  
BHB acts through convergent metabolic, redox, mitochondrial, inflammatory, and epigenetic mechanisms that are mechanistically aligned with the pathophysiology of RPE mitochondrial dysfunction and chronic oxidative/inflammatory stress in dry AMD.  

- Alternative mitochondrial fuel and bioenergetics support. In extrahepatic tissues (including retina), BHB oxidation proceeds via mitochondrial BDH1 (βHB→acetoacetate) followed by SCOT/OXCT1 (AcAc→AcAc-CoA using succinyl-CoA) and acetyl-CoA production for TCA entry and ATP generation, enabling substrate-level maintenance of mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) and oxidative phosphorylation under glucose restriction or impaired glycolytic NADH transfer (Wang et al., 2021; Yang, 2021; Llorente-Folch et al., 2023). The retina expresses monocarboxylate transport routes (e.g., MCT1 at the inner blood–retinal barrier; MCT2 in neurons), and RPE expresses MCT1 and can both produce and consume BHB, providing a plausible uptake pathway for exogenous BHB into RPE and neighboring retinal cells (Tribble et al., 2021; Yang, 2021; Gulette et al., 2024). In neurons, BHB-supported respiration remains robust when the malate–aspartate shuttle is blocked with AOAA—conditions that decrease glucose-fueled ATP synthesis—indicating that BHB can preserve mitochondrial ATP synthesis and respiratory control when cytosolic NADH transfer is compromised, a scenario relevant to glycolysis-impaired RPE (Llorente-Folch et al., 2023).  

- Redox coupling and ROS control. The interconversion of acetoacetate and BHB by βHB dehydrogenase is linked to NADH/NAD⁺ redox; acetoacetate→BHB requires oxidation of NADH to NAD⁺, and nutritional ketosis in human brain increases NAD⁺/NADH ratio, correlating with reductions in mitochondrial ROS generation (Gough, 2021; Morris et al., 2020). Ketones can inhibit mitochondrial ROS production by increasing NADH oxidation, and ketogenic interventions increase NAD⁺/NADH in vivo, though tissue- and context-dependent effects exist (Morris et al., 2020). Complementary cardiac literature reports that β-OHB can reduce the mitochondrial NAD couple while oxidizing coenzyme Q, emphasizing that BHB modulates both NADH/NAD⁺ and Q/QH₂ redox pairs in mitochondria—with directionality that may depend on prevailing substrate status and respiratory state (Chu et al., 2021). In ischemia–reperfusion models and muscle cells, BHB lowers mitochondrial ROS, decreases mitochondrial swelling, maintains Δψm, and increases ATP production, consistent with direct mitochondrial protection (Mierziak et al., 2021; Parker et al., 2018).  

- Antioxidant transcription (Nrf2) and hormesis. Ketogenic exposures can induce a hormetic redox signal that activates Keap1–Nrf2, increasing transcription of detoxification and antioxidant genes; in retina, ketone body exposure mimicked calorie restriction via fumarate–Nrf2 activation (Gough, 2021; Fu et al., 2020). Preclinical optic/retinal models link these antioxidant transcriptional effects to preserved function and reduced oxidative markers (NOS, MDA, superoxide) (Gough, 2021).  

- Anti-inflammatory signaling via HCAR2/ARRB2 and NLRP3/NF-κB suppression. BHB is a ligand for HCAR1/HCAR2 receptors; HCAR2 is robustly expressed in human RPE and retina, and receptor activation by BHB reduces leukocyte infiltration and preserves RPE barrier integrity in inflammation models (Powell et al., 2021). Downstream, BHB–HCAR signaling recruits β-arrestin-2 (ARRB2) that binds NLRP3 to block inflammasome assembly, decreasing ASC and preventing K⁺ efflux to suppress IL-1β maturation; BHB also activates a COX1/PGD2 cascade that inhibits IKK and NF-κB nuclear translocation, reducing TNF-α and microglial/macrophage activation (Gough, 2021). In diabetic retina, systemic BHB–HCA2 activation suppressed ER stress and NLRP3 inflammasome activity, demonstrating the retinal anti-inflammatory efficacy of this axis (Trotta et al., 2019).  

- Mitochondrial calcium homeostasis and mPTP stabilization. In human iPSC-derived microglia, amyloid-β oligomers increased MCU expression, raised mitochondrial Ca²⁺ ~40%, elevated mtROS, and triggered mPTP opening; BHB normalized MCU, decreased mtCa²⁺/mtROS, and completely reversed mPTP activation, restoring phagocytic function (Jin et al., 2023). Although not conducted in RPE, these mitochondrial-stabilizing effects are mechanistically relevant to post-mitotic cells with high oxidative load.  

- Epigenetic modulation and mitochondrial biogenesis. BHB is an endogenous inhibitor of class I/IIa HDACs at physiological millimolar concentrations, leading to histone hyperacetylation and upregulation of FOXO3a and metallothionein genes that encode antioxidant defenses; BHB also increases intracellular acetyl-CoA and supports protein acetylation, and can β-hydroxybutyrylate histones, adding layers of gene regulatory control tied to mitochondrial status (Yang, 2021; Kovács et al., 2021; Mierziak et al., 2021). Through HDAC inhibition and receptor-mediated signaling, BHB increases BDNF via cAMP/PKA/CREB, activates SIRT1/SIRT3 via AMPK/HCA2/NAD⁺ pathways, and promotes PGC1α-driven mitochondrial biogenesis and UCP2 expression that collectively improve mitochondrial function and antioxidant capacity (Kovács et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022). In retinal/optic nerve models, ketogenic diet or enhanced monocarboxylate uptake upregulated PGC1α, COX, SDH, and SOD2 while lowering pAMPK/AMPK, linking substrate availability to mitochondrial biogenesis and anti-inflammatory signaling (Gough, 2021).  

- Immunometabolic reprogramming in retina/CNS. Ketogenic interventions increased anti-inflammatory mediators (IL-4, Arginase-1) and M2-like microglia while decreasing IBA-1⁺ macrophage/microglia, consistent with dampened neuroinflammation secondary to improved bioenergetics (reduced AMPK activation) and prevention of NF-κB p65 nuclear translocation (Gough, 2021). BHB also inhibits FFAR3/GPR41, reducing sympathetic activity in mice, indicating broader metabolic signaling roles that could modulate retinal perfusion/metabolism indirectly (Gough, 2021).  

Collectively, these mechanisms—fuel substitution that sustains Δψm and ATP synthesis under glycolytic impairment, improved redox balance with decreased mitochondrial ROS, Nrf2-dependent antioxidant gene induction, NLRP3/NF-κB anti-inflammatory actions via HCAR2/ARRB2, stabilization of mitochondrial Ca²⁺/mPTP, and epigenetic programs that support mitochondrial biogenesis—map directly onto pathologies implicated in RPE degeneration in dry AMD and provide multiple rescue nodes testable in disease-relevant models (Wang et al., 2021; Morris et al., 2020; Gough, 2021; Powell et al., 2021).  

Expected Effect  
Assay context: The working hypothesis is that exogenous BHB will serve as an alternative mitochondrial substrate in RPE, sustaining Δψm and reducing NADH-driven mitochondrial ROS when glycolysis is impaired—conditions that model bioenergetic stress in geographic atrophy. Mechanistically, this is supported by three converging evidence streams: (1) mitochondrial fuel flexibility to oxidize BHB to acetyl-CoA via BDH1/SCOT, (2) redox modulation that increases NADH oxidation and elevates the NAD⁺/NADH ratio, and (3) multi-layered antioxidant/anti-inflammatory signaling that lowers mitochondrial ROS production and downstream inflammasome/NF-κB activation (Wang et al., 2021; Morris et al., 2020; Gough, 2021).  

- Sustained Δψm and ATP under glycolytic impairment. RPE expresses ketogenesis machinery (HMGCS2) and monocarboxylate transport capacity (MCT1) and is capable of both producing and consuming BHB, while inner BRB MCT1 and neuronal MCT2 provide routes for substrate delivery into retinal tissue (Yang, 2021; Tribble et al., 2021; Gulette et al., 2024). In neurons, BHB preserved oxidative phosphorylation and ATP-linked respiration under MAS inhibition, a condition that effectively reduces mitochondrial access to cytosolic NADH from glycolysis; this predicts that, in RPE exposed to glycolytic blockade or high work demand, BHB could maintain Δψm and respiratory control by supplying acetyl-CoA independent of cytosolic NADH shuttling (Llorente-Folch et al., 2023). In muscle and cardiac models, BHB improved respiratory control ratios, increased ATP production, and maintained Δψm under stress, providing precedent for Δψm preservation in high-energy post-mitotic cells (Parker et al., 2018; Mierziak et al., 2021).  

- Lowered NADH-driven mtROS and improved redox balance. Nutritional ketosis increases the brain NAD⁺/NADH ratio and ketones inhibit mitochondrial ROS production by increasing NADH oxidation, a shift that is expected to suppress reverse electron transport and semiquinone leak at respiratory complexes under high NADH load (Morris et al., 2020). Although some cardiac literature reports context-dependent changes in the mitochondrial NAD couple under BHB with concurrent oxidation of coenzyme Q, the net effect across neural and muscle studies is reduced mitochondrial ROS generation and improved redox handling, a phenotype also observed in optic/retinal models after ketogenic interventions (Chu et al., 2021; Gough, 2021; Mierziak et al., 2021). These redox improvements are reinforced by Nrf2 activation and increased expression of antioxidant defenses (e.g., SOD2), which increase ROS quenching capacity in retinal and optic tissues (Gough, 2021).  

- Anti-inflammatory and barrier-preserving actions in RPE. BHB agonizes HCAR2 expressed by RPE and retinal microglia, reducing leukocyte infiltration, preserving tight junction integrity (ZO-1), and suppressing inflammasome/NF-κB signaling (Powell et al., 2021; Trotta et al., 2019). In hyperglycemic ARPE-19, BHB improved viability and cell cycle progression and modulated inflammatory cytokines, consistent with protection under metabolic stress (Argentino et al., 2025). By preserving ATP and lowering AMPK phosphorylation, BHB/ketone availability is expected to block NF-κB p65 nuclear translocation and downstream expression of IL-6 and TNF-α, reducing the chronic para-inflammatory milieu implicated in RPE degeneration (Gough, 2021).  

- Translational considerations for exposure and transport. Systemically delivered BHB can traverse the blood–retinal barrier via MCT1, and RPE’s own MCT1 provides transmembrane transport capacity; nonetheless, quantitative extracellular BHB at RPE surfaces is likely in the low micromolar range under basal conditions, and RPE produces and consumes BHB dynamically, implying that achieving therapeutic mitochondrial utilization in RPE will require millimolar plasma BHB or local delivery (Tribble et al., 2021; Gulette et al., 2024). Nutritional ketosis and exogenous ketone supplementation readily achieve 0.5–2 mM BHB in plasma in rodent and human studies, which are the concentrations used in many in vitro protection assays (Cooper et al., 2023; Argentino et al., 2025).  

Key readouts and validation metrics for the proposed assay should include Δψm (e.g., TMRM/TMRE), OCR/ECAR profiling and ATP-linked respiration (Seahorse), mitochondrial ROS (MitoSOX and H₂O₂ flux), NAD⁺/NADH ratio (enzymatic cycling and/or fluorescence lifetime imaging), pAMPK/AMPK and NF-κB p65 nuclear localization, Nrf2 translocation and target gene induction (e.g., SOD2), and inflammasome readouts (ASC specks, IL-1β maturation) (Gough, 2021). Given the measurement pitfalls documented in RPE ketone assays, BHB should be quantified by LC–MS/MS or after removal of high-MW confounders by size exclusion rather than by standard enzymatic kits (Gulette et al., 2024).  
Protein expression considerations support the mechanism in the target cell type. HCAR2/GPR109A is expressed by RPE and retinal microglia/endothelium and mediates anti-inflammatory effects of BHB in vivo, making receptor-dependent components of BHB action directly testable in RPE (Powell et al., 2021). RPE expresses HMGCS2 and MCT1, and retinal cells metabolize BHB into TCA intermediates, consistent with BDH1/SCOT/OXCT1 pathway activity enabling BHB oxidation in retinal tissues; while direct SCOT expression in RPE is not provided here, the metabolic tracing data indicating conversion of BHB to Krebs intermediates in retinal cells justify testing oxidative utilization in RPE (Yang, 2021; Llorente-Folch et al., 2023).  

Overall Evaluation  
Strength of biological rationale. The core hypothesis—that BHB can act as an alternative mitochondrial fuel to sustain Δψm and lower NADH-driven mitochondrial ROS in glycolysis-impaired RPE—is strongly aligned with BHB’s established roles in extrahepatic tissues and is supported by multiple mechanistic strands in relevant neural and retinal systems (Wang et al., 2021; Morris et al., 2020; Llorente-Folch et al., 2023). BHB oxidation provides acetyl-CoA and maintains respiration when glycolytic NADH transfer is compromised, as demonstrated in primary neurons; BHB reduces mtROS, preserves Δψm, and increases ATP in ischemic muscle/heart models; and nutrient ketosis increases the NAD⁺/NADH ratio and lowers ROS production in brain—effects that would be expected to decrease reverse-electron transport–driven ROS at complex I and stabilize mitochondrial function in stressed RPE (Llorente-Folch et al., 2023; Mierziak et al., 2021; Morris et al., 2020). In retinal tissue, ketogenic interventions and direct ketone administration reduce oxidative injury, preserve function, and enhance mitochondrial biogenesis and antioxidant defenses in RGC/optic nerve models, while anti-inflammatory benefits via HCAR2—expressed by RPE—reduce leukocyte infiltration and preserve barrier integrity (Gough, 2021; Tribble et al., 2021; Powell et al., 2021; Trotta et al., 2019). The mitochondrial stabilization effects observed in human microglia—lower MCU/mtCa²⁺, reduced mtROS, prevention of mPTP opening—further support a BHB-mediated mitochondrial resilience phenotype that is likely relevant to RPE (Jin et al., 2023).  

Evidence relevant to the target tissue. RPEs possess ketone-related transport and enzymatic capacity (HMGCS2, MCT1) and can both produce and consume BHB; they are responsive to BHB in vitro under metabolic stress (hyperglycemia) with improved viability and inflammatory marker modulation (Yang, 2021; Argentino et al., 2025). HCAR2-mediated anti-inflammatory and barrier maintenance functions have been demonstrated in RPE/retina, providing a receptor-proximal pathway for BHB to suppress chronic para-inflammation and preserve RPE junctional integrity, both relevant to dry AMD pathobiology (Powell et al., 2021). While direct measurements of BDH1/SCOT activity and NAD⁺/NADH dynamics in human RPE treated with BHB remain to be shown here, the metabolic fate of BHB in retinal cells and robust mitochondrial effects in closely related cell types make the translational experiments technically feasible and mechanistically justified (Yang, 2021; Llorente-Folch et al., 2023).  

Translational gaps and risks. No clinical trials have evaluated ketogenic diets or BHB specifically for RP or AMD, and some ketogenic diet studies reported adverse outcomes in wild-type animals (decreased ERG amplitude, altered serum albumin/weight), emphasizing the need to decouple BHB’s effects from broader dietary/metabolic confounders (Gough, 2021). Direct BHB delivery does not always recapitulate ketogenic diet benefits in RGC injury, suggesting that multi-target effects of diet (e.g., NAD metabolism, mTOR, microbiome) contribute to efficacy; this implies that BHB monotherapy may require combination strategies (e.g., nicotinamide co-therapy) or optimized dosing to achieve comparable benefit (Tribble et al., 2021). Quantification of BHB in RPE experiments is non-trivial due to assay interference; LC–MS/MS or validated size-exclusion protocols must be used to avoid false positives and to measure physiologically relevant, often low-micromolar extracellular BHB in RPE cultures (Gulette et al., 2024). Finally, the directionality of NAD redox effects may be context dependent (reports of both increased NAD⁺/NADH ratio in brain and reduced mitochondrial NAD couple in cardiac models), requiring careful interpretation with compartment-specific assays in RPE (Morris et al., 2020; Chu et al., 2021).  

Development path and decision points. Given the strong mechanistic rationale, retina-focused preclinical evidence, and RPE receptor expression data, BHB is a plausible repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD that merits rigorous, RPE-centric validation. Key go/no-go preclinical criteria should include: (1) demonstration that BHB rescues Δψm and ATP-linked respiration in primary human RPE or iPSC-RPE under glycolytic impairment (e.g., 2-deoxyglucose or low glucose plus workload), (2) reduction of mitochondrial ROS (MitoSOX/H₂O₂ flux) and elevation of cellular NAD⁺/NADH ratio with BHB, (3) suppression of pAMPK and NF-κB p65 nuclear translocation, induction of Nrf2 target genes, and reduction of NLRP3 activation, (4) persistence of benefits under HCAR2 antagonism/knockdown to parse receptor versus fuel contributions, and (5) confirmation that effects are contingent on monocarboxylate transport (MCT inhibition) and SCOT activity (e.g., OXCT1 knockdown), which would mechanistically link outcomes to BHB oxidation rather than unrelated signaling (Llorente-Folch et al., 2023; Gough, 2021; Powell et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021). In vivo, a dry-AMD–relevant RPE degeneration model should test systemic or ocular BHB delivery (dose selection to 0.5–2 mM plasma equivalents) with readouts of RPE mitochondrial structure/function, subretinal inflammation, and lesion progression; blood–retina transport via MCT1 at the inner BRB suggests systemic exposure can reach the retina, though intraocular routes might achieve higher local concentrations (Tribble et al., 2021). Throughout, BHB dosing must be titrated to avoid off-target effects and electrolyte load associated with some salt formulations; direct ketone esters or controlled dietary induction may be considered for translational studies, recognizing model-specific differences seen between exogenous BHB and ketogenic diet (Tribble et al., 2021).  

Strengths  
- Mechanistic breadth and convergence on AMD-relevant pathways: alternative fuel supply to support Δψm/ATP, improved NAD redox and ROS control, Nrf2 antioxidant gene induction, and potent suppression of NLRP3/NF-κB–mediated para-inflammation via HCAR2—each independently linked to retinal/RPE resilience (Wang et al., 2021; Morris et al., 2020; Gough, 2021; Powell et al., 2021).  
- Retinal delivery/uptake plausibility: MCT expression at the inner BRB and in RPE, plus RPE’s ability to both produce and consume BHB, establish physiological routes for BHB transit and utilization in target tissues (Tribble et al., 2021; Yang, 2021; Gulette et al., 2024).  
- Preclinical efficacy signals in retinal and CNS models: reduced oxidative/inflammatory injury, preserved neuronal function, enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis/antioxidant defenses, and receptor-mediated anti-inflammatory actions in retina (Gough, 2021; Tribble et al., 2021; Trotta et al., 2019; Powell et al., 2021).  
- Compatibility with RPE stress biology: BHB improved ARPE-19 survival under metabolic stress and modulated cytokines, and microglial mitochondrial data demonstrate BHB reverses mtCa²⁺/mtROS/mPTP derangements that are mechanistically relevant to RPE degeneration (Argentino et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2023).  

Weaknesses and uncertainties  
- Lack of AMD-specific in vivo data and no clinical trials in AMD/RP; extrapolation from RGC/optic nerve/diabetic models requires confirmation in RPE-targeted systems (Gough, 2021).  
- Measurement pitfalls in RPE ketone studies; reliance on inaccurate enzymatic assays historically confounded interpretation, necessitating MS-based quantitation and careful time-course sampling (Gulette et al., 2024).  
- Context-dependent redox effects; reports differ on NAD couple responses to BHB, indicating the need for compartmentalized NAD⁺/NADH readouts in RPE to avoid misattribution (Morris et al., 2020; Chu et al., 2021).  
- Differences between exogenous BHB and ketogenic diet; some benefits in glaucoma were not reproduced with BHB delivery, suggesting that metabolic co-factors (e.g., NAD boosters) or combinatorial strategies may be required (Tribble et al., 2021).  
- Potential systemic/dietary adverse effects; ketogenic diets can reduce ERG amplitudes and alter systemic biomarkers in wild-type mice—although these are not necessarily attributable to BHB per se, they caution against non-specific dietary induction in frail patients (Gough, 2021).  

Overall, BHB (Sigma-Aldrich 389410) is a compelling repurposed candidate for dry AMD based on strong mechanistic plausibility and retina-relevant preclinical evidence, with direct RPE expression of the key anti-inflammatory receptor HCAR2 and demonstrable RPE responses to BHB under metabolic stress (Powell et al., 2021; Argentino et al., 2025). The major risk is the current absence of AMD-specific in vivo evidence and the historical conflation of diet-level ketosis with BHB pharmacology; these can be addressed by an RPE-centric preclinical plan that uses rigorous ketone quantitation, compartmental redox readouts, transporter/enzyme perturbations (MCT/OXCT1), and in vivo validation in RPE degeneration models. If BHB reproducibly maintains Δψm/ATP, reduces mtROS, and suppresses inflammasome/NF-κB signaling in RPE under glycolytic impairment, it would justify advancement to formulation and delivery optimization for ocular exposure and, subsequently, to early clinical safety/pharmacodynamic studies in dry AMD (Llorente-Folch et al., 2023; Gough, 2021; Wang et al., 2021).
