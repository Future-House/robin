Proposal for Urolithin A (Sigma-Aldrich SML1791)

1. Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Urolithin A (UA) is a naturally occurring polyphenolic metabolite that is generated in humans via the microbial transformation of dietary ellagitannins, which are abundant in pomegranates, berries, walnuts, and other fruits and nuts. UA belongs to the class of gut-derived α-benzocoumarin compounds and is produced through the specific metabolic activities of intestinal bacteria on ellagitannins and ellagic acid. The discovery of UA emerged from studies aimed at understanding the beneficial effects of diets rich in polyphenols on aging and cellular health, and its structure was elucidated as a 6H-dibenzopyran-6-one derivative. In general, compounds of this class are known for their anti-oxidative, anti-inflammatory, and pro-mitophagic properties. Over time, UA has been investigated increasingly for its ability to modulate mitochondrial function and cellular homeostasis, garnering interest in its potential applications beyond traditional nutritional supplementation. Its multifaceted biological activities have been documented in several preclinical studies where it enhanced mitochondrial quality control, improved muscle endurance in aging populations, and mitigated neurodegenerative processes by clearing dysfunctional mitochondria, lending support to its candidacy as a repurposed drug for chronic conditions such as dry age-related macular degeneration (Jayatunga et al., 2021; Makarov & Korkotian, 2023).  

Historically, naturally derived compounds with similar mechanisms of action have been used to address age-related cellular dysfunction. UA, however, stands out by virtue of its dual ability to invoke both mitophagy and lysophagy, which are critical for the removal of damaged organelles—key processes implicated in aging and neurodegeneration. This compound is not synthesized chemically in the traditional pharmaceutical sense; rather, its production relies on the interplay between dietary intake and the gut microbiota, reinforcing the concept that natural metabolic products can be harnessed as therapeutic agents. Its inclusion in the spectrum of therapeutics aimed at cellular rejuvenation has sparked significant interest, particularly because its widespread availability in the human diet and favorable safety profile, as observed in early clinical trials, set a promising foundation for further drug development (Maksimović et al., 2025; Liu, 2022).

2. Therapeutic History  
Urolithin A has been extensively studied in the context of aging and degenerative diseases, and its therapeutic history spans several biomedical, clinical, and biochemical investigations. Preclinical studies have shown that UA is effective in enhancing mitophagy, thereby promoting the clearance of damaged mitochondria. In animal models, UA supplementation has been correlated with improved mitochondrial function and increased muscle endurance, which has led to its exploration as an “anti-aging” agent. Notably, several clinical trials have confirmed its safety in elderly populations, with doses up to 2,500 mg/day being well tolerated and associated with improvements in mitochondrial biomarkers in skeletal muscle (Jayatunga et al., 2021; Liu, 2022).  

In the realm of neurodegeneration, UA has been recognized for its neuroprotective activities. Studies have demonstrated that UA can ameliorate cognitive impairment and reduce inflammatory markers, suggesting benefits in neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s diseases (Jayatunga et al., 2021; Fehér et al., 2022). Importantly, recent investigations have extended the focus to ocular health. Preclinical models of retinal degeneration—particularly those mimicking dry AMD—have emerged as critical systems in which UA’s multifactorial actions are being tested. In models employing sodium iodate (SI) to induce retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) damage, UA treatment resulted in enhanced RPE cell survival, restoration of autophagic flux, and preservation of visual function (Jiménez-Loygorri & Boya, 2024; Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024). These studies underscore UA’s potential in mitigating geographic atrophy, a defining feature of dry AMD.  

Beyond its direct mitochondrial effects, UA has been evaluated for its impact on lysosomal function. Some reports indicate that UA exerts potent neuroprotective benefits by promoting SQSTM1/p62-dependent lysophagy, leading to the clearance of damaged lysosomes and restoration of cellular proteostasis. This lysophagy mechanism appears to be crucial in maintaining retinal cellular homeostasis and preventing RPE atrophy, a process that is marked by an accumulation of dysfunctional organelles and consequent oxidative stress in AMD (Jiménez-Loygorri & Boya, 2024; Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024). Although direct clinical use of UA in the treatment of dry AMD or similar ocular diseases has not yet been fully realized, the wealth of preclinical evidence—particularly from relevant animal models—provides a strong rationale for its repurposing in this context. In summary, UA’s therapeutic history demonstrates its utility across multiple age-related pathologies, with growing evidence supporting its potential application in dry age-related macular degeneration (Jiménez-Loygorri & Boya, 2024; Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024).

3. Mechanism of Action  
The molecular mechanisms underpinning the actions of Urolithin A are multifaceted and involve intricate interactions across several cellular pathways. Central to UA’s mechanism is its ability to induce selective autophagy, a process that includes both mitophagy and lysophagy, leading to the clearance of damaged mitochondria and lysosomes, respectively. Under normal physiological conditions, mitochondria that lose membrane potential become marked for degradation through the action of PINK1 (PTEN-induced kinase 1), which accumulates on the outer mitochondrial membrane. This accumulation results in the recruitment and activation of Parkin, an E3 ubiquitin ligase that ubiquitinates mitochondrial surface proteins, thereby flagging them for autophagic degradation. UA has been shown to upregulate the PINK1/Parkin pathway, thereby enhancing the clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria and ensuring mitochondrial quality control (Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024; Jayatunga et al., 2021).  

In addition to its role in mitophagy, UA has been demonstrated to elicit pronounced effects on lysosomal recycling processes through the promotion of SQSTM1/p62-dependent lysophagy. This pathway is critical because damaged lysosomes, if not effectively removed, can contribute to cellular toxicity and inflammation. The engagement of p62 in the targeting of these vesicles for autophagosome formation has been observed as a necessary step in the restoration of autophagic flux within RPE cells (Jiménez-Loygorri & Boya, 2024; Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024). The dual activation of mitophagy and lysophagy by UA helps mitigate the accumulation of damaged organelles, thereby reducing the levels of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) and subsequent oxidative stress.

At the biochemical level, UA influences several upstream signaling pathways. Notably, it activates the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)/ULK1 axis—a key regulator of autophagy. Activation of AMPK leads to phosphorylation of ULK1, kick-starting the autophagy process, and is associated with improved energy balance and mitochondrial turnover. This activation is complemented by an upregulation of factors such as PGC-1α (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha), which is instrumental in mitochondrial biogenesis. Together, these events not only promote the removal of impaired mitochondria but also support the generation of new, functional mitochondria, thereby restoring mitochondrial homeostasis (Jayatunga et al., 2021; Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024).

Furthermore, the anti-inflammatory properties of UA are closely tied to its capacity to modulate oxidative stress. By clearing mitochondria that are prone to leaking mtROS, UA indirectly diminishes the activation of pro-inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB and AP-1, which have been implicated in the pathogenesis of dry AMD. UA’s activation of SIRT1, SIRT3, and AMPK also play a role in upregulating antioxidant defenses; for instance, the enhanced transcription of Nrf2-dependent genes contributes to a robust antioxidative response. These molecular interactions collectively participate in mitigating the inflammatory milieu that is characteristic of neurodegenerative conditions and retinal disorders (Makarov & Korkotian, 2023; Zhang et al., 2025; Jayatunga et al., 2021).

In the context of retinal degeneration, several studies have delineated how UA’s molecular actions translate into cytoprotective outcomes. In SI-induced retinal injury models, the re-establishment of autophagic flux by UA is correlated with reduced RPE cell death and preservation of photoreceptor function. Although some findings suggest that UA’s neuroprotective effects in these models are independent of PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, there is substantial evidence that UA comprehensively enhances cellular quality control mechanisms through both mitophagy and lysophagy. This dichotomy might reflect the compound’s capability to engage multiple parallel pathways to ensure the efficient clearance of damaged organelles (Jiménez-Loygorri & Boya, 2024; Fehér et al., 2022). Given that the retina is especially sensitive to oxidative stress and that impaired mitochondrial dynamics are a hallmark of RPE dysfunction in dry AMD, these molecular mechanisms underscore UA’s therapeutic potential.

At a more granular level, it is worth noting that UA appears to influence the expression levels of key mitophagy-related proteins not only at the post-translational level but also transcriptionally. For instance, treatment with UA has been associated with increased expression of Parkin and proteins involved in the formation of autophagosomes, such as BECN1, further reinforcing its role in maintaining mitochondrial and lysosomal integrity. This transcriptional regulation may be mediated by the activation of transcription factors such as FOXO, which have been implicated in cellular stress responses and longevity pathways. As mitochondrial dysfunction contributes significantly to the pathology of dry AMD, the ability of UA to simultaneously promote the degradation of damaged mitochondria and stimulate mitochondrial biogenesis positions it as a strong candidate for addressing the underlying etiologies of RPE degeneration (Jayatunga et al., 2021; Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024).

In summary, the detailed biochemistry of UA illustrates that its mechanism of action involves a well-coordinated activation of autophagic processes through both the PINK1/Parkin axis for mitophagy and the SQSTM1/p62 pathway for lysophagy, with auxiliary support from AMPK/ULK1 and SIRT-mediated signaling. This comprehensive modulation of cellular clearance pathways is critical in contexts where mitochondrial dysfunction and lysosomal impairment drive disease progression, such as in dry age-related macular degeneration (Jiménez-Loygorri & Boya, 2024; Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024).

4. Expected Effect  
The central hypothesis behind repurposing Urolithin A for dry AMD posits that the compound enhances PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells, thereby accelerating the clearance of damaged mitochondria that contribute to excessive mtROS production. In the cellular assays designed to evaluate this therapeutic intervention, the expected effect would be a measurable reduction in mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress within RPE cells, ultimately leading to improved cellular viability and resistance to atrophy.  
 
Experimental models of RPE degeneration, particularly those utilizing sodium iodate (SI) as a toxic insult, have provided a platform for assessing the cytoprotective properties of UA. In these models, UA treatment leads to a restoration of autophagic flux—a critical determinant of cellular health—by enhancing both the degradation of impaired mitochondria and the recycling of damaged lysosomes through the p62-dependent pathway. The net effect is a significant decrease in RPE cell death and, consequently, the preservation of photoreceptor cells and visual function. This outcome is particularly relevant for dry AMD, a condition characterized by geographic atrophy of the RPE and subsequent photoreceptor degeneration (Jiménez-Loygorri & Boya, 2024).

At the molecular level, the activation of the PINK1/Parkin pathway by UA is expected to facilitate the tagging of dysfunctional mitochondria for autophagic removal. Consequently, a reduction in mtROS levels would follow, as damaged mitochondria are a major source of these reactive species. In parallel, the activation of AMPK/ULK1 signaling by UA further promotes autophagosome formation, ensuring that the cell’s degradative capacity is optimized to handle the increased load of dysfunctional organelles. The coordinated activation of these pathways not only improves mitochondrial quality control but also indirectly suppresses downstream inflammatory cascades that are typically exacerbated by oxidative stress. For instance, by reducing mtROS levels, UA is anticipated to attenuate the activation of pro-inflammatory transcription factors such as NF-κB, thereby contributing to a more quiescent cellular environment supportive of RPE survival (Jayatunga et al., 2021; Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024).

More specifically, in the assay proposed by the research team, RPE cells would be evaluated for changes in markers of mitophagy and lysophagy as well as for functional readouts of mitochondrial health. Biochemical assays might include quantification of PINK1 and Parkin recruitment to mitochondria, measurement of autophagic flux using LC3 conversion assays, and assessment of p62 levels. Furthermore, evaluation of mitochondrial membrane potential and ROS production through fluorometric assays would provide additional functional evidence for UA’s efficacy. Given that studies in animal models have shown that UA treatment not only restores autophagic flux but also leads to increased cone survival and improved visual function, it is reasonable to expect that similar beneficial effects could be recapitulated in RPE cells in vitro (Jiménez-Loygorri & Boya, 2024; Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024).

Another anticipated effect of UA treatment in RPE cells is the upregulation of mitochondrial biogenesis. The activation of AMPK and the consequent stimulation of PGC-1α are critical steps in fostering the production of new mitochondria, which serve to replace those that have been degraded. This replenishment of the mitochondrial pool is essential for maintaining cellular energy homeostasis in an environment that is otherwise challenged by the metabolic demands of ongoing stress. The dual action of UA in both clearing damaged mitochondria and promoting the biogenesis of healthy ones creates a favorable cellular milieu that could slow, or potentially reverse, the progression of RPE atrophy—a key pathological feature of dry AMD (Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024).

Moreover, the expected reduction in mtROS levels upon UA treatment has broader implications for the cellular stress response. High levels of ROS not only cause direct oxidative damage to cellular components but also activate signaling pathways that lead to further inflammation and apoptosis. By mitigating this excessive ROS production, UA is likely to reduce the activation of stress-related kinases and inflammatory mediators that contribute to the degeneration of RPE cells. This protective effect on cellular integrity is anticipated to translate into preserved retinal function, as the health of the RPE is integral to the maintenance of the outer retina and photoreceptor survival (Jiménez-Loygorri & Boya, 2024; Fehér et al., 2022).

In summary, the expected effect of UA in the proposed assay is multifactorial:  
– Enhanced clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria via the upregulation of the PINK1/Parkin pathway, as well as p62-dependent lysophagy.  
– A corresponding reduction in mtROS, leading to decreased oxidative stress and lower inflammatory signaling.  
– Improvement in mitochondrial biogenesis and overall cellular energy homeostasis via activation of the AMPK/ULK1 pathway and upregulation of PGC-1α.  
– Ultimately, these molecular changes should culminate in improved RPE cell survival and a delay or reversal of atrophic processes that underpin dry AMD (Jiménez-Loygorri & Boya, 2024; Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024).

5. Overall Evaluation  
In evaluating Urolithin A as a therapeutic candidate for dry age-related macular degeneration, several strengths and potential areas for further investigation emerge. First and foremost, UA offers a robust safety profile supported by both preclinical studies and early-phase clinical trials in elderly populations, where it has been administered at relatively high doses without observable toxicity or adverse events (Jayatunga et al., 2021; Liu, 2022). This favorable safety and pharmacokinetic profile is critical given the generally conservative approach required when developing treatments for chronic, age-related conditions like AMD.

The molecular versatility of UA is another significant strength. Its capacity to engage multiple autophagic pathways—specifically, the PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy and the SQSTM1/p62-dependent lysophagy—provides a multifaceted approach to mitigating RPE degeneration. Given that the pathogenesis of dry AMD involves a complex interplay of mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammation, the ability of UA to target these processes simultaneously renders it a particularly attractive candidate (Jiménez-Loygorri & Boya, 2024; Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024). Furthermore, the activation of upstream signaling pathways such as AMPK/ULK1 not only enhances autophagic activity but also supports mitochondrial biogenesis, thereby ensuring that the clearance of damaged mitochondria does not result in an energy deficit within RPE cells (Jayatunga et al., 2021; Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024).

Preclinical data from retinal degeneration models provide compelling evidence that UA can ameliorate pathological features reminiscent of dry AMD. In SI-induced models, UA treatment has resulted in restored autophagic flux, reduced RPE cell death, and preservation of visual function—all of which are promising indicators for its potential to slow or reverse the atrophic processes underlying geographic atrophy in AMD (Jiménez-Loygorri & Boya, 2024; Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024). Moreover, its ability to mitigate inflammatory signaling and reduce mtROS production adds another layer of protective activity that is critical in managing chronic retinal degeneration.

Despite these strengths, several weaknesses and challenges warrant consideration. One area of potential ambiguity concerns the precise mechanistic contributions of the PINK1/Parkin pathway versus the SQSTM1/p62-mediated lysophagy in mediating UA’s protective effects. While some studies emphasize the role of PINK1/Parkin in clearance of damaged mitochondria, others have reported that UA’s neuroprotective benefits are maintained even with genetic manipulations of these proteins, suggesting that p62-dependent lysophagy may be the predominant mechanism in certain contexts (Jiménez-Loygorri & Boya, 2024; Fehér et al., 2022). This duality raises questions regarding which pathway is most critical in RPE cells, particularly given that the proposed hypothesis centers on enhanced PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy. Further mechanistic studies in RPE-specific models will be necessary to delineate the relative contributions of these pathways and to optimize dosing and formulation strategies accordingly.

Another potential weakness is the translational gap between preclinical studies and clinical application in dry AMD. Although UA has shown promise in animal models of retinal degeneration, human disease is complex, and dry AMD is known for its multifactorial etiology. The pharmacokinetic properties of UA, including its relatively short half-life and the challenges associated with ensuring adequate ocular bioavailability, might necessitate novel delivery strategies for effective retinal targeting. Formulation development—potentially involving sustained-release systems or ocular-specific delivery vehicles—may be required to overcome these hurdles (Jayatunga et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2025). Additionally, while the evidence from non-ocular (e.g., muscle and neural) studies is robust, direct clinical evidence in AMD patients remains sparse, necessitating carefully designed human trials to confirm efficacy and safety in this specific population.

It is also important to contextualize UA’s potential within the current landscape of AMD treatments. While existing therapies for wet AMD (e.g., anti-VEGF agents) have transformed clinical outcomes, there remains no established treatment for dry AMD. The multifaceted mode of action of UA—targeting mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammation—offers a unique therapeutic approach that could complement or, in some cases, provide an alternative to emerging interventions. However, comparative studies against other experimental agents will be critical to fully establish its translational relevance and competitive advantage in the therapeutic space (Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024).

Overall, Urolithin A presents several compelling attributes that support its development as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD. Its origin as a naturally derived, gut-microbiota transformation product provides a favorable starting point from a safety perspective. Preclinical studies consistently demonstrate its ability to enhance autophagic clearance mechanisms, reduce oxidative damage, and improve cell survival in models that recapitulate key features of AMD. The ability of UA to simultaneously stimulate both mitophagy and lysophagy—the former via the PINK1/Parkin pathway and the latter via p62-dependent mechanisms—positions it as a multi-targeted intervention capable of addressing the multifactorial pathology of RPE degeneration (Jiménez-Loygorri & Boya, 2024; Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024).

Nonetheless, for the successful translation of UA into a clinically effective therapy for dry AMD, several challenges need to be addressed. Detailed mechanistic insights specifically in RPE cells are required to understand the relative importance of the PINK1/Parkin versus the lysophagy pathway. Optimizing drug delivery to ensure sustained retinal bioavailability and addressing potential issues related to its pharmacokinetic profile are also key areas that will require further research. Finally, controlled clinical studies in AMD patients will be essential to validate the promising preclinical findings and to ensure that the observed benefits in animal models translate into meaningful clinical outcomes.

In conclusion, based on the comprehensive literature reviewed from biochemical, clinical, and biomedical sources, Urolithin A demonstrates significant potential as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for the treatment of dry age-related macular degeneration. Its established ability to enhance mitochondrial quality control through both mitophagy and lysophagy, alongside its additional anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, offers a novel mechanism to counteract the progressive degeneration observed in RPE cells during AMD. Given the encouraging preclinical data and a robust safety profile already evidenced in other indications, further investigations—particularly those focusing on RPE-specific mechanisms and optimized ocular drug delivery—are warranted to fully ascertain UA’s clinical potential in halting or reversing geographic atrophy in dry AMD (Jiménez-Loygorri & Boya, 2024; Jiménez-Loygorri et al., 2024; Jayatunga et al., 2021).
