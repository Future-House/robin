Proposal for Sulforaphane (Sigma-Aldrich S4441)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Sulforaphane is a naturally occurring isothiocyanate originally identified in cruciferous vegetables such as broccoli and Brussels sprouts. It is generated through the hydrolysis of glucoraphanin by the enzyme myrosinase, which is released upon plant tissue damage. As a member of the phytochemical family, sulforaphane belongs to a class of natural compounds known for inducing phase II detoxification enzymes and antioxidant proteins. Historically, this compound has been extensively investigated for its chemopreventive and cytoprotective properties, particularly through the activation of the nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway. Being one of the most studied Nrf2 activators, its discovery stemmed from early investigations into dietary antioxidants and their role in reducing carcinogenesis and inflammation. In the context of drug development, sulforaphane is considered a promising candidate for repurposing because it combines a favorable preclinical safety profile with a well-characterized mechanism of action that includes modulation of intracellular redox signaling. Its broad application in preclinical studies to ameliorate oxidative stress and inflammation suggests that targeted delivery to retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells might help alleviate the oxidative injury observed in geographic atrophy, the advanced form of dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) (Bellezza, 2018; Chang et al., 2020).

Therapeutic History:  
Sulforaphane has attracted considerable attention for its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties over the past two decades. Preclinical studies have demonstrated its protective effects in various models of oxidative stress, including those involving retinal tissues and RPE cells. In in vitro studies using ARPE-19 cells—a well-accepted human RPE model—sulforaphane exhibited dose-dependent protection against oxidative insults induced by agents such as tert-butyl hydroperoxide (t-BOOH) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂). These experiments demonstrated that sulforaphane pretreatment significantly reduced reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and mitigated mitochondrial membrane potential damage, ultimately leading to reduced apoptotic cell death (Chang et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2021). Similarly, other investigations have reported sulforaphane’s ability to induce cytoprotective genes in RPE cells through Nrf2 activation, resulting in improved cell survival under stress conditions (Zhang et al., 2024; Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019).

Moreover, several in vivo studies in rodent models have provided evidence of sulforaphane-mediated retinal neuroprotection. Examples include models of photooxidative damage in mice, where sulforaphane delayed photoreceptor degeneration and prevented RPE dysfunction—mechanisms that are highly relevant to the pathogenesis of dry AMD (Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019; Bellezza, 2018). However, it should be noted that although sulforaphane has been studied in various retinal degeneration models, there is no established clinical history of its use in human dry AMD. Nevertheless, its promising preclinical efficacy in ameliorating oxidative injury in RPE cells, coupled with its demonstrated safety across multiple biological systems including ocular tissues, supports its potential repurposing as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD (Qin, 2007; del Cano et al., 2008).

Mechanism of Action:  
The principal mechanism underlying sulforaphane’s protective effects is its ability to activate the Nrf2 pathway. Under basal conditions, the transcription factor Nrf2 is sequestered in the cytosol via binding to its inhibitor, Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1), which directs Nrf2 for ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation. Sulforaphane, owing to its electrophilic isothiocyanate moiety, reacts with critical cysteine residues on Keap1. This modification alters the conformation of Keap1 and prevents the degradation of Nrf2, leading to its stabilization and subsequent nuclear translocation (Wherry, 2013). Once in the nucleus, Nrf2 heterodimerizes with small Maf proteins and binds to antioxidant response elements (ARE) in the promoter regions of various cytoprotective genes. The result is a robust induction of phase II detoxification enzymes and antioxidant proteins including heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), NAD(P)H:quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1), and superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2). These enzymes play critical roles in neutralizing ROS, repairing oxidative damage, and maintaining cellular redox balance (Qin, 2007; Zhang et al., 2024).

In addition to its classic Nrf2-dependent antioxidant response, sulforaphane has been shown to modulate other cellular pathways that are important for mitochondrial health. In retinal cells, sulforaphane has been reported to attenuate mitochondrial membrane potential collapse and reduce mitochondrial superoxide production, as evidenced by decreased MitoSOX fluorescence in treated ARPE-19 cells (Chang et al., 2020). These mitochondrial protective actions are thought to involve the upregulation of antioxidant enzymes that localize to the mitochondria, as well as enhancement of the spare respiratory capacity—thereby preserving ATP generation under oxidative stress conditions. Furthermore, sulforaphane has been implicated in the regulation of the SIRT1/PGC-1α axis, which is critical for mitochondrial biogenesis and metabolic homeostasis. This dual engagement—both through Nrf2-driven transcriptional activation and mitochondrial biogenesis pathways—positions sulforaphane as a multifaceted agent capable of sustaining cellular energy production and reducing oxidative injury in the RPE, a cell type that is particularly vulnerable in geographic atrophy (Yang et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024).

Beyond its antioxidant effects, sulforaphane also exerts anti-inflammatory properties by downregulating the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and adhesion molecules (e.g., ICAM-1, MCP-1). This is particularly relevant in the context of AMD, where chronic low-grade inflammation is believed to exacerbate RPE dysfunction and drusen formation (Chang et al., 2020; del Cano et al., 2008). In summary, the molecular interactions of sulforaphane—ranging from direct modification of Keap1 to the induction of mitochondrial antioxidant defenses—provide a robust mechanistic rationale for its potential utility in mitigating the multifactorial oxidative and inflammatory challenges associated with dry AMD.

Expected Effect:  
Given its well-documented mechanism of action, the hypothesis that sulforaphane induces Nrf2-mediated mitochondrial antioxidant pathways in RPE cells is strongly supported by preclinical evidence. When RPE cells are subjected to oxidative stress, it is expected that sulforaphane treatment will lead to a dose-dependent upregulation of Nrf2 target genes including HO-1, NQO1, and SOD2. The elevation of these transcripts correlates with a reduction in mitochondrial superoxide levels, as measured by decreased MitoSOX fluorescence, and an improvement in the spare respiratory capacity—a surrogate marker for mitochondrial function and ATP generation (Chang et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2021).

In cellular assays using ARPE-19 cells, administration of sulforaphane prior to oxidative challenge has been shown to maintain mitochondrial membrane potential and decrease cytochrome c release, thereby reducing the activation of downstream apoptotic cascades. The suppression of ROS production and the preservation of mitochondrial bioenergetics result in improved cell viability and reduced apoptotic indices. For instance, studies have demonstrated that sulforaphane pretreatment leads to lower levels of cleaved caspase-3 and PARP-1, indicating a direct link between enhanced antioxidant defense and the inhibition of programmed cell death pathways in RPE cells (Yang et al., 2021; Chang et al., 2020).

At the molecular level, the expected outcome of sulforaphane administration in a dry AMD context is multifactorial. The compound would elevate the expression of cytoprotective enzymes that not only detoxify reactive oxygen species but also facilitate the repair of oxidatively damaged macromolecules. This should translate into a measurable decrease in overall oxidative stress markers, preservation of mitochondrial function (evidenced by sustained ATP production and improved spare respiratory capacity), and stabilization of the RPE cell phenotype. Moreover, since mitochondrial dysfunction is a central element of geographic atrophy pathogenesis, sulforaphane’s ability to maintain ATP generation could directly counteract the energy deficit observed in degenerating RPE cells (Zhang et al., 2024; Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019).

These expected effects are supported by a range of preclinical studies. For example, quantitative redox fluorometry results reported in ARPE-19 cells indicate that sulforaphane-treated cells maintain a significantly higher redox ratio during oxidative challenge compared to untreated controls, which is indicative of an enhanced antioxidant state. Additionally, the upregulation of mitochondrial antioxidants and improved bioenergetic profiles have been correlated with better cell survival outcomes, reinforcing the concept that sulforaphane can counteract the deleterious cellular changes observed in models of dry AMD (del Cano et al., 2008; Qin, 2007).

Overall Evaluation:  
Sulforaphane, as exemplified by the Sigma-Aldrich S4441 formulation, presents a compelling therapeutic candidate for the treatment of dry AMD, particularly targeting the geographic atrophy form characterized by pronounced RPE degeneration. One of the major strengths of sulforaphane lies in its robust preclinical evidence demonstrating its ability to induce the Nrf2 pathway, a master regulator of oxidative stress responses. The compound’s direct modification of Keap1 and subsequent stabilization and nuclear translocation of Nrf2 trigger the upregulation of several antioxidant enzymes, such as HO-1, NQO1, and SOD2—which are critical for detoxifying reactive oxygen species and repairing oxidative damage in RPE cells (Qin, 2007; Zhang et al., 2024).

Furthermore, sulforaphane’s additional capacity to preserve mitochondrial function by maintaining membrane potential and supporting ATP synthesis addresses a major pathological hallmark of geographic atrophy, where mitochondrial dysfunction leads to energy depletion and accelerates cell death. The integration of mitochondrial antioxidant defenses with the broader cytoprotective effects of Nrf2 activation provides a convergent mechanism aimed at both preventing oxidative damage and sustaining cellular energy production under stress conditions (Yang et al., 2021; Chang et al., 2020).

Another notable strength is the extensive safety data available for sulforaphane from preclinical studies. Its natural occurrence, combined with its well-documented bioactivity in a range of cell types, underscores its potential for repurposing in ocular applications. Moreover, sulforaphane’s ability to attenuate inflammatory responses by reducing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines adds an extra layer of therapeutic benefit, considering the role of chronic inflammation in exacerbating AMD pathology (del Cano et al., 2008; Chang et al., 2020).

Despite these promising attributes, several challenges remain. One potential limitation is the bioavailability of sulforaphane. Like many natural compounds, sulforaphane may face pharmacokinetic hurdles such as poor water solubility and limited tissue penetration, which could complicate systemic delivery or localized administration to the retina. Furthermore, while the preclinical efficacy in ARPE-19 cell models and animal studies is encouraging, the translation of these findings to human patients with dry AMD remains to be validated. Variability in the cellular responses between in vitro models and the complex microenvironment of the human retina, including the influence of the blood–retinal barrier, poses a potential risk for suboptimal therapeutic delivery (Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019; Qin, 2007).

Additionally, some of the supportive data for sulforaphane’s efficacy in ocular systems come from studies published in journals with variable impact, and additional high-quality clinical studies will be necessary to establish its safety and efficacy profile in the context of dry AMD. The dosing regimens, potential off-target effects, and long-term impact of chronic Nrf2 activation in retinal tissues also require careful investigation. There is ongoing debate over the potential for Nrf2 activators to have biphasic dose responses, where excessive activation might inadvertently cause cellular stress or dysregulate normal redox homeostasis (Bellezza, 2018; del Cano et al., 2008).

In conclusion, sulforaphane demonstrates strong mechanistic potential for the treatment of dry AMD by elevating key antioxidant defenses in RPE cells and preserving mitochondrial function under oxidative stress. Its ability to induce HO-1, NQO1, and SOD2 in response to oxidative challenges and thereby reduce superoxide production while maintaining ATP synthesis is directly aligned with the therapeutic needs of geographic atrophy patients. The preclinical evidence suggests that, with appropriate formulation and delivery strategies, sulforaphane could provide a dual benefit by mitigating oxidative injury and reducing inflammation in the retina. However, further studies are required to address bioavailability challenges and to validate its clinical efficacy in well-designed human trials. Overall, sulforaphane merits further investigation as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD, and its advancement into early-phase clinical testing is justified by the compelling preclinical data available (Yang et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024; Chang et al., 2020; Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019; del Cano et al., 2008).
