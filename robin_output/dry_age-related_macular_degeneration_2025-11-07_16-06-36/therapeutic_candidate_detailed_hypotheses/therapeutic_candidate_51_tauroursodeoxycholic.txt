Proposal for Tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA; Sigma-Aldrich 12399)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA) is an endogenous bile acid, a taurine conjugate of ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), that was originally isolated from bear bile and is now produced synthetically for laboratory and clinical research (Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021). As a member of the bile acid family, TUDCA has a dual role: its traditional function in lipid digestion is accompanied by potent signaling activities that provide chemical chaperoning properties. This property enables TUDCA to stabilize misfolded proteins and alleviate endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress. In addition, TUDCA exerts anti-apoptotic effects by modulating the mitochondrial cell death pathway. Its application as a neuroprotectant has emerged over the last decade, and its favorable ocular safety profile in preclinical studies has invited consideration for repurposing in retinal degenerative conditions such as dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) (Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021; Alhasani et al., 2020).  

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, TUDCA and related bile acids have been evaluated in several models of neurodegeneration and retinal degeneration. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that TUDCA protects retinal cells against both oxidative injury and ER stress. In vitro, TUDCA treatment of ARPE-19 retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells exposed to hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) not only attenuated cell death but also reduced markers of oxidative damage, increased cell viability, and modulated antioxidant gene expression (Alhasani et al., 2020). In parallel, multiple in vivo studies have reported that systemic administration of TUDCA reduces photoreceptor apoptosis and preserves retinal structure and function in animal models of inherited retinal degeneration including retinitis pigmentosa and in models of exudative AMD such as laser-induced choroidal neovascularization (Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021; Li et al., 2024). Although the bulk of these preclinical investigations has been conducted in models focusing on photoreceptors, retinal ganglion cells, or vascular endpoints, the underlying mechanisms of TUDCA’s cytoprotective activity raise the possibility that its benefits may extend to the RPE—the cell type that is centrally involved in dry AMD and geographic atrophy. Importantly, little direct evidence exists from dedicated geographic atrophy models or experiments that solely target RPE mitochondrial endpoints; however, the promising biochemical and electrophysiological data from rodent models suggest that the candidate’s safety and overall neuroprotective profile are well established in ocular systems (Alhasani et al., 2020; Li et al., 2024).  

Mechanism of Action:  
The mechanistic basis of TUDCA’s therapeutic potential is multifaceted. A central theme is its ability to reduce ER stress in cells that are undergoing chronic protein-folding challenges. Under conditions of sustained stress, the unfolded protein response (UPR) is activated, and key transcription factors such as CHOP (CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein homologous protein) become upregulated to drive cell death when the stress is unmanageable. TUDCA has been shown in various cell models to act as a chemical chaperone, thereby reducing ER stress and suppressing CHOP induction (Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021; Alhasani et al., 2020). For instance, in ARPE-19 cell models treated with thapsigargin to induce ER stress, TUDCA treatment led to a marked decrease in CHOP expression and reduced apoptosis (Alhasani et al., 2020). This suggests that TUDCA modulates the ER stress response, possibly by stabilizing protein folding and facilitating the proper trafficking of misfolded proteins.  

In addition to its ER‐related effects, TUDCA exerts anti-apoptotic actions by stabilizing the mitochondrial membrane. Under stress conditions, loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) triggers the intrinsic apoptosis pathway, characterized by the translocation of pro-apoptotic proteins such as Bax, the release of cytochrome c, and subsequent caspase activation (Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021; Kusaczuk, 2019). Evidence from neuronal models and some retinal studies indicates that TUDCA prevents the depolarization of the mitochondrial membrane, thereby preserving Δψm, and reduces downstream apoptotic events. This effect has been linked to a reduction in mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS), which are produced excessively when mitochondrial integrity is compromised. TUDCA’s actions in reducing overall intracellular ROS levels, as reported in H₂O₂-exposed RPE cells, further support the idea that improved mitochondrial function may contribute to cell survival (Alhasani et al., 2020).  

Moreover, TUDCA can modulate additional cellular pathways that are pertinent to retinal health. It has been reported to up-regulate antioxidant defenses via increased expression of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) and enhancement of glutathione (GSH) levels. These effects likely contribute to the reduction in both cytosolic and mitochondrial oxidative stress (Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021). Other studies have highlighted TUDCA’s influence on phagocytic pathways in the RPE—for example, by promoting MerTK-mediated phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments—which, although not directly linked to ER or mitochondrial stress, represent an important aspect of RPE physiology that could indirectly improve cell survival in degenerative conditions (Murase et al., 2015).  

Taken together, the biochemical and molecular data indicate that TUDCA’s mechanism of action is well aligned with addressing the cellular stress observed in dry AMD. By inhibiting CHOP induction, preserving Δψm, and reducing mtROS, TUDCA could feasibly stop or slow the progression of RPE cell death that underpins geographic atrophy (Alhasani et al., 2020; Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021).  

Expected Effect:  
The proposal hypothesizes that TUDCA will reduce ER stress in the RPE, thereby preventing secondary mitochondrial dysfunction and the associated production of reactive oxygen species. In the proposed assay, RPE cells (either ARPE-19 or primary RPE cultures) subjected to an ER stressor (such as thapsigargin or H₂O₂) are expected to exhibit elevated markers of ER stress, particularly CHOP, along with signs of mitochondrial distress including decreased mitochondrial membrane potential and increased mtROS production. On treatment with TUDCA at appropriate dosages (often in the 100 μM range in similar in vitro assays), one expects to observe a significant reduction in CHOP induction along with the preservation or restoration of Δψm, as measured by fluorescent indicators such as JC-1 or TMRM, and a concomitant decrease in mitochondrial ROS as assessed by probes such as MitoSOX. These effects are expected because prior in vitro studies in retinal cells have shown that TUDCA can ameliorate cell death by modulating oxidative stress and apoptosis, and similar outcomes have been observed in neuronal and non-retinal cell models (Alhasani et al., 2020; Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021).  

Furthermore, given that dry AMD is characterized by chronic ER stress and secondary mitochondrial dysfunction in the RPE, the ability of TUDCA to reduce these stress responses may translate into improved RPE survival and function. RPE cells express high levels of ER stress markers under pathologic conditions, and proteins such as CHOP are known mediators of apoptosis in these cells. It is therefore expected that TUDCA will attenuate the cascade of events initiated by ER stress—reducing apoptotic signaling, preserving mitochondrial function, reducing mtROS, and ultimately leading to enhanced cell viability. As such, the anticipated outcome in the proposed assay is a clear biochemical and functional improvement in RPE cultures challenged with ER stressors, supporting the use of TUDCA in dry AMD (Alhasani et al., 2020).  

Overall Evaluation:  
TUDCA presents as a promising candidate for drug repurposing in dry AMD due to its well-documented roles as an ER stress inhibitor and mitochondrial stabilizer. One of the major strengths of TUDCA is its dual mechanism of action: it not only functions as a chemical chaperone to reduce ER stress and inhibit the pro-apoptotic effector CHOP but also stabilizes the mitochondrial membrane to prevent the cascade of events leading to cell death. These effects are complemented by its ability to reduce overall reactive oxygen species and bolster antioxidant defenses, which are critical in tissues affected by chronic oxidative stress such as the RPE in geographic atrophy (Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021; Alhasani et al., 2020).  

In terms of therapeutic history, TUDCA has been used extensively in preclinical models of retinal degeneration, with multiple studies demonstrating its protective effects on photoreceptors and retinal ganglion cells. Its prior use in ocular contexts and a favorable safety profile in animal models provide important translational support. However, one potential weakness in repurposing TUDCA for dry AMD is that most of the preclinical evidence is derived from models of photoreceptor degeneration, diabetic retinopathy, or choroidal neovascularization rather than from targeted assessments in RPE cells or dry AMD models. While there is suggestive evidence from several studies that TUDCA benefits RPE function—such as improved phagocytosis via MerTK activation—the direct demonstration of its ability to inhibit CHOP induction, preserve mitochondrial membrane potential, or specifically reduce mitochondrial ROS in RPE is less robust (Alhasani et al., 2020; Murase et al., 2015).  

Furthermore, although mechanistic data in other tissue and neuronal models strongly support TUDCA’s capacity to modulate both ER stress and mitochondrial dysfunction (Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021; Kusaczuk, 2019), these endpoints have not been unequivocally established in RPE cells within the context of geographic atrophy. This gap highlights the need for further targeted preclinical studies. For example, experiments using polarized primary RPE cultures or RPE-specific animal models of dry AMD should be undertaken to directly measure CHOP expression, mitochondrial membrane potential via JC-1/TMRM assays, and mtROS levels via MitoSOX or equivalent assays following TUDCA treatment. Additionally, while in vitro assays have established its general safety in retinal cells, chronic dosing studies in appropriate AMD models will be important to establish long-term efficacy and safety.  

Another consideration is that the multifaceted nature of dry AMD pathogenesis extends beyond ER stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. While TUDCA’s actions on these pathways are compelling, resistance or compensatory mechanisms in RPE cells could limit its efficacy in the complex environment of geographic atrophy. Nonetheless, the broad-spectrum anti-apoptotic and anti-oxidative actions of TUDCA provide a strong rationale for its further examination in dry AMD.  

In summary, TUDCA is a highly attractive candidate for repurposing as a therapy for dry AMD based on its natural origin as a bile acid, its established use as a chemical chaperone and mitochondrial stabilizer, and its demonstrated neuroprotective and cytoprotective effects in multiple retinal models. Its capacity to reduce ER stress—with particular inhibition of CHOP induction—and to preserve mitochondrial membrane potential and lower oxidative stress aligns well with the proposed mechanism underlying RPE degeneration in geographic atrophy. However, critical gaps remain in the direct evidence for its efficacy in RPE-specific systems and AMD/dry AMD models. The next steps should include rigorous experimental validation in relevant in vitro and in vivo models to establish that TUDCA indeed preserves Δψm and suppresses mitochondrial ROS in ER-stressed RPE cells while reducing pro-apoptotic markers such as CHOP. If these endpoints can be achieved in targeted preclinical studies, TUDCA could represent a promising, safe, and mechanistically rational therapeutic candidate for the treatment of dry AMD (Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021; Alhasani et al., 2020).
