Proposal for Metformin (Sigma-Aldrich D150959)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Metformin is a well‐known therapeutic agent belonging to the biguanide class, originally derived from guanidine compounds found in the plant Galega officinalis. The molecule is a synthetic derivative of naturally occurring galegine and has been refined into a modern oral antihyperglycemic agent (Augustin & Atorf, 2023). This class of compounds has historically been used as a first-line oral treatment for type 2 diabetes mellitus, with metformin being one of the most prescribed drugs worldwide for managing hyperglycemia. Its synthesis did not follow the classical target‐driven design path but instead emerged from an adaptation of naturally occurring structures into a chemically optimized, orally bioavailable, and generally well-tolerated drug (Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Dang et al., 2021). Given its long clinical history starting from the 1950s and subsequent FDA approval in the 1990s, metformin has accumulated a significant amount of safety data and clinical experience, which collectively make it an attractive candidate for repurposing into other disease areas, such as dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) characterized by geographic atrophy (Amin et al., 2022).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, metformin has been used extensively as an antidiabetic agent to lower hepatic glucose output and improve peripheral insulin sensitivity. Its beneficial pleiotropic effects beyond glycemic control have been recognized over the years; these include anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antiangiogenic properties, which have been observed in both biochemical and clinical investigations. Multiple epidemiologic studies have examined the association between metformin use and ocular outcomes. Retrospective analyses in predominantly diabetic cohorts have indicated that metformin use correlates with reduced odds or hazard for developing age-related macular degeneration and other retinal diseases, although some studies report mixed results depending on cumulative dose, exposure timing, and concomitant diabetes-related complications (Moir et al., 2023; Amin et al., 2022; Liang et al., 2022). Notably, while metformin has not historically been approved for any ocular indication, several preclinical studies using retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cell models have demonstrated that metformin may protect against oxidative damage and improve mitochondrial function. Moreover, a Phase II single-blind randomized trial was initiated to assess the efficacy of metformin in slowing the progression of geographic atrophy among non-diabetic patients with advanced dry AMD (Shen et al., 2024), suggesting that the concept of repurposing metformin for dry AMD is already under active clinical investigation.

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, metformin’s primary mechanism of action involves the inhibition of mitochondrial complex I of the electron transport chain, resulting in a decrease in ATP production and a concomitant increase in the intracellular AMP:ATP ratio. This metabolic shift leads to the activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), a master regulator of cellular energy homeostasis that orchestrates shifts in metabolism under conditions of energy deficit (Moir et al., 2023; Augustin & Atorf, 2023). AMPK activation exerts multiple downstream effects that are particularly relevant to RPE cell function and the pathogenesis of geographic atrophy. First, AMPK activation promotes autophagy, including mitophagy, a quality-control mechanism that ensures the removal of dysfunctional mitochondria. Preclinical studies in RPE cell models, such as those using ARPE-19 cells, have provided evidence that metformin increases autophagosome formation, reduces intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), lowers apoptosis rates, and helps maintain the mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) under oxidative stress conditions (Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Toppila et al., 2024). Additionally, the activation of AMPK in RPE cells has been associated with upregulation of protective pathways including enhanced nitric oxide (NO) production and increased expression of mitochondrial biogenesis factors such as PGC-1α, which together contribute to improved mitochondrial quality control (Moir et al., 2023; Augustin & Atorf, 2023).  

Furthermore, metformin is known to activate both AMPK-dependent and AMPK-independent signaling pathways. The AMPK-independent effects may include inhibition of NF-κB activation, leading to a reduction in pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α, which are implicated in chronic inflammation observed in the aging retina (Jiang et al., 2022; Amin et al., 2022). From a biochemical standpoint, the inhibition of complex I by metformin also directly reduces the leakage of electrons that can form ROS, thereby lowering mitochondrial oxidative stress—a key aspect in the progression of dry AMD pathogenesis (Moir et al., 2023; Augustin & Atorf, 2023). It is important to note, however, that several reports have highlighted the concern that activation of AMPK in vivo or in vitro appears to require suprapharmacological doses of metformin, casting uncertainty on whether standard clinical dosing is sufficient to achieve these retinal effects (Moir et al., 2023). Nonetheless, the preclinical evidence supports a model whereby metformin’s inhibition of mitochondrial complex I—thereby reducing ROS and enhancing autophagy—provides a rationale for its potential use in protecting RPE cells from oxidative stress-induced damage (Amin et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2022).

Expected Effect:  
The hypothesis driving the repurposing of metformin for dry AMD is predicated on its ability to activate AMPK and inhibit mitochondrial complex I leakage in RPE cells, leading to reduced ROS production and enhanced mitochondrial quality control. In the context of geographic atrophy, RPE dysfunction and death play a central role in the degeneration of photoreceptors and the progression of AMD. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that metformin can protect ARPE-19 cells (an established human RPE cell line) from oxidative insults by lowering intracellular ROS levels, augmenting autophagic processes, and preserving mitochondrial membrane potential (Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Toppila et al., 2024). Additionally, through the activation of AMPK, metformin is expected to stimulate the expression of mitochondrial biogenesis factors, leading to improved mitochondrial function, which is critical given the high metabolic demands of the RPE (Moir et al., 2023). 

Epidemiologically, several retrospective cohort and case-control studies have observed associations between metformin use and a reduced risk of developing AMD. Some studies even suggest that these associations are dose- and timing-dependent, with earlier or sustained use of metformin correlating with greater protective effects (Moir et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2022). Therefore, in an assay designed to measure the effects of metformin on mitochondrial function and oxidative stress in RPE cells, one would expect to see an enhancement in autophagic flux, decreased markers of oxidative stress, and preservation or restoration of Δψm. These cellular effects would ideally translate into a slower progression of geographic atrophy in vivo, as suggested by the decreased degeneration of the retinal layers that has been observed in certain mouse models of retinal damage (Jiang et al., 2022; Amin et al., 2022). RPE cells are known to express AMPK and other components of the autophagy machinery, which further supports the rationale for targeting these pathways in the treatment of dry AMD (Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Moir et al., 2023).

Overall Evaluation:  
Metformin shows considerable promise as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry age-related macular degeneration, particularly for geographic atrophy, due to several key strengths. First, its long and well-documented clinical history in the management of type 2 diabetes provides a robust safety profile and widespread clinical familiarity, which is a significant advantage when considering repurposing strategies (Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Dang et al., 2021; Amin et al., 2022). Second, the molecular and cellular mechanisms by which metformin acts—namely, the inhibition of mitochondrial complex I, subsequent activation of AMPK, and promotion of autophagy—are highly relevant to the pathophysiology of dry AMD. The ability of metformin to reduce ROS and promote mitochondrial quality control addresses one of the core aspects of RPE dysfunction and degeneration seen in geographic atrophy (Moir et al., 2023; Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Jiang et al., 2022).

Furthermore, the epidemiologic data showing associations between metformin use and a reduced incidence of AMD lend additional support to its potential utility in this setting (Liang et al., 2022; Moir et al., 2023). The possibility that metformin’s protective effects might be independent of its glycemic control properties is particularly intriguing, as it expands the target population to non-diabetic AMD patients—a group that represents the majority of those affected by geographic atrophy (Moir et al., 2023; Brown et al., 2019; Moir et al., 2024). On a mechanistic level, the detailed understanding of metformin’s interaction with AMPK and mitochondrial pathways not only provides a clear therapeutic target but also offers multiple biomarkers for assessing efficacy in both preclinical and clinical studies.

Despite its strengths, several weaknesses and challenges must be acknowledged. One significant concern is whether standard clinical doses of metformin are sufficient to activate the AMPK pathway in retinal tissues, given that some in vitro studies indicate suprapharmacological doses are required to see a robust effect (Moir et al., 2023). This raises questions of retinal bioavailability and whether alternative delivery strategies, such as local ocular administration or formulation enhancements, might be required to achieve therapeutic concentrations in the RPE. Additionally, while the epidemiologic studies are promising, they are not uniformly consistent; some retrospective analyses have reported no association or even conflicting results depending on dosing and timing parameters (Buonfiglio et al., 2024; Brown et al., 2019; Amin et al., 2022). These discrepancies underscore the inherent limitations of observational data, including potential confounding factors such as the presence and severity of diabetes, concomitant medications, and the challenges of accurately classifying AMD subtypes using administrative coding.

Another consideration is that although metformin’s broad mechanism of action (i.e., modulation of inflammation, oxidative stress, and autophagy) is advantageous in addressing multifactorial diseases like AMD, it may also lead to off-target effects or systemic side effects such as vitamin B12 deficiency or, in rare instances, lactic acidosis (Jiang et al., 2022; Augustin & Atorf, 2023). In elderly populations—who are generally most affected by dry AMD—these risks must be carefully weighed against the potential benefits. Moreover, the heterogeneity in study designs and patient populations in the available epidemiologic literature means that prospective, well-designed clinical trials remain essential to definitively determine whether metformin can meaningfully slow the progression of geographic atrophy in AMD (Shen et al., 2024).

In summary, the overall evaluation of metformin as a repurposed therapeutic for dry AMD is cautiously optimistic. Its well-established safety profile, combined with mechanistic data that align closely with the underlying pathology of geographic atrophy, make it a compelling candidate. The strengths lie in its ability to activate AMPK, thereby reducing mitochondrial ROS, promoting autophagy, and potentially preserving RPE function—molecular actions that directly address key pathological features of dry AMD. However, critical questions regarding dosing, ocular bioavailability, and the variability in epidemiologic outcomes necessitate further rigorous investigation in controlled clinical settings. Moving forward, clinical trials designed to specifically assess metformin’s efficacy in non-diabetic populations with early to intermediate stages of geographic atrophy will be crucial to validate its repurposing potential. Overall, while metformin has significant promise as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD, additional studies are needed to resolve questions about its optimal dosing regimen, delivery method, and long-term efficacy in preserving retinal structure and function (Moir et al., 2023; Augustin & Atorf, 2023; Shen et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2022).
