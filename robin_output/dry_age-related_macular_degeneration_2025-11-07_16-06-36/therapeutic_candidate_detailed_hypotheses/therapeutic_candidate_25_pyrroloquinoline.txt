Proposal for Pyrroloquinoline quinone (PQQ; Sigma-Aldrich PQQ)

Below is our comprehensive evaluation of pyrroloquinoline quinone (PQQ) as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD):

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate –  
Pyrroloquinoline quinone (PQQ) is an aromatic o‐quinone originally discovered as a redox cofactor for bacterial dehydrogenases and is naturally synthesized by various Gram‐negative prokaryotes. It was first identified in microbial systems where it serves as a prosthetic group in enzymes involved in alcohol and sugar oxidation, and it has since been detected at trace levels in human tissues and milk, suggesting dietary exposure through common foods such as fermented soy products, green peppers, spinach, and human milk. PQQ is categorized within the quinone family of compounds and is recognized for its redox‐active properties; in this role, it has been considered a “vitamin‐like accessory factor” by some researchers given its involvement in cellular energy metabolism and antioxidant defense (Petricca et al., 2025; Canovai & Williams, 2025). Commercially, PQQ is available in a water‐soluble disodium salt formulation; for example, the Sigma‐Aldrich PQQ product is typical of these preparations. As a small redox‐active molecule, PQQ is distinct among nutraceuticals and has been explored in several preclinical contexts for its potential to regulate oxidative stress and promote mitochondrial function (Canovai & Williams, 2025). Consequently, this candidate represents a repurposing opportunity where its established dietary exposure, safety profile as assessed by regulatory bodies such as EFSA, and its unique bioactivity as a mitochondrial modulator may be leveraged to address the mitochondrial dysfunction implicated in dry AMD.

Therapeutic History –  
PQQ has been the subject of intensive biochemical and preclinical investigation over the past decades. Early studies concentrated on its role as a microbial cofactor, but subsequent research has documented that dietary supplementation with PQQ produces measurable effects on mitochondrial‐related metabolism and markers of inflammation in animal models and humans (Canovai & Williams, 2025). In various rodent models, oral PQQ has been shown to modulate mitochondrial number and function, thereby improving outcomes in contexts as diverse as cardiac ischemia/reperfusion, neurodegeneration, and metabolic syndrome (Akagawa, Nakano, & Ikemoto, 2016; Canovai & Williams, 2025). Additionally, EFSA conducted a safety assessment of the PQQ disodium salt formulation as a novel food ingredient and concluded that at proposed supplemental doses (up to 20 mg/day) the width of the safety margin is acceptable relative to animal toxicology data (Turck et al., 2017; Hwang & Willoughby, 2018). In human studies, although clinical trials have largely focused on endpoints such as cognition, inflammatory markers, and lipid metabolism, these trials demonstrate that oral PQQ is bioavailable, well‐tolerated, and can meaningfully impact systemic markers of oxidative stress and energy metabolism (Canovai & Williams, 2025). While no study to date has directly employed PQQ in patients with dry AMD, there is indirect evidence suggesting that PQQ’s capacity to improve cellular bioenergetics may be broadly beneficial in clinical states characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction. Some studies even indicate that PQQ upregulates mitochondrial proteins and improves ATP synthesis in cellular models derived from age‐related macular degeneration donor tissues (Canovai & Williams, 2025), although direct evidence in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) remains sparse. In summary, the therapeutic history of PQQ includes diverse preclinical applications ranging from cardioprotection to neuroprotection, with established safety in both animal and limited human studies, making it a plausible candidate for repurposing in dry AMD where mitochondrial dysfunction is a key pathogenic driver.

Mechanism of Action –  
The molecular basis of PQQ’s bioactivity is multifaceted, centering on its redox chemistry and its ability to modulate mitochondrial biogenesis. Chemically, PQQ is a potent redox‐active o‐quinone that undergoes reversible reduction to its hydroquinone form (PQQH₂) via a rapid semiquinone intermediate. This redox cycling allows PQQ to function both as a free radical scavenger and as an electron‐transfer catalyst. In vitro assays have established that the reduced form, PQQH₂, exhibits robust antioxidant capacity, with reports indicating that its aroxyl radical quenching activity can be significantly greater than that of classical antioxidants such as vitamin C and α‐tocopherol (Canovai & Williams, 2025; Charrier et al., 2024). Through its antioxidant activity, PQQ directly neutralizes reactive oxygen species (ROS) and may protect cellular constituents from oxidative damage.

Beyond its direct radical scavenging, PQQ has been implicated as an inducer of mitochondrial biogenesis. Several mechanistic studies indicate that treatment with PQQ leads to the upregulation of key transcriptional regulators involved in mitochondrial proliferation. Specifically, PQQ treatment has been shown to activate the cAMP response element‐binding protein (CREB), which in turn stimulates the expression of peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor gamma coactivator‐1 alpha (PGC‐1α) (Charrier et al., 2024; Canovai & Williams, 2025). PGC‐1α is a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis and coordinates the transcriptional activation of nuclear respiratory factors (NRF‐1/NRF‐2) and mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM), all of which are critical for the transcription and replication of mitochondrial DNA. Some studies also suggest that PQQ modulates sirtuin activity, particularly SIRT1 and SIRT3, which are NAD⁺‐dependent deacetylases that regulate mitochondrial function and cellular metabolic homeostasis (Canovai & Williams, 2025). In addition, PQQ has been reported to restore mitochondrial membrane potential, reduce proton leak, and enhance ATP production during oxidative challenge in several cellular systems (Petricca et al., 2025; Canovai & Williams, 2025). Thus, the composite mechanism of action posited for PQQ—which includes both robust redox buffering and the activation of mitochondrial biogenesis via CREB/PGC‐1α signaling—provides a molecular rationale for its use in conditions characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative injury.

Mechanistically, it is also suggested that PQQ may improve the NAD⁺/NADH ratio, thereby favoring the activity of sirtuins, which contributes to a more favorable intracellular redox state and efficient energy utilization (Jonscher, Chowanadisai, & Rucker, 2021). Although these effects have been most clearly demonstrated in hepatic, muscular, and neuronal cell models, they are directly relevant to RPE cells that rely heavily on oxidative phosphorylation for their energy demands and, hence, are particularly vulnerable to mitochondrial decay under chronic oxidative stress—a hallmark of dry AMD pathology. It is important to note that PQQ’s actions are context‐dependent; while low concentrations have been associated with protective effects, some studies suggest that higher doses can induce a pro‐oxidant effect and even impair mitochondrial dynamics in sensitive cellular contexts (Petricca et al., 2025; Turck et al., 2017). This biphasic dose response underscores the need to identify an optimal dosing window that maximizes mitochondrial biogenesis and antioxidant defense without tipping cells into oxidative stress.

Expected Effect –  
The central hypothesis guiding the repurposing of PQQ for dry AMD is that it can stimulate mitochondrial biogenesis in the RPE by upregulating PGC‐1α, thereby increasing the antioxidant capacity and restoring optimal mitochondrial respiration in cells under chronic oxidative injury. In the proposed assay, treatment with PQQ is expected to yield multiple beneficial outcomes. First, by activating CREB and augmenting PGC‐1α expression, PQQ should enhance the transcription of genes involved in mitochondrial replication and function, leading to an increased mitochondrial mass within RPE cells (Canovai & Williams, 2025; Charrier et al., 2024).

Second, increased mitochondrial mass and improved electron transport chain efficiency are expected to elevate ATP production, thereby restoring cellular energy stores that are typically depleted in dry AMD due to chronic oxidative damage. Experimental data from other cell types, including neuronal and hepatic cells, have shown that PQQ treatment enhances ATP output and normalizes mitochondrial membrane potential under oxidative stress conditions, suggesting that similar responses might be observed in RPE cells (Petricca et al., 2025; Canovai & Williams, 2025). Third, PQQ is anticipated to upregulate antioxidant enzymes, such as manganese superoxide dismutase (SOD2), which plays a critical role in mitigating ROS accumulation in mitochondria. By lowering oxidative stress levels in RPE cells, PQQ can help maintain cellular homeostasis and prevent the degeneration that is characteristic of dry AMD (Canovai & Williams, 2025).

Additionally, there is emerging evidence that certain studies using donor‐derived RPE cells from AMD patients have demonstrated that PQQ treatment can upregulate mitochondrial proteins and improve ATP generation in these cells (Canovai & Williams, 2025). Although the available data in retinal tissues remain limited, these findings align with the overall rationale; because RPE cells are highly metabolically active and heavily reliant on mitochondrial function, the ability to augment their biogenesis and enhance oxidative phosphorylation directly addresses a critical pathogenic mechanism of dry AMD. Complementing these expected biochemical outcomes, one would predict that functional assays (for instance, assessments of oxygen consumption rate and extracellular acidification rate) will reveal improved mitochondrial respiration and overall metabolic resilience in PQQ‐treated RPE cells.

Overall Evaluation –  
In synthesizing the extensive body of literature, we find that PQQ demonstrates considerable promise as a candidate for repurposing in dry AMD, based on the following strengths and weaknesses:

Strengths:  
1. Robust Mechanistic Rationale: PQQ’s dual role as an antioxidant and an inducer of mitochondrial biogenesis is well documented. The capacity to elevate PGC‐1α expression and thereby drive mitochondrial proliferation and enhanced energy metabolism is highly relevant given that dry AMD is characterized by RPE mitochondrial dysfunction and heightened oxidative stress (Petricca et al., 2025; Charrier et al., 2024; Canovai & Williams, 2025).  
2. Positive Safety and Bioavailability Data: Numerous preclinical studies, alongside regulatory evaluations (e.g., EFSA’s novel food assessment), indicate that PQQ is orally bioavailable and well tolerated in both animal models and limited human studies. The available toxicology data support the safety of typical supplemental doses (up to 20 mg/day), which is encouraging for repurposing efforts (Turck et al., 2017; Hwang & Willoughby, 2018; Canovai & Williams, 2025).  
3. Broad Therapeutic History: Although PQQ has not been directly tested in dry AMD in clinical settings, its prior use as a dietary supplement and its beneficial effects in neuroprotection, cardioprotection, and metabolic regulation suggest therapeutic potential in conditions underpinned by mitochondrial decay and oxidative stress (Canovai & Williams, 2025).  
4. Hypothesis‐Driven Approach: The specific hypothesis—that PQQ will stimulate RPE mitochondrial biogenesis via upregulation of PGC‐1α to improve cellular resilience against chronic oxidative injury—is both plausible and supported by mechanistic data from other cell types. Given that RPE cells are known to express PGC‐1α and that deficits in mitochondrial function have been implicated in AMD pathology, this provides a strong rationale for further investigation (Canovai & Williams, 2025).

Weaknesses:  
1. Limited Direct Evidence in Ocular Tissues: One of the main gaps in the literature is the paucity of direct preclinical studies involving retinal pigment epithelium or in vivo AMD models. Although there is some suggestive data from studies in retinal ganglion cells and donor‐derived RPE cells, definitive evidence that PQQ can counteract the specific aspects of dry AMD pathology in the retina is currently lacking (Ebeling et al., 2020; Petricca et al., 2025).  
2. Context‐Dependent and Biphasic Effects: Several studies indicate that PQQ’s efficacy is highly dose‐dependent. While low concentrations are cytoprotective by enhancing mitochondrial function, higher doses may exert pro‐oxidant effects and even impair mitochondrial membrane potential in sensitive cell types. Establishing the optimal dosing regimen for RPE cells will be critical to avoid potential deleterious effects (Petricca et al., 2025; Turck et al., 2017).  
3. Ocular Pharmacokinetics Uncertain: Although oral absorption in the gastrointestinal tract has been characterized in animal studies with favorable kinetics, it remains unclear whether orally administered PQQ achieves sufficient concentrations—or even penetrates ocular tissues such as the retina—to exert the desired effects. This issue of tissue‐specific bioavailability is a significant barrier that must be addressed in future pharmacokinetic studies (Hwang & Willoughby, 2018).  
4. Quality of Some Supporting Studies: While a substantial body of research supports PQQ’s bioactivity, some of the literature originates from journals or sources with variable quality. This underscores the need for additional high‐quality, rigorously controlled studies—particularly focused on ocular models—to validate the proposed mechanisms and therapeutic outcomes for dry AMD (Akagawa, Nakano, & Ikemoto, 2016; Hwang & Willoughby, 2018).

In conclusion, based on the available biomedical, clinical, and biochemical literature, PQQ emerges as a promising repurposed candidate for dry AMD. Its well‐documented ability to modulate mitochondrial biogenesis—principally through the activation of CREB and upregulation of PGC‐1α—aligns with the mechanistic requirements for restoring mitochondrial function in RPE cells under chronic oxidative stress, a central feature of dry AMD pathogenesis (Petricca et al., 2025; Charrier et al., 2024; Canovai & Williams, 2025). Moreover, the favorable safety profile indicated by both animal toxicology studies and human supplementation trials, combined with its oral bioavailability, adds significant translational appeal.

However, critical gaps remain. Principal among these is the need for direct evidence from retinal models. Specifically, robust, controlled preclinical studies in RPE cells and animal models of dry AMD must be performed to confirm that PQQ can effectively upregulate mitochondrial biogenesis and confer cytoprotection in ocular tissues. Additionally, further investigation into the optimal dosing strategy is required, particularly given the context‐dependent and potentially biphasic nature of PQQ’s activity, as well as the necessity to ascertain whether oral administration can yield therapeutic concentrations in the retina.

Given these considerations, the recommendation is to advance PQQ as a candidate for further investigation in dry AMD. This progression should involve a series of well‐designed in vitro and in vivo experiments targeting RPE‐specific pathways, coupled with ocular pharmacokinetic studies to evaluate tissue penetration and residence times. Should these studies provide supportive evidence, early‐phase human clinical trials focusing on retinal biomarkers and functional outcomes in dry AMD patients would be warranted.

Overall, while the current literature provides a strong mechanistic rationale and preliminary safety data supporting the repurposing of PQQ for dry AMD, it also highlights the need for targeted ocular studies to bridge the gap between systemic mitochondrial enhancements and specific retinal therapeutic benefits. With these additional investigations, PQQ could potentially emerge as a viable therapeutic option in the armamentarium against dry AMD, addressing the unmet need for treatments that restore RPE mitochondrial resilience and retard degeneration (Canovai & Williams, 2025).
