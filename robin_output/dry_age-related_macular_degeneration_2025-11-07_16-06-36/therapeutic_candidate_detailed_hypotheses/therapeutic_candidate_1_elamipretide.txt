Proposal for Elamipretide (SS-31; MedChemExpress HY-103007)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Elamipretide (SS-31; D-Arg-2,6-dimethyl-Tyr-Lys-Phe-NH2) is a synthetic, C-terminally amidated tetrapeptide belonging to the Szeto–Schiller (SS) class of mitochondria-targeted peptides, originally discovered through structure–activity optimization of alternating cationic–aromatic motifs that confer high cell permeability and strong partitioning to the negatively charged inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) enriched in cardiolipin (CL) (Szeto & Schiller, 2011; Tung et al., 2025). Early work demonstrated that an aromatic–cationic design was sufficient to drive potent mitochondrial uptake and that dimethyltyrosine (Dmt) confers radical-scavenging chemistry while improving chemical stability; subnanomolar-to-nanomolar extracellular concentrations concentrate >1 μM at the IMM, yielding robust activity under oxidative stress in diverse cell types (Szeto & Schiller, 2011). SS-31 and related SS peptides were initially identified as potent inhibitors of mitochondrial swelling and oxidative cell death in intact cells and organs, with mechanistic clues pointing to direct interactions with cardiolipin-rich membranes and mitigation of ischemia–reperfusion injury (Szeto & Schiller, 2011; Szeto & Birk, 2014).

Mechanistically, elamipretide selectively binds CL in the IMM via electrostatic anchoring of its cationic residues to anionic phosphate headgroups and hydrophobic partitioning of its aromatic residues into the bilayer interfacial region; biophysical studies (NMR and molecular dynamics simulations) and binding quantitation to model CL-containing bilayers demonstrate CL dependence and saturable effects on bilayer surface electrostatics and lipid packing without destabilizing lamellar phases (Mitchell et al., 2019; Sabbah et al., 2025). Once bound, elamipretide modulates several biophysical and biochemical properties central to mitochondrial function: it down-tunes the negative surface potential of CL-rich membranes, increases CL self-association, reduces lateral lipid diffusivity, and increases lateral packing, changes that preserve cristae architecture and respiratory supercomplex organization under stress while suppressing cytochrome c peroxidase activity and CL oxidation (Sabbah et al., 2025). Collectively, these actions improve electron transfer efficiency, stabilize oxidative phosphorylation (OxPhos) protein assemblies, reduce electron leak and reactive oxygen species (ROS), and preserve ATP synthesis capacity in dysfunctional mitochondria (Mitchell et al., 2022; Sabbah et al., 2025; Szeto & Birk, 2014). Importantly for retinal applications, elamipretide uptake is not dependent on mitochondrial membrane potential, supporting entry into depolarized or injured mitochondria—a feature often found in AMD RPE (Ji et al., 2021).

Elamipretide is therefore best classified as a CL-targeting, membrane-active mitochondrial therapeutic that acts primarily by modulating the physicochemical properties of CL-rich IMM domains and CL-dependent protein assemblies, rather than binding a single protein target, with downstream effects on respiratory supercomplex assembly, cytochrome c catalysis, electron-transport efficiency, ROS emission, and ATP production (Mitchell et al., 2019; Sabbah et al., 2025; Mitchell et al., 2022). Members of this class have been deployed across preclinical models of cardiac, skeletal muscle, renal, neurologic, and ocular dysfunction, and a substantial clinical program has tested elamipretide in primary mitochondrial disorders, cardiometabolic diseases, and ophthalmic indications including age-related macular degeneration (AMD) (Sabbah et al., 2025; Tung et al., 2025). The drug is water-soluble, with a short elimination half-life on the order of ~2–4 hours in preclinical species and humans, and is administered systemically by daily subcutaneous injection in AMD studies at a nominal 40 mg dose; topical ocular delivery has also been explored preclinically and clinically (Szeto & Birk, 2014; Lenin et al., 2023; Guimaraes et al., 2022; Ji et al., 2021).

Therapeutic History  
The SS peptide class emerged from efforts to concentrate antioxidant and mitochondrial-protective chemistry directly at the IMM through cationic–aromatic motifs that target anionic phospholipids—principally cardiolipin—enabling inhibition of mitochondrial swelling, reduction of ROS emission, and protection from reperfusion injury in intact cells and organs; SS-31 in particular was estimated to concentrate ~5,000-fold in mitochondria and to inhibit lipid peroxidation, positioning it to prevent CL peroxidation in the IMM (Szeto & Schiller, 2011). These membrane-centric mechanisms rapidly translated into broad preclinical testing across organ systems, demonstrating improvements in OxPhos efficiency, ATP production, ROS control, and preservation of mitochondrial ultrastructure (cristae) in disease and aging models (Szeto & Birk, 2014; Mitchell et al., 2022).

Ocular and retinal applications came into focus as mitochondrial dysfunction in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and photoreceptors was implicated as a driver of non-neovascular AMD, including reduced OxPhos, increased glycolytic shift, impaired mitophagy, structural cristae disorganization, and accumulation of damaged mitochondria in AMD donor RPE (Ferrington et al., 2020; Fisher, 2022; Fisher et al., 2022). In this context, elamipretide demonstrated several AMD-relevant preclinical effects. In a mouse model of dry AMD-like pathology, subcutaneous elamipretide reversed electroretinographic (ERG) deficits and improved clearance of sub-RPE deposits, corresponding to reversal of vision loss, consistent with activity at both the RPE/sub-RPE interface and photoreceptor function (Kim & Lad, 2021; Ji et al., 2021). In ApoE4 mice with diet-induced sub-RPE deposit formation linked to mitochondrial dysfunction, subcutaneous elamipretide reversed deposit formation, strengthening the mechanistic link to CL-dependent mitochondrial physiology in retinal tissues (Guimaraes et al., 2022). RPE-targeted cytoprotection has also been reported: elamipretide protected primary RPE and improved mitochondrial function in models of AMD and prevented persistent injury phenotypes induced by oxidative stressors such as hydroquinone in cultured RPE cells (Sabbah et al., 2025). Ex vivo and in vivo aging paradigms provide convergent support: in aged mice with visual impairments, elamipretide mitigated visual decline and normalized visual acuity and contrast sensitivity within two months, with delivery via topical eye drops producing effects comparable to subcutaneous injections; in RPE cultures from AMD donors, elamipretide improved RPE cell viability and mitochondrial function (Sabbah et al., 2025; Alam et al., 2020). In an RPE-specific SOD2 knockout model, daily topical elamipretide prevented RPE cell size increase consistent with reduced RPE cell death/senescence, further underscoring RPE-specific benefits (Tong et al., 2022). Additional retinal disease models support cross-indication activity: SS-31 restored visual function in a streptozotocin-induced mouse diabetic retinopathy model (Ferrington et al., 2020). Collectively, preclinical retinal evidence aligns with the hypothesized CL-centric rescue of mitochondrial function at the RPE and photoreceptor inner segments, preventing AMD-relevant cellular dysfunction and functional decline (Ji et al., 2021; Kim & Lad, 2021; Guimaraes et al., 2022).

Clinical development has included both topical and subcutaneous AMD studies. A phase I/II topical study enrolled 21 patients with diabetic macular edema (DME) and non-exudative intermediate AMD, demonstrating feasibility of ocular delivery; subsequent systemic AMD trials have been more extensively reported (Ji et al., 2021). The open-label, 24-week Phase 1 ReCLAIM study administered daily subcutaneous elamipretide (40 mg) to 40 patients with either non-central geographic atrophy (NCGA; n = 19) or high-risk drusen without GA (n = 21), reporting mean functional improvements: in the NCGA subgroup, +4.6 letters in best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), +5.4 letters in low-luminance visual acuity (LLVA), and an approximate 25-letter gain in low-luminance reading acuity (LLRA), with a slower GA growth rate than historical natural history; in the high-risk drusen subgroup, mean gains were +3.6 (BCVA), +5.6 (LLVA), and ~15 (LLRA) (Kim & Lad, 2021). Safety was favorable, with no serious adverse events and general tolerability in intermediate AMD (Ji et al., 2021). A subsequent randomized, double-masked, placebo-controlled Phase 2 trial (ReCLAIM-2) in ≥55-year-old patients with non-central GA tested daily subcutaneous elamipretide for 48 weeks; it did not meet primary endpoints on LL-BCVA and GA area, but elamipretide was associated with a 43% reduction in mean progression of total ellipsoid zone (EZ) attenuation and a 47% reduction in partial EZ attenuation versus placebo, with a post hoc analysis showing significant reductions and a higher proportion of patients achieving ≥2-line LL-BCVA gains, and a correlation between LL-BCVA change and change in total EZ attenuation (Sabbah et al., 2025). EZ attenuation is a clinically meaningful OCT marker that reflects photoreceptor integrity in mitochondria-rich inner segments and precedes GA and irreversible vision loss; thus, preservation of EZ is mechanistically consistent with the drug’s mitochondrial-protective intent and could represent an early biomarker of benefit (Sabbah et al., 2025). Additional summaries concur: the Phase 2 program reported negative primary endpoints but a structural OCT signal (slowed EZ degradation), warranting confirmatory studies with longer duration and functional correlates (Tung et al., 2025).

Beyond AMD, elamipretide has undergone extensive clinical evaluation in mitochondrial disorders (e.g., Barth syndrome), heart failure, ischemia–reperfusion contexts, and renal microvascular dysfunction, with mixed outcomes: many trials did not meet primary endpoints but reported improvements in mitochondrial bioenergetics, coupling, or patient-reported quality-of-life metrics, consistent with a class effect of improving mitochondrial physiology in vivo (Sabbah et al., 2025; Tung et al., 2025). Pharmacokinetically, elamipretide exhibits rapid distribution/elimination with elimination half-lives of ~2 hours in preclinical species and on the order of ~4 hours in humans, renal excretion of parent/metabolites, and ocular pharmacology indicating that topical dosing in rabbits achieves high anterior-chamber and periocular concentrations with nonzero penetration to the retina; this, together with murine aged-vision data, suggests both topical and systemic routes are viable for retinal targeting, although intraocular/vitreous levels after topical dosing may be low (Szeto & Birk, 2014; Alam et al., 2020).

Mechanism of Action  
Elamipretide’s mechanism centers on high-affinity interaction with cardiolipin in the IMM and consequent modulation of membrane electrostatics, lipid organization, and CL-dependent protein complexes, leading to improved electron transport, reduced ROS production, and preserved ATP synthesis. Several convergent lines of evidence define this mechanism in molecular detail.

1) Cardiolipin-dependent binding and membrane electrostatics. SS-31/ELAM partitions into the interfacial leaflet of CL-containing bilayers with affinity proportional to surface charge, binding via electrostatic interactions of its basic residues with CL phosphates and via hydrophobic interactions of its aromatic residues with lipid acyl chains; no measurable binding occurs to neutral phosphatidylcholine membranes (Mitchell et al., 2019). NMR and molecular dynamics identify two binding modes—“peptide approach” (electrostatic anchoring) and “peptide burial” (aromatic partition)—producing a controlled down-tuning of the negative surface potential, increased CL self-association, and altered lateral packing/diffusivity without disrupting lamellar bilayer integrity (Sabbah et al., 2025). The net effect is to modulate ion distribution at the membrane interface (e.g., divalent cations such as Ca2+), stabilize bilayer structure under electrochemical stress, and resist electroporation-like damage, thereby preserving IMM properties under stress (Mitchell et al., 2019; Tamucci et al., 2025). These membrane-physical changes occur without altering the transmembrane potential per se, consistent with a surface–electrostatics mechanism that adjusts protein–lipid interactions and lipid–lipid organization (Sabbah et al., 2025).

2) Respiratory complexes, supercomplexes, and cytochrome c. Cardiolipin stabilizes respiratory complexes and promotes assembly of supercomplexes to optimize electron transport; elamipretide binding to CL supports supercomplex assembly, increases coupling efficiency, and reduces electron leak and ROS while enhancing ATP synthesis (Mitchell et al., 2022; Sabbah et al., 2025). A central node is the CL–cytochrome c interface: elamipretide is proposed to modulate the hydrophobic/π–π interactions between cytochrome c and CL, promoting efficient electron transfer from complex III to complex IV at the rate-limiting step while inhibiting cytochrome c peroxidase activity that otherwise peroxidizes CL and destabilizes cristae under oxidative stress (Szeto & Birk, 2014). Biochemical studies show that elamipretide reduces cytochrome c peroxidase activity and prevents oxidative modifications of CL acyl chains by forming a complex with CL, thereby protecting membrane integrity (Sabbah et al., 2025). In failing human heart tissue ex vivo, elamipretide improved supercomplex coupling control, respiratory control ratio, and CI/CIV activities, consistent with a supercomplex-stabilizing activity that enhances OXPHOS efficiency (Sabbah et al., 2025).

3) Direct protein interactions and bioenergetic consequences. Proteomics using biotinylated elamipretide (b-ELAM) in cross-linking/MS identified binding to multiple IMM proteins that themselves interact with CL, including complex IV subunit NDUA4 and the adenine nucleotide translocase ANT1; functionally, elamipretide increased maximum uncoupled and CIV respiration and decreased H2O2 production in mitochondria from old mouse hearts, increased ADP sensitivity and uptake via ANT, reduced ANT1-mediated proton leak, stabilized membrane potential, and reduced mitochondrial permeability transition pore opening (Sabbah et al., 2025). Stabilization of larger IMM protein assemblies such as the ATP synthasome (ANT, ATP synthase, creatine kinase, phosphate carrier) has been reported, with improvements in CI–CIII–CIV coupling and oxygen flux in failing human heart tissue (Sabbah et al., 2025). These molecular effects translate to rapid increases in mitochondrial energetic capacity—including a human randomized placebo-controlled trial demonstrating that a single dose of elamipretide increased the maximal rate of ATP production (ATPmax) in skeletal muscle—validating in vivo bioenergetic rescue (Sabbah et al., 2025).

4) ROS control, IMM integrity, and cristae structure. Across cell and organ models, elamipretide reduces ETS-derived ROS without acting as a primary radical scavenger; instead it optimizes respiratory efficiency, reduces electron leak, and protects CL from peroxidation, breaking the cycle of cristae disruption and respiratory dysfunction prevalent in aged or diseased mitochondria (Sabbah et al., 2025; Mitchell et al., 2022). SS-31 lowers intracellular ROS, prevents mitochondrial depolarization, maintains ΔΨm, prevents lipid peroxidation, and curtails oxidative cell death in a dose-dependent manner (Mitchell et al., 2019). In retinal contexts, elamipretide increased mitochondrial cristae density and preserved ultrastructure in aged mouse retinal mitochondria and diabetic mouse retinopathy models, supporting retinal applicability (Sabbah et al., 2025). In multiple disease models, elamipretide increased mitochondrial density and cristae connectivity, normalized proton leak, improved respiratory capacity, and corrected dysregulated fusion/fission and biogenesis regulators after longer treatment, further indicating structural remodeling capacity (Sabbah et al., 2025).

Mechanistic relevance to AMD and RPE is strong. AMD donor RPE exhibits cristae disorganization, accumulation of damaged mitochondria, decreased OxPhos, impaired mitophagy, and significant mtDNA damage in ETC gene regions, all linked to diminished ATP production and a glycolytic shift that disrupts metabolic coupling with photoreceptors and promotes retinal degeneration (Ferrington et al., 2020; Fisher et al., 2022; Fisher, 2022). Because CL is crucial for maintaining OxPhos function and cristae architecture, pharmacologic stabilization of CL and CL-dependent protein assemblies by elamipretide directly addresses these pathologies, offering preservation of ATP production, reduction of ROS, and resistance to mPTP opening and apoptosis in RPE under chronic oxidative stress (Fisher et al., 2022; Sabbah et al., 2025; Szeto & Birk, 2014). Photoreceptors are mitochondria-rich in the inner segments, and OCT-defined EZ attenuation is an early marker of photoreceptor compromise that precedes GA; elamipretide-associated reduction in EZ attenuation progression in AMD patients links its mitochondrial mechanism to clinically observable retinal structure (Sabbah et al., 2025).

Expected Effect  
In RPE cell assays that model AMD-relevant stressors (oxidative and bioenergetic), elamipretide is expected to stabilize IMM biophysics and CL-dependent protein systems, restoring mitochondrial bioenergetics, reducing oxidative injury, and preventing apoptosis. Mechanistically supported expectations include:

- Restoration of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) and coupling: Elamipretide maintains ΔΨm and improves respiratory control, consistent with increased OXPHOS efficiency and reduced proton leak; thus, assays using potentiometric dyes (e.g., JC-1) should show improved ΔΨm relative to oxidant-injured controls (Mitchell et al., 2019; Sabbah et al., 2025).

- Reduction of mitochondrial ROS and lipid peroxidation: By stabilizing CL, optimizing electron transport, and inhibiting cytochrome c peroxidase activity, elamipretide reduces H2O2 emission and lipid peroxidation, leading to lower MitoSOX and lipid oxidation signals under oxidative challenge (Sabbah et al., 2025; Mitchell et al., 2019).

- Preservation of ATP synthesis: In serum-deprivation or oxidant-injury paradigms in RPE cell lines (e.g., ARPE-19), mitochondria-targeted peptides including elamipretide increased cell viability and cellular ATP at nanomolar dosing; this is consistent with expected improvements in ATP content and Seahorse OXPHOS parameters in primary RPE under stress (Mitchell et al., 2021). Human AMD donor RPE exposed ex vivo to elamipretide show improved mitochondrial function and viability, corroborating translatability of these endpoints (Sabbah et al., 2025).

- Cytoprotection against AMD-relevant toxicants: Elamipretide prevents persistent injury phenotypes induced by oxidative stressors (e.g., hydroquinone) in cultured RPE, supporting generalizability to sodium iodate or H2O2 injury; in an RPE SOD2 knockout model, topical elamipretide prevented cell size increase consistent with reduced cell death/senescence (Sabbah et al., 2025; Tong et al., 2022).

- Structural preservation of cristae: Electron microscopy of retinal mitochondria in aged/diabetic mice treated with elamipretide indicates increased cristae density and improved ultrastructure; similar effects are expected in RPE mitochondrial ultrastructure under chronic oxidative stress (Sabbah et al., 2025).

- In vivo retinal/RPE endpoints: In mouse models of AMD-like pathology, elamipretide improved ERG deficits and enhanced clearance of sub-RPE deposits, tracking to improvements in photoreceptor function and RPE health; ERG, OCT (drusen/sub-RPE deposit metrics), and RPE morphometrics are therefore expected to improve (Kim & Lad, 2021; Ji et al., 2021; Guimaraes et al., 2022). In aged-vision paradigms, spatial vision and contrast sensitivity improved with systemic or topical elamipretide, indicating photopic function benefits and supporting cone function endpoints (Alam et al., 2020).

- Clinical correlates: In patients with intermediate AMD or non-central GA, improvements in LLVA/LLRA and slowing of EZ attenuation were observed; therefore, clinical PoC studies should prioritize EZ attenuation as a primary structural endpoint with LL-BCVA/LLVA and reading measures as functionally sensitive secondaries, acknowledging that GA area may not be sensitive over ≤1 year (Kim & Lad, 2021; Sabbah et al., 2025; Tung et al., 2025). Given that photoreceptor inner segments are mitochondria-rich, EZ integrity serves as a proximal clinical biomarker of the drug’s mitochondrial mechanism (Sabbah et al., 2025).

Expression and target rationale in RPE: The target of elamipretide is not a single protein but the CL-rich IMM domain; CL is ubiquitous and crucial for maintaining OxPhos function, cristae architecture, and supercomplex assembly in RPE mitochondria, which are compromised in AMD (Fisher et al., 2022; Ferrington et al., 2020). Deficits in mitophagy (reduced PINK1/Parkin responsiveness), increased fission and delayed fusion, and global OxPhos reduction in AMD donor RPE define a mitochondrial fragility state in which CL stabilization is expected to be beneficial (Fisher et al., 2022). Because elamipretide uptake is not dependent on ΔΨm, it can access depolarized/dysfunctional mitochondria in AMD RPE, positioning it to act upstream of apoptosis (Ji et al., 2021).

Overall Evaluation  
Elamipretide presents a coherent, mechanistically grounded, and translationally explored therapeutic candidate for non-neovascular AMD predicated on CL-centric rescue of mitochondrial function in RPE and photoreceptors. The totality of evidence spans molecular biophysics, mitochondrial proteomics, in vivo bioenergetics, preclinical retinal disease models, and controlled clinical imaging and functional outcomes, allowing a sober assessment of strengths and residual risks.
