Proposal for Kinetin riboside (Carbosynth #KT0012)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Kinetin riboside (KR; N6-furfuryladenosine) is an adenine-derived cytokinin riboside originally linked to the discovery of kinetin as a plant hormone from autoclaved herring sperm DNA, and later detected in plant matrices such as coconut water, highlighting its occurrence in natural systems beyond synthetic origins (Yong et al., 2009; Gorincioi, 2010). Evidence from biochemical studies suggests that KR can also form in mammalian systems via oxidative chemistry: hydroxyl radical-induced deoxyribose oxidation generates furfural, which reacts with adenine/adenosine to yield N6-furfuryl adducts including kinetin and its riboside, supporting its characterization as a product of cellular oxidative metabolism and a potential “free radical sink” (Gorincioi, 2010). As a pharmacological entity, KR sits within the cytokinin riboside class, where the riboside and its phosphorylated triphosphate (KTP) function as ATP analogues/substrates for specific protein kinases, most notably PINK1, thereby enabling medicinal chemistry strategies around nucleotide prodrugs (e.g., monophosphate ProTides) to enhance intracellular delivery and activation (Lambourne & Mehellou, 2018). Cytokinin ribosides have been explored historically as anti-proliferative agents and differentiation inducers in oncology, and more recently as modulators of mitochondrial quality control and neuroprotective pathways through PINK1 activation (Lambourne & Mehellou, 2018; Lambourne et al., 2023; Voller et al., 2010).

Therapeutic History  
Cytokinins and their ribosides have a long history of investigation in dermatology and oncology, with kinetin demonstrating favorable skin tolerability and anti-photoaging benefits in clinical skin-care contexts, although these data pertain largely to kinetin base rather than KR (Fathy et al., 2022). KR and related ribosides have been extensively profiled as anticancer agents, exhibiting cell cycle modulation and pro-apoptotic activity in diverse tumor lines (e.g., HL-60, HeLa, B16F-10), often with relative sparing of normal fibroblasts at active concentrations, supporting a degree of tumor selectivity (Gorincioi, 2010; Voller et al., 2010). Reported in vivo and in vitro data indicate significant antiproliferative potency at low micromolar concentrations alongside dose-related toxicity (e.g., ~25 mg/kg toxicity in certain models), underscoring a narrow therapeutic index in oncology paradigms (Gorincioi, 2010). Beyond oncology, KR (and related cytokinins) have been investigated for neuroprotective and immunomodulatory effects, including improvement of splicing defects in familial dysautonomia and potential roles in neurodegenerative disease models, although much of the splicing literature historically focuses on kinetin base, and translational clarity for KR per se varies across reports (Fathy et al., 2022; Oshchepkov et al., 2020; Voller et al., 2017).

For PINK1 biology specifically, medicinal chemistry and cell biological studies demonstrate that KR and its phosphorylated metabolites activate PINK1 in mammalian and neuronal cells, positioning this chemotype as a pharmacological tool for augmenting mitophagy in diseases linked to mitochondrial dysfunction such as Parkinson’s disease; more potent KR analogs (e.g., MTK458) further validate this mechanism in cellular and animal models (Hertz et al., 2024; Lambourne & Mehellou, 2018; Lambourne et al., 2023). Prodrug strategies using kinetin riboside monophosphate (KMP) ProTides improve serum stability and intracellular delivery, yielding robust PINK1 activation in HEK293 cells, which is germane to drug design considerations for KR and derivatives (Lambourne & Mehellou, 2018). To our knowledge, there are no published clinical or preclinical studies directly testing KR in dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) patients or AMD animal models; however, the target pathway (PINK1/Parkin mitophagy) is mechanistically implicated in RPE degeneration in dry AMD, thus providing a strong rationale for repurposing (Datta et al., 2023; Hyttinen et al., 2018; Skeie et al., 2021).

Mechanism of Action  
- PINK1/Parkin mitophagy axis and AMD relevance in RPE:  
  Upon loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm), full-length PINK1 accumulates on the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM), where it phosphorylates both Parkin and ubiquitin to initiate Parkin-dependent mitophagy (Skeie et al., 2021). In RPE, PINK1 and Parkin form the core of a quality-control module that identifies and ubiquitinates dysfunctional mitochondria, recruiting autophagy machinery (p62/LC3) for organelle turnover; cumulative evidence supports that age and stress compromise this system in AMD, resulting in damaged mitochondria, elevated ROS, and RPE degeneration (Hyttinen et al., 2018; Skeie et al., 2021). Human and mouse RPE data show that PINK1 loss reduces activated phospho-Parkin (p-PRKN) and blunts mitophagic responses to depolarizing stimuli such as CCCP; conversely, in controls, CCCP elevates MAP1LC3B lipidation, validating PINK1 dependence for mitophagy activation in RPE (Datta et al., 2023). Early AMD human RPE exhibits decreased PINK1 in perifoveal regions, correlating with impaired mitophagy and providing a direct disease-context linkage between PINK1 deficiency and RPE dysfunction (Datta et al., 2023). Studies in AMD-relevant oxidative-stress mouse models (e.g., nfe2l2/pgc-1α−/−) indicate early activation of PINK1/Parkin signaling (e.g., increased Parkin colocalization with pS65 ubiquitin and accumulation of LC3B-positive autophagosomes) but suggest a downstream impairment in autolysosomal clearance, implying that upstream activation alone may not normalize flux under chronic stress (Gurubaran et al., 2020).

- KR → KTP conversion and direct PINK1 activation:  
  KR exerts its PINK1-activating effect primarily through metabolic conversion to kinetin riboside triphosphate (KTP), an ATP neo-substrate that binds PINK1 with higher affinity than ATP, thereby enhancing the kinase’s catalytic activity on relevant substrates (Georgakopoulos et al., 2017; Lambourne & Mehellou, 2018). Cellular enzymology supports that cytosolic nucleotide salvage and phosphoribosylation pathways enable the conversion of adenine derivatives (including kinetin) to triphosphate forms; APRT has been implicated in cytokinin biochemistry in mammalian systems and may contribute to the intracellular generation of KTP from kinetin precursors, though the precise enzymatic steps from KR to KTP in RPE require direct elucidation (Naseem et al., 2020). Pharmacological studies show that KR, KMP, and KTP activate PINK1 in mammalian and neuronal cells; KMP ProTides with various ester masks demonstrate serum stability and intracellular unmasking to yield active nucleotides, suggesting that glycosylation to the riboside and subsequent phosphorylation are key determinants of PINK1 activation (Lambourne & Mehellou, 2018; Lambourne et al., 2023). Stabilized PINK1 then phosphorylates Parkin at serine 656 (as reported in the source text) and ubiquitin at serine 65, which accelerates Parkin recruitment and amplifies ubiquitin chain phosphorylation on OMM substrates, thereby seeding mitophagosome formation and progression (Lambourne et al., 2023). In cell systems, kinetin-derived activation of PINK1 is observed in the context of mitochondrial membrane depolarization and can be potentiated by mild, reversible uncoupling in some chemical series without overt mitochondrial toxicity when carefully tuned, underscoring a feasible pharmacological window (Lambourne & Mehellou, 2018). Complementary small molecules (e.g., niclosamide, T0466/T0467, BC1464) act via PINK1 stabilization or degradation-pathway modulation, and USP30 inhibition further biases mitochondrial ubiquitination toward mitophagy, situating KR in a broader druggable landscape for PINK1-pathway enhancement (Masaldan et al., 2022).

- Convergence on RPE pathobiology:  
  Given that PINK1 expression and signaling are reduced in early AMD RPE, an agent that increases PINK1 catalytic activity and stabilizes PINK1 on damaged mitochondria could restore Parkin-dependent tagging and clearance of dysfunctional organelles, thereby lowering ROS loads and downstream RPE stress (Datta et al., 2023; Hyttinen et al., 2018). The RPE expresses and utilizes the PINK1/Parkin/LC3 autophagy machinery, as evidenced by immunochemical and functional data linking p-PRKN regulation, LC3B lipidation, and mitophagic flux to PINK1 state in mouse and human RPE systems (Datta et al., 2023). However, AMD models with oxidative stress show that autophagosome accumulation can occur without efficient clearance if lysosomal function is compromised; thus, any PINK1-activating therapy may need to be co-optimized with strategies that maintain autophagosome–lysosome fusion and lysosomal competence to realize net mitochondria turnover (Gurubaran et al., 2020).

Expected Effect  
- In RPE assays:  
  KR should increase PINK1 activity upon mitochondrial depolarization or basal damage, resulting in higher levels of phospho-ubiquitin (pS65), increased Parkin recruitment to the OMM, augmented polyubiquitin decoration of OMM proteins (e.g., VDAC), enhanced LC3B-II accumulation, and increased formation of mitophagosomes and subsequent mitolysosomes, assuming lysosomal function is intact (Lambourne et al., 2023; Skeie et al., 2021). In PINK1-proficient RPE, a KR challenge following CCCP or physiological stressors is expected to recapitulate or potentiate the canonical PINK1/Parkin mitophagy cascade; notably, experiments in mouse RPE indicate that CCCP-induced elevation of LC3B lipidation is abrogated in pink1−/−, establishing a dependence of the autophagy readout on PINK1 that KR could rescue only if sufficient PINK1 is present (Datta et al., 2023). Given early AMD’s reduced PINK1 expression in the RPE perifovea, KR’s effect size may scale with residual PINK1 levels; the KTP–PINK1 interaction may partially compensate by enhancing the activity and dwell time of the remaining PINK1 on depolarized mitochondria, but absolute PINK1 scarcity could limit maximal response (Datta et al., 2023; Georgakopoulos et al., 2017). In AMD-like oxidative stress settings where the early mitophagy steps activate but autolysosomal clearance is impaired, KR is predicted to elevate upstream markers (pUb, Parkin localization, LC3B-II) yet may not fully normalize flux without restoring lysosomal function; this risk of autophagosome accumulation has been observed in AMD-model RPE, implying combination approaches (e.g., lysosome enhancers) may be required for optimal benefit (Gurubaran et al., 2020).

- In dry AMD disease biology:  
  By accelerating removal of dysfunctional mitochondria, KR is anticipated to reduce mitochondrial ROS and downstream oxidative damage, restoring bioenergetic homeostasis and decreasing RPE stress signals that contribute to drusen-associated pathology and cell death in dry AMD (Brooks et al., 2023; Hyttinen et al., 2018). Mechanistically, successful PINK1 activation should rebalance the population of healthy versus damaged mitochondria, mitigate chronic ROS-driven damage, and potentially stabilize RPE cellular functions (e.g., phagocytic capacity, lysosomal turnover of photoreceptor outer segments) that are compromised in AMD due to accumulation of dysfunctional organelles (Brooks et al., 2023; Hyttinen et al., 2018). Because the PINK1/Parkin axis is implicated in RPE degeneration and is downregulated in early AMD, targeted pharmacological enhancement of PINK1 represents a pathway-corrective approach aligned with disease mechanism, though it must be tested under AMD-relevant stressors that include both mitochondrial and lysosomal deficits (Datta et al., 2023; Gurubaran et al., 2020).

Therapeutic History—Safety, Toxicology, and Translational Considerations  
Nonclinical safety evaluations of kinetin (base) indicate a dose-dependent profile: low doses show favorable tolerability in skin applications and animal studies, whereas higher systemic doses (e.g., ≥1 mg/kg in rats) may perturb renal biomarkers, highlighting the need for careful exposure control; while this is not KR, it provides a class-level caution on dosing margins (Fathy et al., 2022; Othman et al., 2021). In mammalian cell systems, kinetin shows a concentration-dependent duality—sub-100 nM levels are cytoprotective against oxidative stress, while ≥500 nM can elicit cytotoxicity and genotoxicity—again reflecting the necessity of tight pharmacological windows in clinical development; KR shares pro-apoptotic properties at higher concentrations in tumor cells, indicating that exposure management will be critical to avoid off-target mitochondrial stress in non-malignant RPE (Gorincioi, 2010; Naseem et al., 2020). Tumor-selective cytotoxicity observed for KR (e.g., activation of caspase-3 following mitochondrial membrane disruption in HeLa and B16F-10) suggests that normal cells may be relatively resistant at appropriate concentrations, but translation to post-mitotic, metabolically active RPE requires dedicated therapeutic index definition (Gorincioi, 2010).

Pharmacokinetics for cytokinin ribosides present challenges, including short plasma half-life and potential limitations in barrier penetration; nevertheless, certain ribosides can achieve tissue exposure, and ProTide engineering of monophosphate forms has demonstrated improved serum stability and intracellular activation, offering a medicinal chemistry route to optimize ocular delivery and target engagement (Lambourne & Mehellou, 2018; Voller et al., 2017). Importantly, the mitochondrial pharmacology dimension requires that PINK1 activation not rely on sustained pathological depolarization; prior work shows that PINK1 activation by certain KR-derived chemotypes can be coupled to reversible membrane effects without lasting mitochondrial injury, implying an achievable safety profile with rational chemistry and dosing (Lambourne & Mehellou, 2018). Finally, the target pathway-level success with more potent PINK1 activators (e.g., MTK458) in cellular and animal models of Parkinson’s supports the druggability of this axis and suggests headroom to optimize KR analogs if KR itself proves insufficient in the RPE context (Hertz et al., 2024).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
- Strong mechanistic alignment with AMD pathophysiology in RPE: Dry AMD features impaired mitophagy and mitochondrial dysfunction in RPE, and early AMD shows decreased PINK1 expression and diminished mitophagy competence; KR directly activates PINK1, enhances Parkin recruitment via ubiquitin/PRKN phosphorylation, and augments mitophagy flux in mammalian and neuronal cells, providing a targeted correction to a disease-relevant defect (Datta et al., 2023; Hyttinen et al., 2018; Lambourne et al., 2023).  
- Direct enzymatic activation via KTP: KR’s biotransformation to KTP, an ATP neo-substrate with higher affinity for PINK1, offers a plausible biochemical mechanism to boost catalytic throughput even when PINK1 protein is partially reduced, thereby potentially amplifying residual PINK1 signaling in AMD RPE (Georgakopoulos et al., 2017; Lambourne & Mehellou, 2018).  
- Drug development precedent and tool compounds: Demonstrated PINK1 activation by KR, KMP/KTP, and related chemotypes, plus successful implementation of ProTide strategies and in vivo efficacy signals with potent analogs (e.g., MTK458), de-risk the pathway and provide a roadmap for medicinal chemistry and delivery optimization (Hertz et al., 2024; Lambourne & Mehellou, 2018).  
- Disease-biology congruence of pharmacodynamic readouts: RPE models have validated the dependency of CCCP-induced LC3B lipidation and p-PRKN formation on PINK1, while AMD-relevant models show upstream activation markers (pUb, Parkin colocalization) that are rate limited by downstream clearance—clear, quantifiable biomarkers exist to track KR engagement and flux restoration in RPE (Datta et al., 2023; Gurubaran et al., 2020).

Risks and Gaps  
- PINK1 insufficiency in early AMD: The perifoveal reduction of PINK1 in human RPE may limit the magnitude of KR-mediated activation; if PINK1 levels are below a critical threshold, KTP amplification alone might not suffice, necessitating combination with approaches that increase PINK1 expression/stability (e.g., BC1464) or that act downstream (e.g., USP30 inhibition to potentiate ubiquitin signaling) (Datta et al., 2023; Masaldan et al., 2022).  
- Lysosomal/autophagic clearance deficits: Oxidative stress models indicate initiation of PINK1/Parkin mitophagy with impaired autolysosomal clearance; KR may heighten early mitophagy steps without resolving terminal flux, risking autophagosome accumulation unless lysosomal function is co-restored (e.g., via agents that enhance autophagosome–lysosome fusion or lysosomal biogenesis) (Gurubaran et al., 2020).  
- Safety window and mitochondrial stress: KR exhibits pro-apoptotic, mitochondria-targeted cytotoxicity in tumor cells and cytotoxic/genotoxic effects at higher concentrations in non-tumor cells; precise dosing that achieves PINK1 activation without excessive mitochondrial depolarization or non-specific stress in RPE must be empirically determined (Gorincioi, 2010; Naseem et al., 2020).  
- PK and delivery barriers: Cytokinin ribosides generally have short half-lives and potential distribution limitations; ocular delivery (e.g., intravitreal, suprachoroidal, periocular) and ProTide/nanoparticulate strategies may be required to achieve durable RPE exposure while minimizing systemic exposure, an aspect not yet demonstrated for KR in the AMD setting (Lambourne & Mehellou, 2018; Voller et al., 2017).  
- Lack of direct RPE/AMD in vivo efficacy data: While the mechanistic rationale is strong, KR has not been directly tested in RPE AMD models; translational viability will hinge on demonstrating improved mitophagy flux, decreased mitochondrial ROS, and RPE functional rescue in AMD-relevant models (Datta et al., 2023; Gurubaran et al., 2020).

Recommendation and Development Path  
Given the alignment between KR’s mechanism and the pathobiology of dry AMD in RPE, KR is a plausible repurposing candidate, but it should be advanced with a data-driven gating strategy that addresses target sufficiency, lysosomal function, safety window, and ocular delivery:  
- In vitro RPE assays (human primary or hPSC-derived) under AMD-relevant stress (e.g., photo-oxidative stress, lipofuscin/oxLDL exposure): test KR and KMP ProTides for dose–response effects on pUb (S65), p-PRKN, Parkin recruitment, LC3B lipidation, mitolysosome formation, mitochondrial turnover (mtDNA copy loss in mitolysosomes), and mitochondrial ROS/bioenergetics, benchmarking against CCCP controls and PINK1 knockdown/knockout lines (Datta et al., 2023; Lambourne & Mehellou, 2018; Skeie et al., 2021).  
- Lysosomal competence assays: co-assess autophagic flux with lysosomal pH and cathepsin activity; if KR increases early mitophagy markers without flux completion, evaluate combination with lysosome enhancers or TFEB agonists to restore throughput (Gurubaran et al., 2020).  
- PINK1 sufficiency and sensitization: quantify PINK1 levels in AMD-like conditions and test whether KR effects scale with PINK1 expression; consider combination with agents that stabilize PINK1 (e.g., BC1464) or increase mitochondrial ubiquitination via USP30 inhibition to offset low PINK1 (Datta et al., 2023; Masaldan et al., 2022).  
- Safety and therapeutic window in RPE: map KR exposure–response for PINK1 activation versus cytotoxicity/apoptosis, including concentrations that preserve RPE viability and mitochondrial membrane potential; incorporate off-target stress readouts (e.g., CHOP, ISR activation) (Gorincioi, 2010; Naseem et al., 2020).  
- Medicinal chemistry and delivery: prioritize KMP ProTides with favorable stability and reversible mitochondrial engagement to minimize off-target depolarization; assess ocular pharmacokinetics and RPE distribution in relevant species, given cytokinin riboside PK liabilities (Lambourne & Mehellou, 2018; Voller et al., 2017).  
- In vivo models: test KR/ProTide in AMD-relevant models exhibiting RPE mitochondrial defects (e.g., NFE2L2/PGC-1α pathway disruption, photo-oxidative stress paradigms), with endpoints spanning RPE ultrastructure, mitochondrial turnover, oxidative markers, and functional readouts (ERG, RPE phagocytosis), while monitoring safety (renal/hepatic markers given systemic tox precedent) (Gurubaran et al., 2020; Othman et al., 2021).

Overall Evaluation  
Kinetin riboside is a mechanistically credible, pathway-directed candidate for dry AMD that targets a well-established deficit—PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy—in RPE, with robust supportive biology that PINK1 activation can be pharmacologically achieved by KR and its nucleotide metabolites to promote Parkin-dependent mitochondrial clearance (Datta et al., 2023; Hyttinen et al., 2018; Lambourne et al., 2023). The strengths include disease-relevant target engagement, availability of medicinal chemistry tactics (KMP ProTides) to address delivery and stability, and quantifiable pharmacodynamic markers in RPE that predict on-pathway efficacy (Lambourne & Mehellou, 2018; Skeie et al., 2021). The principal risks are biological (insufficient PINK1 in early AMD and lysosomal flux defects that may cap benefit), pharmacological (narrow window between desired PINK1 activation and mitochondrial stress), and translational (PK/ocular delivery), none of which are insurmountable but all of which require rigorous preclinical validation in RPE-centric systems (Datta et al., 2023; Gurubaran et al., 2020; Naseem et al., 2020; Voller et al., 2017).

On balance, KR merits advancement to target-validation studies in human RPE models with concurrent optimization of ProTide prodrugs and combination strategies that secure lysosomal flux, with a clear go/no-go framework based on restoration of mitophagy flux and mitochondrial function without eliciting RPE toxicity (Gurubaran et al., 2020; Lambourne & Mehellou, 2018). If KR proves insufficient alone due to PINK1 insufficiency or PK constraints, more potent or targeted PINK1 activators (e.g., MTK458-class) and/or adjuncts that stabilize PINK1 or enhance downstream ubiquitin signaling may be prioritized, preserving the same mechanistic rationale for treating dry AMD via restoration of mitochondrial quality control in the RPE (Hertz et al., 2024; Masaldan et al., 2022).
