Proposal for Nicotinamide riboside (NR; ChromaDex NR-100)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Nicotinamide riboside (NR) is a naturally occurring vitamin B3–class nucleoside and a leading NAD⁺ precursor that has been isolated from natural sources such as cow milk and other dietary materials, and is now produced by synthetic chemical means for therapeutic applications (Biță et al., 2023). NR belongs to the class of NAD⁺‐precursor nucleosides within the broader family of vitamin B3 compounds, the same family that includes nicotinic acid (NA) and nicotinamide (NAM), which historically have been used to prevent pellagra and improve cellular energy metabolism (Pîrvu et al., 2021; Biță et al., 2023). The synthetic production of NR involves the glycosylation of nicotinamide with appropriately protected ribofuranose donors via methods that require careful control due to the labile and reactive nature of its glycosidic bond, which often results in partial degradation during synthesis and storage (Biță et al., 2023; Mehmel et al., 2020). In order to overcome these issues, researchers have developed alternative stabilized forms such as NR borate, although the commercially available ChromaDex NR-100 is typically presented as the crystalline chloride salt (NRCl) and has become a benchmark for purity due to its improved manufacturing protocols and rigorous quality control (Biță et al., 2023; Mehmel et al., 2020). Additionally, NR’s classification as an “orally bioavailable” NAD⁺ precursor distinguishes it from other precursors such as NMN by virtue of its ability to directly cross cell membranes without requiring extracellular conversion, with transport mediated by equilibrative nucleoside transporters (Mehmel et al., 2020; Biță et al., 2023). Taken together, these attributes place NR in a unique position as a repurposable compound with potential applications in metabolic, neurodegenerative, and now ocular diseases, including the dry forms of age-related macular degeneration (AMD) characterized by geographic atrophy (Biță et al., 2023; Braidy et al., 2019).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, the use of vitamin B3 derivatives such as NA and NAM has been well established in both clinical and biochemical practice for the improvement of energy metabolism and for prevention of niacin deficiency diseases; however, nicotinamide riboside represents a more recently discovered alternative that offers superior pharmacokinetic profiles and fewer side effects such as flushing or liver toxicity (Braidy et al., 2019; Mehmel et al., 2020). Preclinical studies have shown that NR supplementation rapidly increases tissue and circulating NAD⁺ levels across a range of animal models, including mice and rats, where it has been tested for its effects on metabolic dysfunction, neurodegeneration, insulin sensitivity, and mitochondrial biogenesis (Biță et al., 2023). Numerous clinical trials have demonstrated the safety and oral bioavailability of NR in healthy human subjects and in populations with metabolic or neurodegenerative conditions; for example, chronic supplementation at doses up to 2 g/day has been well tolerated and has successfully elevated NAD⁺ in middle-aged and older adults (Mehmel et al., 2020; ClinicalTrials.gov, 2023). In addition to these metabolic and neuroprotective applications, NR has been investigated in various models of inflammation, organ injury, and even in oncology-related situations, where its ability to modulate cellular stress responses has proven beneficial (Biță et al., 2023). Notably, while NR has undergone extensive research in contexts such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and peripheral neuropathy, its application in ocular diseases—particularly dry AMD characterized by geographic atrophy—has been less comprehensively studied; however, emerging evidence supports the notion that NAD⁺ decline in the retina and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) may be mitigated by NAD⁺ precursors, providing a rationale for repurposing NR for dry AMD (Braidy et al., 2019). Veterinary studies and animal models have also utilized NR to stimulate mitochondrial function and improve bioenergetics in tissues with high metabolic demands, further solidifying its profile as a potent NAD⁺ booster with broad potential therapeutic applications (Biță et al., 2023).

Mechanism of Action:  
NR exerts its biological effects via a well-defined metabolic pathway that begins with its cellular uptake through equilibrative nucleoside transporters (ENT1, ENT2, and ENT4) followed by phosphorylation by nicotinamide riboside kinases (NRK1 and NRK2) in the cytosol, which convert NR to nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) (Biță et al., 2023; Braidy et al., 2019). NMN is subsequently adenylated by nicotinamide mononucleotide adenylyltransferases (NMNATs) to generate NAD⁺, thereby replenishing the intracellular NAD⁺ pool that is essential for numerous metabolic and signaling processes (Mehmel et al., 2020). The elevation of NAD⁺ levels activates a host of NAD⁺-dependent enzymes including sirtuins (SIRT1, SIRT3, among others) and poly(ADP-ribose) polymerases (PARPs), which are critical for deacetylating mitochondrial proteins, enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis, and facilitating DNA repair processes both in the nucleus and mitochondria (Biță et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2016). Activation of SIRT3 in particular within the mitochondria leads to the deacetylation and activation of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2), thereby reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) and mitigating oxidative stress (Zhang et al., 2020; Jadeja et al., 2020). Moreover, increased NAD⁺ availability supports the activity of PARP enzymes that are required for efficient DNA repair, including the repair of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) damage arising from oxidative stress (Cimaglia et al., 2020). In RPE cells, this mechanism is particularly relevant as the high-energy–demand environment and frequent exposure to light-induced oxidative stress make robust mitochondrial and genomic maintenance critical to cell survival and homeostasis (Zhang et al., 2020). Additionally, NR’s effect on boosting NAD⁺ may facilitate the activation of autophagy and mitophagy pathways, leading to the clearance of damaged cellular components and improved cellular renewal processes (Braidy et al., 2019; ClinicalTrials.gov, 2023). Such biochemical actions converge to foster an enhanced cellular defense mechanism against oxidative damage, support mitochondrial function through improved repair and turnover, and ultimately promote cellular longevity (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2023; Biță et al., 2023).

Expected Effect:  
The central hypothesis underpinning the repurposing of NR for dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is that supplementation with NR will lead to an elevation of intracellular NAD⁺ pools specifically within retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells, thereby enhancing mitochondrial DNA repair and upregulating antioxidant defenses that mitigate the oxidative damage implicated in geographic atrophy (Biță et al., 2023). In vitro studies using iPSC-derived RPE cells from AMD donors have demonstrated that NR supplementation results in rapid increases in intracellular NAD⁺, which is accompanied by the activation of sirtuin-mediated deacetylation of key mitochondrial proteins (Jadeja et al., 2020; Mehmel et al., 2020). This deacetylation is believed to lead to improved mitochondrial function as evidenced by restored maximal oxygen consumption rate (OCR) measured via Seahorse XF assays, and the lowering of reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels as detected by fluorescent probes such as DCFDA (Zhang et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2016). The expected outcome in RPE is that the elevated NAD⁺ not only promotes the repair of oxidative damage to mitochondrial DNA but also augments the intrinsic antioxidant enzyme systems—including upregulation of enzymes like SOD2 and catalase—which collectively reduce the cellular oxidative burden (Biță et al., 2023). As a result, NR supplementation is anticipated to stabilize the RPE cell phenotype, reduce markers of cellular senescence and apoptosis, and potentially restore a healthier metabolic profile in RPE cells, factors that are critical for halting or even reversing the progression of geographic atrophy (Jadeja et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2016). Moreover, by enhancing the NAD⁺-dependent activation of PARP and other DNA repair enzymes, NR may directly improve the repair of mitochondrial and nuclear DNA damage that accumulates under chronic oxidative stress conditions prevalent in the aging retina (Braidy et al., 2019; Mehmel et al., 2020). In preclinical models of retinal degeneration such as light-induced retinal injury, systemic administration of NR has been shown to preserve retinal morphology, reduce photoreceptor apoptosis, and maintain tissue NAD⁺ levels despite strong oxidative challenges, which supports the translational rationale for its application in dry AMD (Zhang et al., 2020; Braidy et al., 2019). Ultimately, the expected therapeutic effect of NR in the treatment of geographic atrophy is to provide cytoprotection to the RPE by mitigating oxidative damage and improving mitochondrial function, thus slowing the progression of RPE degeneration and vision loss (Zhu et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2020).

Overall Evaluation:  
In evaluating NR (specifically the ChromaDex NR-100 formulation) as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry age-related macular degeneration, several strengths and promising attributes emerge alongside areas that warrant further investigation. One of the primary strengths of NR is its established reputation as a safe and orally bioavailable NAD⁺ precursor with extensive preclinical and clinical data supporting its ability to elevate NAD⁺ levels systemically and within specific target tissues (Mehmel et al., 2020). This property is particularly relevant for RPE cells because the high metabolic demands of the retina make efficient NAD⁺ replenishment crucial for maintaining mitochondrial function and for mediating effective DNA repair mechanisms (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2023; Mehmel et al., 2020). Additionally, the robust mechanistic data showing that NR supplementation activates sirtuin pathways—most notably SIRT1 and SIRT3—and subsequently deacetylates and activates key mitochondrial and antioxidant enzymes provide a compelling molecular rationale for its potential to ameliorate oxidative stress in the RPE (Biță et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2020). The activation of these pathways not only enhances mitochondrial bioenergetics but also promotes the clearance of damaged cellular components through autophagy, which is expected to contribute to improved cell survival under oxidative challenge (Braidy et al., 2019; Jadeja et al., 2020).  
However, there are some weaknesses and critical gaps in the current data when it comes to translating these beneficial effects specifically to the context of geographic atrophy in dry AMD. Although preclinical models have demonstrated the protective effects of NR in retinal degeneration (particularly in light-induced injury models), direct evidence demonstrating that NR selectively elevates NAD⁺ pools in RPE cells derived from AMD patients, enhances mitochondrial DNA repair specifically in RPE, and reduces ROS levels to a degree that meaningfully impacts the progression of geographic atrophy remains limited (Braidy et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2020). Furthermore, the chemical reactivity and inherent instability of NR have historically posed challenges to its synthesis, storage, and consistent formulation, raising concerns about batch-to-batch variability and the possibility of degradation products in commercial formulations; although recent advances and improved production standards appear to have mitigated these issues, further analytical validation is needed to ensure that the NR-100 formulation consistently meets high purity criteria (Biță et al., 2023; Mehmel et al., 2020). Another consideration is that while the mechanisms underlying NR’s beneficial effects are well established in metabolic and neurodegenerative contexts, the unique metabolic and microenvironmental characteristics of the RPE in the aging eye—including the contributions of the local immune system, extracellular matrix alterations, and the interplay with the choroidal circulation—may represent additional variables that could influence NR’s efficacy in dry AMD, and these factors are not yet fully delineated (Biță et al., 2023; Cimaglia et al., 2020).  
From a clinical development perspective, the fact that NR has already been evaluated in multiple Phase I/II clinical trials for other indications, and has demonstrated an excellent safety profile even at high doses, is a significant advantage that could accelerate its repurposing for ocular applications; careful dose-ranging studies focused on ocular bioavailability and pharmacodynamics in the context of AMD will be critical (Mehmel et al., 2020; Biță et al., 2023). Additionally, mechanistic endpoints such as NAD⁺ elevation, sirtuin activation, and improvements in mitochondrial function have been validated in other tissues and could be adapted to RPE-specific assays, including measurements of oxygen consumption rate (OCR), mitochondrial membrane potential, ROS quantification, and biomarkers of DNA repair (Zhang et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2016; Braidy et al., 2019). Moreover, early translational studies using iPSC-derived RPE from AMD donors have already begun to show that NR can reduce oxidative stress and restore metabolic function in these cells, lending credibility to its potential efficacy in geographic atrophy settings (Biță et al., 2023; Braidy et al., 2019).  
In summary, NR (ChromaDex NR-100) emerges as a highly promising repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD on the basis of its well-established ability to raise NAD⁺, activate sirtuin-mediated mitochondrial maintenance and DNA repair processes, and mitigate oxidative stress—all of which are central drivers of RPE degeneration in geographic atrophy (Zhang et al., 2020; Jadeja et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2016). The strengths of this candidate include a robust mechanistic foundation, a favorable safety and pharmacokinetic profile established in other clinical contexts, and encouraging preclinical data that indirectly support its potential benefit in the retina (Biță et al., 2023; Mehmel et al., 2020; Braidy et al., 2019). Nonetheless, significant challenges remain, particularly the need for direct evidence in RPE-specific and geographic atrophy models, confirmation of consistent formulation quality due to NR’s chemical instability, and the potential variability in tissue-specific uptake and metabolism in an aging ocular environment (Mehmel et al., 2020; Biță et al., 2023). Future studies should therefore focus on comprehensive in vitro investigations in primary and iPSC-derived RPE cells, detailed pharmacodynamic assessments in animal models of dry AMD, and ultimately carefully designed early-phase clinical trials targeting RPE function and retinal structure in patient populations with geographic atrophy (Zhang et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2016; Braidy et al., 2019).  
Overall, while there is a compelling mechanistic rationale and a solid foundation of preclinical and clinical data supporting the repurposing of NR for dry AMD, particularly in mitigating oxidative damage, enhancing mitochondrial DNA repair, and improving antioxidant defenses in RPE cells, further targeted research is required to validate these effects specifically in the context of geographic atrophy. The available literature suggests that NR has significant translational promise, and its favorable safety profile alongside extensive clinical experience in other indications positions it as a frontrunner among NAD⁺ precursors for potential therapeutic intervention in dry AMD (Biță et al., 2023; Mehmel et al., 2020). The next phase of research should therefore include detailed mechanistic studies in ocular models, combined with rigorous clinical endpoint evaluations to determine whether NR supplementation can effectively slow or reverse the degenerative processes underlying geographic atrophy in dry AMD (Zhang et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2016; Jadeja et al., 2020).
