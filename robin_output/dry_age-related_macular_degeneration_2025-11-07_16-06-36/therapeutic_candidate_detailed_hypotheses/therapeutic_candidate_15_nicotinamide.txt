Proposal for Nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN; Sigma-Aldrich N3501)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) is a naturally occurring nucleotide derivative that functions as a key intermediate in the biosynthesis of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+), an essential coenzyme in cellular energy metabolism and redox regulation (Chen et al., 2020). Originally identified as part of the salvage pathway of NAD+ synthesis, NMN is synthesized endogenously from nicotinamide and subsequently converted by NMN adenylyltransferase (NMNAT) into NAD+ (Gerdes, 2018; Jadeja et al., 2020). Commercially produced by chemical synthesis and available from suppliers such as Sigma-Aldrich (catalog number N3501), NMN belongs to the class of NAD+ precursors that have attracted considerable attention due to their potential to counteract the age-related decline in NAD+ levels (Chen et al., 2020). Compounds of this class have historically been explored mainly in the context of metabolic and aging interventions, with the rationale that restoring NAD+ pools may reverse or delay cellular senescence and mitochondrial dysfunction (Jadeja et al., 2018; Ren et al., 2022). NMN, as a small molecule with a molecular weight of approximately 334 g/mol, has been demonstrated to readily cross cell membranes and enhance cellular bioenergetics, making it a promising candidate for diseases in which energy metabolism is disrupted, such as dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) (Chen et al., 2020; Gerdes, 2018). The compound’s natural occurrence and favorable safety profile observed in preclinical aging models have contributed to its development as a therapeutic candidate across multiple indications, which now includes potential ophthalmological applications (Jadeja et al., 2018; Ebeling et al., 2020).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, NMN has been investigated in a variety of preclinical settings addressing metabolic dysfunction and age‐associated decline, with numerous studies across biochemical, cellular, and animal models demonstrating that NMN supplementation effectively elevates NAD+ levels and improves mitochondrial function (Chen et al., 2020). In models of retinal degeneration, particularly in contexts simulating photoreceptor loss and retinal detachment, NMN administration has shown neuroprotective effects by restoring NAD+ levels and activating key deacetylases such as SIRT1, which are central to oxidative stress mitigation and cell survival (Chen et al., 2020). Preclinical studies conducted in mouse models of retinal detachment have reported that NMN treatment not only boosts NAD+ concentrations beyond the physiological baseline but also attenuates the inflammatory response and reduces photoreceptor cell death, thereby preserving retinal structure and function (Chen et al., 2020). In addition to these acute models, experimental data from primary retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells derived from donors with age‐related macular degeneration have revealed that NMN can improve mitochondrial respiration and restore the NAD+/NADH balance critical to RPE cell health (Ebeling et al., 2020). Moreover, NMN’s ability to limit cellular senescence has been documented in RPE models challenged with oxidative stressors and pro‐senescent compounds, where supplementation led to a reduction in markers such as p16INK4a and p21Waf/Cip1, along with preservation of SIRT1 expression (Jadeja et al., 2018; Ren et al., 2022). Clinical exploration of NMN in the context of aging and metabolic disorders—though not yet fully extended to ophthalmologic diseases—provides an encouraging translational backdrop, indicating that NMN has a robust therapeutic history in conditions characterized by NAD+ depletion and mitochondrial dysfunction (Jadeja et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2024). Collectively, the therapeutic history suggests that NMN has been thoroughly vetted in preclinical settings relevant to neurodegeneration and retinal health, thereby supporting its potential repurposing for dry AMD (Ren et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024).

Mechanism of Action:  
NMN exerts its biological effects primarily through its role as a biosynthetic precursor of NAD+, a critical coenzyme involved in numerous cellular processes including energy production, DNA repair, and redox balance (Chen et al., 2020). Once administered, NMN is rapidly taken up by cells and converted via NMNAT enzymes into NAD+, thereby replenishing intracellular NAD+ pools that typically decline with age and stress (Gerdes, 2018; Jadeja et al., 2020). This increase in NAD+ levels has a twofold impact. First, elevated NAD+ directly energizes metabolic pathways, namely glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, by serving as a cofactor for dehydrogenases, which is essential for mitochondrial ATP production (Will-Orrego, 2020; Zhang et al., 2024). Second, NAD+ acts as a substrate for a family of NAD+-dependent deacetylases—the sirtuins—most notably SIRT1, SIRT3, and SIRT5, which are involved in regulating mitochondrial integrity and function (Chen et al., 2020; Gerdes, 2018). In the specific context of dry AMD, the hypothesis posits that NMN-mediated restoration of NAD+ in retinal pigment epithelial cells leads to reactivation of mitochondrial sirtuins SIRT3 and SIRT5. SIRT3, located in the mitochondrial matrix, deacetylates key metabolic enzymes including manganese superoxide dismutase (MnSOD), whose activity is crucial for neutralizing mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) (Gerdes, 2018; Ren et al., 2022). The proper deacetylation of MnSOD enhances its antioxidant capacity, thereby reducing mtROS accumulation and protecting the mitochondria from oxidative damage (Chen et al., 2020; Ren et al., 2022). SIRT5, another mitochondrial deacetylase, plays a complementary role by regulating the acylation status of proteins involved in mitochondrial dynamics and energy metabolism; its activation, in turn, contributes to improved mitochondrial respiration and bioenergetics (Ebeling et al., 2020; Gerdes, 2018). Furthermore, the elevation of NAD+ levels via NMN supplementation is known to indirectly influence other signaling pathways such as those modulated by AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and Akt/mTOR, which are critical for mitochondrial biogenesis and cellular homeostasis (Ren et al., 2022). In dry AMD, where metabolic dysfunction and increased oxidative stress are central to disease progression, the biochemical cascade triggered by NMN restores the balance between energy production and oxidative damage by ensuring that sirtuins are adequately fueled (Jadeja et al., 2018). This cascade not only preserves mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) but also limits the proton leak and bioenergetic inefficiency that characterize dysfunctional RPE cells (Ebeling et al., 2020). In summary, at the molecular level, NMN’s mechanism of action in RPE cells aligns with a strategy of metabolic reconditioning, whereby the reactivation of SIRT3 and SIRT5—via increased NAD+ availability—leads to improved deacetylation of mitochondrial enzymes, reduction in mtROS levels, and enhancement of overall mitochondrial function (Lin, 2020).

Expected Effect:  
In the context of a dry AMD model, the anticipated effect of NMN supplementation is the restoration of NAD+ homeostasis within RPE cells and consequent reactivation of mitochondrial sirtuins such as SIRT3 and SIRT5, which will correct mitochondrial deacetylation deficits that are known to contribute to cellular dysfunction (Chen et al., 2020; Gerdes, 2018). Specifically, NMN is expected to normalize the NAD+/NADH ratio, an essential parameter for efficient electron transport chain function and ATP generation; this normalization is projected to result in enhanced oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and improved mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) in the RPE (Ebeling et al., 2020). As a direct consequence of restored sirtuin activity, particularly SIRT3’s deacetylation of MnSOD, there should be a significant reduction in mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS), which in turn decreases the oxidative burden on the RPE cells—a critical factor in the development and progression of dry AMD (Gerdes, 2018; Ren et al., 2022). The expected downstream effects include not only enhanced bioenergetic function but also a reduction in the cellular senescence markers and inflammatory cytokines typically upregulated in the aging and stressed RPE (Jadeja et al., 2018; Ren et al., 2022). In vitro studies using ARPE-19 cells and primary RPE cultures have demonstrated that NMN supplementation can rescue cells from FK866-induced NAD+ depletion, thereby restoring ATP levels and maintaining the expression of RPE identity genes, which are fundamental to retinal homeostasis (Will-Orrego, 2020). Moreover, by mitigating oxidative damage and correcting mitochondrial dysfunction, NMN is expected to preserve the structural integrity of the outer retina, which is essential for sustained photoreceptor function and visual acuity in dry AMD (Chen et al., 2020). Ultimately, the mechanistic actions of NMN—spanning from NAD+ replenishment to sirtuin activation—are predicted to yield measurable improvements in both cellular assays (e.g., increased OCR, stabilized Δψm, and decreased ROS levels) and functional endpoints such as electroretinography (ERG) responses in animal models of retinal degeneration (Chen et al., 2020). The robust preclinical data, including evidence of normalized mitochondrial respiration and amelioration of RPE senescence following NMN treatment, provide a strong rationale for expecting similar beneficial effects in a dry AMD assay (Jadeja et al., 2018).

Overall Evaluation:  
The cumulative evidence from biochemical, cellular, and in vivo studies positions NMN as a promising therapeutic candidate for dry age-related macular degeneration. One of the primary strengths of NMN is its well‐characterized role as a NAD+ precursor, which underpins its ability to restore intracellular NAD+ levels and reactivate essential sirtuin activities—most notably SIRT3 and SIRT5—in metabolically compromised cells (Chen et al., 2020; Gerdes, 2018). By addressing the root cause of mitochondrial dysfunction observed in dry AMD, namely NAD+ depletion and subsequent impairment of protein deacetylation, NMN targets a fundamental pathogenic mechanism that leads to photoreceptor and RPE degeneration (Chen et al., 2020; Ren et al., 2022). The broad spectrum of supportive evidence, ranging from neuroprotective effects in retinal detachment models to the reversal of cellular senescence in AMD-relevant RPE cells, further reinforces the therapeutic rationale behind NMN’s application in dry AMD (Jadeja et al., 2018; Ren et al., 2022). Additionally, the translational feasibility of NMN is bolstered by ongoing clinical developments in aging and metabolic disorders, suggesting that the pharmacokinetic and safety profiles of NMN are sufficiently favorable to warrant its repurposing for retinal diseases (Jadeja et al., 2018).

However, several potential limitations must be considered. Variability in drug response among diseased RPE cells from different AMD donors has been documented, indicating that while NMN can robustly restore mitochondrial function in some cases, a subset of patients may exhibit a less pronounced therapeutic response, necessitating personalized dosing strategies or combinatorial therapies (Ebeling et al., 2020). Furthermore, although NMN’s activation of sirtuins is generally beneficial, there exists a theoretical risk that overactivation of these enzymes could induce off-target effects or exacerbate cellular stress if not properly titrated, as some studies have suggested that SIRT1 overactivation may in certain contexts lead to deleterious outcomes (Chen et al., 2020; Ren et al., 2022). Moreover, much of the current evidence derives from preclinical models, and while these data are compelling, the extrapolation of these results to human subjects with dry AMD will require rigorously designed clinical trials to evaluate efficacy and safety in the specific target population (Chen et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2024).

In summary, the overall assessment of NMN as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD is highly favorable, particularly given its dual capacity to restore NAD+ levels and reengage mitochondrial deacetylation pathways that are crucial for RPE health. Its mechanistic basis, rooted in the reactivation of SIRT3 and SIRT5 leading to improved mitochondrial respiration and reduced oxidative stress, directly addresses the central pathogenic features of dry AMD. The extensive preclinical validation, coupled with encouraging translational data from aging-related indications, positions NMN as a strong candidate for further development and clinical evaluation in dry AMD, albeit with careful consideration of dosing strategies and patient heterogeneity (Cimaglia et al., 2020). Moving forward, prioritizing studies that assess long-term outcomes in appropriate animal models and early-phase clinical trials in AMD patients will be critical to firmly establish NMN’s therapeutic potential and optimize its utility as part of a broader strategy to mitigate vision loss associated with dry AMD (Jadeja et al., 2020; Lin, 2020).
