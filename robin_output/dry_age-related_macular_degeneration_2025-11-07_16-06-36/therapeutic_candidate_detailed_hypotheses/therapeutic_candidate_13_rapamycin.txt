Proposal for Rapamycin (Abcam #ab120224)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Rapamycin (also known as sirolimus and available as Abcam #ab120224) is a naturally occurring macrolide compound originally isolated from Streptomyces hygroscopicus. It belongs to the class of macrocyclic lactones and is known for its potent inhibitory effects on the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1). This compound has been extensively used in various contexts such as immunosuppression, cancer therapy, and more recently, as a tool to modulate autophagy in age-related degenerative diseases. In the context of retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) dysfunction and dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD), rapamycin is being explored as a repurposed therapeutic candidate with the hypothesis that mTORC1 inhibition will enhance both general autophagy and selective mitophagy. This clearance of reactive oxygen species (ROS)–generating, damaged mitochondria may alleviate oxidative stress, reduce the accumulation of toxic lipofuscin compounds (including A2E), and ultimately slow the progression of dry AMD (Mei et al., 2022; Nita & Grzybowski, 2023).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, rapamycin was first discovered for its potent immunosuppressive properties and later developed for use in preventing transplant rejection and certain cancers. Its ability to inhibit mTORC1 has made it a valuable tool in research related to aging and neurodegeneration. Preclinical studies have demonstrated its effectiveness in modulating autophagy and protecting cellular integrity under stress conditions, particularly in the retinal environment. In animal models of retinal degeneration and even some early clinical trials, rapamycin was shown to reduce the accumulation of autofluorescent granules and inflammatory cytokines in the RPE, which are both hallmarks of dry AMD pathology (Zhang et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2022). Notably, rapamycin has been administered via diverse routes, including intravitreal and subconjunctival injections, in attempts to treat geographic atrophy or other dry AMD phenotypes, although the clinical outcomes have been mixed. For example, clinical trials such as those referenced in studies with identifiers NCT00766649 and NCT01445548 demonstrated that while rapamycin was generally safe and well-tolerated in ocular applications, its efficacy in slowing down the progression of geographic atrophy and improving visual acuity remained limited (National Eye Institute, 2008; National Eye Institute, 2011; Wang et al., 2022). These historical findings highlight both the promise and the challenges associated with translating rapamycin’s mechanistic benefits into robust clinical outcomes for dry AMD (Mei et al., 2022; Nita & Grzybowski, 2023).

Mechanism of Action:  
The primary mechanism of rapamycin involves the formation of a complex with the immunophilin FKBP12. This complex binds to mTORC1 and inhibits its kinase activity, leading to a decrease in the phosphorylation of downstream targets such as ribosomal protein S6 kinase (S6K) and the eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E-binding protein 1 (4EBP1). Under normal circumstances, active mTORC1 suppresses autophagy by phosphorylating these targets, which in turn prevents the formation of autophagosomes. By inhibiting mTORC1, rapamycin effectively removes this blockade, thereby promoting the induction of autophagy as evidenced by increased conversion of LC3-I to LC3-II and clearance of the autophagic substrate p62 (Zhang et al., 2015).

Within retinal pigment epithelial cells, this induction of autophagy is particularly important. RPE cells are responsible for the phagocytosis and degradation of photoreceptor outer segments; a process that generates large amounts of cellular debris and metabolic byproducts such as lipofuscin. In dry AMD, impaired autophagic flux leads to the accumulation of these compounds, which, in turn, contributes to oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. Rapamycin’s inhibition of mTORC1 not only enhances general autophagy but also promotes selective mitochondrial clearance (mitophagy). Key players in the mitophagy pathway include the serine/threonine kinase PINK1 and the E3 ubiquitin ligase Parkin, both of which accumulate on depolarized mitochondria and signal their removal via autophagic mechanisms. By stimulating this PINK1/Parkin pathway, rapamycin enhances the clearance of damaged, ROS-generating mitochondria, thus preserving overall mitochondrial health (Hyttinen et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2015).

Additionally, rapamycin’s downstream effects include activation of transcription factors such as TFEB, which regulates lysosomal biogenesis and further enhances the autophagic process. This dual action—promoting both autophagy and lysosomal function—helps to mitigate the accumulation of toxic aggregates such as lipofuscin and A2E in RPE, therefore reducing oxidative stress and inflammation (Falcão et al., 2024; Falcão et al., 2025). Through these biochemical interactions, rapamycin establishes a cellular environment that is more capable of dealing with metabolic challenges and oxidative damage, both of which are central to the pathogenesis of dry AMD (Chang et al., 2022; Falcão et al., 2024).

Expected Effect:  
Based on the proposed hypothesis, the administration of rapamycin in RPE cells is expected to result in a cascade of beneficial cellular events. In our proposed assay, rapamycin is anticipated to inhibit mTORC1 activity, leading to a measurable increase in autophagic flux. This will be evident from the enhanced lipidation of LC3-II, the clearance of p62 (an autophagy adaptor protein that accumulates when autophagy is impaired), and the activation of the PINK1/Parkin pathway in response to oxidative stress (Zhang et al., 2015).

The specific clearance of damaged mitochondria is particularly critical in the RPE, where mitochondrial dysfunction is a significant contributor to the generation of reactive oxygen species and subsequent cellular damage. By removing these dysfunctional mitochondria, rapamycin is expected to reduce intracellular ROS levels and thereby lower oxidative stress, a driving force in AMD pathology. In parallel, the enhanced autophagic degradation of accumulated lipofuscin and the toxic fluorophore A2E is predicted to further limit the inflammatory and apoptotic cascades triggered by these compounds, thus preserving RPE cell viability and function (Zhang et al., 2015; Falcão et al., 2025).

Moreover, the expression of PINK1 and Parkin in RPE cells has been well documented, and their activation is indicative of mitophagic turnover. Their proper functioning is a key determinant in mitochondrial quality control, and rapamycin is expected to potentiate this process, ensuring that mitochondria remain healthy and capable of maintaining cellular energy production and reducing the risk of cell death (Pinelli et al., 2025; Hyttinen et al., 2018). Given that impaired mitochondrial function and the resulting energy production deficits are central to the progression of dry AMD, the expected outcomes from rapamycin treatment would include not only a reduction in oxidative stress markers but also improvements in mitochondrial dynamics and overall cellular homeostasis. Such effects would ideally be reflected in functional assays measuring cell viability, mitochondrial respiration, and reductions in inflammatory cytokine production (Zhang et al., 2015; Falcão et al., 2025).

Overall Evaluation:  
In our comprehensive evaluation of rapamycin as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD, several strengths and potential weaknesses emerge that warrant careful consideration. One of the key strengths of rapamycin lies in its well-characterized mechanism of action and its ability to broadly modulate autophagy through mTORC1 inhibition. Preclinical studies have repeatedly demonstrated that rapamycin can induce autophagic flux, leading to the clearance of cellular debris and damaged mitochondria in RPE cells. This is particularly compelling given the central role of impaired autophagy in the accumulation of lipofuscin and A2E, which are major pathological features of dry AMD (Mei et al., 2022; Nita & Grzybowski, 2023; Hyttinen et al., 2018). The dual-action mechanism—enhancing both autophagy and lysosomal biogenesis via TFEB activation—further supports its potential in addressing multiple aspects of the disease pathology, from oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction to inflammation and cellular senescence (Falcão et al., 2024; Falcão et al., 2025).

Another strength is the substantial body of preclinical evidence showing that rapamycin not only stimulates autophagy but also improves mitochondrial function. The activation of the PINK1/Parkin pathway is particularly relevant since these proteins are indispensable for mitophagy and mitochondrial quality control. By facilitating the removal of ROS-generating, damaged mitochondria, rapamycin could directly impact the cellular energy balance and reduce damaging oxidative insults in the RPE, which is pivotal for maintaining retinal health (Hyttinen et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2015).

Despite its promising mechanistic profile, several significant challenges remain. Clinical studies investigating the use of rapamycin for AMD have yielded mixed results. While the drug has been shown to be safe and tolerable in ocular applications, several clinical trials, such as those involving subconjunctival or intravitreal administration, did not demonstrate significant efficacy in halting the progression of geographic atrophy or improving visual acuity. In some instances, adverse effects such as decreased visual acuity and retinal thinning have been observed, raising concerns about the balance between therapeutic benefit and potential retinal toxicity (National Eye Institute, 2008; National Eye Institute, 2011; Wang et al., 2022). These mixed clinical findings underscore the complexity of modulating mTORC1 in a disease with multifactorial etiology and suggest that the optimal dosage, treatment duration, and patient selection criteria still require refinement.

Furthermore, the variability in response among different donor-derived RPE cells—where only about 50% of AMD donor samples responded positively to rapamycin in certain studies—points to potential issues regarding patient heterogeneity and the need for personalized therapeutic regimens (Ebeling et al., 2020). This heterogeneity may be due to differences in the intrinsic capacity for autophagy, genetic variations affecting mitochondrial quality control, or the extent of existing cellular damage. Such variability complicates the development of standardized treatment protocols and could limit the broad applicability of rapamycin unless predictive biomarkers of response are identified (Zhang et al., 2015).

Another important consideration is the dual nature of autophagy in the context of RPE health. While enhancing autophagy can provide protective benefits by clearing damaged organelles and reducing oxidative stress, excessive autophagic flux under certain stress conditions might paradoxically contribute to cell death. This is especially relevant in advanced stages of dry AMD, where cellular stress is severe and prolonged autophagy induction might tip the balance toward detrimental outcomes rather than recovery (Chang et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2019). In addition, the potential for rapamycin to interfere with beneficial mTOR signaling under specific conditions (for example, when mTOR activity supports survival pathways via growth factors such as nerve growth factor) further complicates its use, as evidenced by reports that observed mixed effects on retinal ganglion cell survival in different pathological contexts (Wang et al., 2022; Niu et al., 2021).

The delivery method and pharmacokinetics of rapamycin also represent important variables. While nanotechnology-based delivery systems—such as synthetic high-density lipoprotein nanoparticles—have shown promise in achieving more targeted and sustained delivery of rapamycin to the RPE (Mei et al., 2022; Niu et al., 2021), these strategies require further translational studies to assess their safety, efficacy, and scalability in clinical settings. Moreover, the risk of systemic side effects, including metabolic toxicities and immunosuppression, despite the generally favorable ocular safety profile observed in trials, necessitates caution in developing rapamycin-based therapies for chronic conditions like AMD (Wang et al., 2022).

In summary, rapamycin presents a compelling therapeutic candidate for dry AMD based on its robust mechanism of action—specifically, its capacity to inhibit mTORC1, thereby stimulating both general autophagy and the selective removal of damaged mitochondria via the PINK1/Parkin pathway. Preclinical evidence strongly supports the idea that such modulation can reduce oxidative stress, clear toxic accumulations like lipofuscin/A2E, and ultimately preserve mitochondrial and RPE cellular function (Zhang et al., 2015; Hyttinen et al., 2018). However, the translation of these benefits into clinical improvements remains uncertain, with several trials indicating that while rapamycin is safe and modulates cellular pathways implicated in AMD, its clinical efficacy in terms of preserving visual function has not been conclusively demonstrated. The heterogeneity in patient responses, the potential for adverse outcomes related to excessive autophagy, and the challenges in optimizing dosing and delivery all represent significant hurdles. These considerations suggest that while rapamycin is a promising candidate worthy of further investigation, its ultimate role in the treatment paradigm for dry AMD will depend on our ability to refine treatment regimens, develop predictive biomarkers for patient selection, and perhaps combine mTOR modulation with complementary therapeutic strategies that reinforce its protective effects while mitigating its risks (Wang et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2019).

Given the cumulative evidence, our overall recommendation is to continue exploring rapamycin as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD, but with a strategic focus on further preclinical investigations that address the dose–response relationships in donor-derived RPE models and the development of advanced drug delivery systems. It will also be important to design future clinical trials with stratified patient populations and integrated biomarkers that can help identify responders. This iterative approach could eventually lead to a carefully balanced regimen where rapamycin’s autophagy-enhancing properties are harnessed to maintain retinal integrity while avoiding the pitfalls of over-inhibition or systemic toxicity. In essence, the promise of rapamycin in modulating autophagy to improve mitochondrial health and reduce oxidative stress in RPE cells is clear at the molecular and cellular levels, but translating these benefits into a safe and effective therapy for dry AMD will require overcoming substantial clinical challenges (Mei et al., 2022; Hyttinen et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2022).

Strengths of rapamycin include its well-established mechanism of action, the significant supportive preclinical data demonstrating improvements in autophagic flux and mitochondrial clearance in RPE models, and its potential to address several key pathological features of dry AMD simultaneously. Weaknesses include the mixed clinical trial outcomes to date, the potential for adverse effects in certain contexts (including decreased visual acuity and retinal thinning), and the observed heterogeneity in therapeutic response among different patient samples. These factors highlight the importance of further mechanistic studies and carefully controlled clinical trials. Overall, rapamycin remains one of the more mechanistically promising candidates for repurposing in dry AMD, yet its future success will depend on how effectively we can tailor its delivery and dosing to maximize protective effects while minimizing risks in the vulnerable RPE of AMD patients (Niu et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2022).

In conclusion, rapamycin’s role as an mTORC1 inhibitor with the capacity to induce autophagy and mitophagy presents an exciting therapeutic avenue for addressing the complex pathology of dry AMD. By alleviating the buildup of toxic metabolites and preserving mitochondrial health in RPE cells, rapamycin could potentially slow disease progression and preserve vision. Nonetheless, the balance between benefit and adverse effects remains delicate, and further research is essential to elucidate how best to leverage rapamycin’s molecular actions in a clinically meaningful way. This comprehensive review supports the continued investigation of rapamycin as a candidate for dry AMD therapy, provided that subsequent studies can address the highlighted challenges and refine the therapeutic strategy to achieve consistent, positive clinical outcomes (Mei et al., 2022; Hyttinen et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2022).
