Proposal for Deferoxamine (Sigma-Aldrich D9533)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Deferoxamine (also known as desferrioxamine B; DFO) is a natural microbial siderophore originally isolated from Streptomyces pilosus and developed as an iron‐chelating drug in the 1960s, with clinical deployment beginning in 1968 under the trade name Desferal for treatment of iron overload disorders (notably transfusional iron loading in β-thalassemia) (Faa & Crisponi, 1999; Sharpe et al., 2011; Birch et al., 2006). Chemically, DFO is a hexadentate, tris-hydroxamate ligand that coordinates ferric iron (FeIII) at six oxygen donors derived from three bidentate hydroxamate groups to form a very stable 1:1 FeIII–DFO complex with distorted octahedral FeIIIO6 geometry (Sharpe et al., 2011; Richardson & Poňka, 1998). The FeIII affinity is exceptionally high and selective: reported formation constants for FeIII–DFO include log β110 ≈ 30.5 and conditional pM/pFe values around 26–31, while affinity for FeII is ∼20 orders of magnitude lower, consistent with the observation that DFO selectively chelates FeIII in vivo (Sharpe et al., 2011; Kontoghiorghes & Kontoghiorghe, 2020; Birch et al., 2006). The compound is highly hydrophilic, carries a positive charge near physiological pH, and exhibits poor membrane permeability and oral absorption; as a result, DFO is administered parenterally and tends to localize extracellularly or within endosomal/lysosomal compartments after endocytosis (Birch et al., 2006; Kontoghiorghes & Kontoghiorghe, 2020; Zhao et al., 2021). Pharmacokinetically, native DFO has a very short plasma half-life on the order of minutes, with multiple studies reporting ∼10–30 minutes after intravenous dosing, rapid renal clearance, and elimination of chelated iron via urine and bile; the short exposure necessitates prolonged subcutaneous or intravenous infusions for clinical efficacy (Sharpe et al., 2011; Jones et al., 2020; Entezari et al., 2022). DFO is not blood–neural barrier penetrant, limiting access to the central nervous system and retina when given systemically (Li et al., 2023).

As a class, siderophores are low-molecular microbial metabolites evolved for FeIII acquisition and storage; medicinally, DFO is the prototypical siderophore drug and, for decades, has served as the clinical standard for chelation in iron-overload conditions (Hatcher et al., 2009). Within therapeutic iron chelators, DFO’s hexadentate hydroxamate architecture confers high thermodynamic and kinetic stability of FeIII complexes, limiting redox cycling and hydroxyl radical generation relative to weaker or redox-active iron ligands (Hider & Kong, 2013).

Therapeutic History  
DFO has an extensive clinical history as the benchmark iron chelator for transfusional iron overload, especially in β-thalassemia major and other transfusion-dependent anemias; it was approved in the United States in 1968 and used for decades as life-saving therapy, albeit limited by the need for prolonged parenteral infusions and high cost (Sharpe et al., 2011; Birch et al., 2006; Yang et al., 2012). Beyond chronic iron overload, labeled and established uses include acute iron poisoning and treatment of aluminum toxicity, reflecting DFO’s strong FeIII selectivity and capacity to form inert complexes with polyvalent cations in vivo (Jones et al., 2020). The standard practice has involved subcutaneous or intravenous infusions at 20–60 mg/kg daily for 8–24 hours over 5–7 days per week, with urinary and fecal excretion of iron–DFO complexes as pharmacodynamic endpoints; however, adherence and patient acceptance have been major barriers due to the burdensome administration (Tam et al., 2003; Entezari et al., 2022).

The clinical pharmacology and chemistry of DFO have motivated multiple improved-delivery strategies: liposomal encapsulation and multivesicular liposome systems to extend half-life, and macromolecular conjugates (e.g., starch or HES carriers) to prolong exposure and shift biodistribution to iron-storage organs; these approaches have demonstrated prolonged circulation, increased urinary iron excretion, and organ accumulation (Jones et al., 2020; Sharpe et al., 2011; Tam et al., 2003). Nevertheless, DFO remains orally inactive and requires parenteral dosing, in contrast to later oral chelators (deferiprone, deferasirox), which have supplanted it in many indications due to ease of administration while sometimes being deployed in combination regimens with DFO to enhance iron removal and cardiac protection via complementary pharmacokinetics and chelation kinetics (Hider & Kong, 2013; Ma et al., 2012).

In ophthalmology and retinal biology, DFO’s evaluation is mixed. On one hand, iron chelation with DFO has shown protective effects in experimental retinal contexts: it reduced retinal ganglion cell death induced by hydrogen peroxide and paraquat in oxidative-stress paradigms, consistent with inhibition of iron-dependent hydroxyl radical production (Rohowetz et al., 2018). In hemorrhagic retinal injury, DFO protected photoreceptor tissues after subretinal blood in animal models, suggesting iron chelation can blunt heme/Fe-driven toxicity in the subretinal space (Song & Dunaief, 2013). In RPE-relevant cell models, DFO mitigated lipid peroxidation, prevented glutathione depletion, decreased Fe2+ accumulation, and preserved viability of ARPE-19 cells under tert-butyl hydroperoxide and blue-light stress, and it enhanced cell survival by modulating antioxidant defense systems (GSH–GPX4 and FSP1–CoQ10–NADH axes) (Zhao et al., 2025). A zinc–DFO complex (Zn/DFO) designed to improve cellular delivery reduced retinal iron content, lipid peroxidation, and oxidative DNA damage, preserved photoreceptor structure, and improved ERG responses in rd10 mice, demonstrating in vivo neuroprotection when systemic penetration is enhanced (Zhao et al., 2025). Ex vivo, DFO decreased histochemically detectable chelatable iron (Perls–DAB) in macular sections from AMD eyes, supporting the feasibility of removing pathologic labile iron in human AMD tissue (He et al., 2007).

On the other hand, DFO has a documented profile of retinal/ocular toxicity in patients receiving systemic therapy: reports include macular and peripheral pigmentary degeneration, impaired night vision, optic neuritis/neuropathy, cataract, ERG amplitude reductions, and EOG light-peak abnormalities, prompting recommendations for ophthalmic surveillance, and leading some experts to conclude that DFO is a dubious candidate for AMD treatment (He et al., 2007; Li et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2021). In RPE culture, repeated DFO exposure was cytotoxic relative to a prochelator control, and clinical series describe deferoxamine retinopathy across pediatric and adult populations, indicating that while iron chelation can be protective in vitro under specific stress conditions, DFO itself can injure RPE and photoreceptors under sustained or high-exposure regimens (Charkoudian et al., 2008; Błasiak et al., 2011). Importantly, DFO’s poor cell permeability and lack of blood–retinal barrier penetration raise practical concerns about achieving therapeutic intra-retinal exposure without systemic toxicity when administered conventionally (Li et al., 2023; Birch et al., 2006). Finally, although iron chelation is mechanistically compelling for retinal degenerations in which iron accumulation and ferroptotic stress participate, application of systemic chelators like DFO to retinal vascular disease and AMD remains at the basic research stage, with unresolved issues including perturbation of systemic/local iron homeostasis and stability of intracellular chelate complexes (Li et al., 2023).

Mechanism of Action  
At the molecular level, DFO is a high-affinity FeIII-selective chelator that forms kinetically inert hexacoordinate FeIIIO6 complexes via three hydroxamate ligands, each providing bidentate O,Oʹ coordination; this renders FeIII chemically sequestered, suppresses redox cycling, and prevents iron from entering the Haber–Weiss/Fenton chemistry that generates hydroxyl radicals from superoxide/hydrogen peroxide (Sharpe et al., 2011; Richardson & Poňka, 1998; Hider & Kong, 2013). The FeIII/FeII couple of ferrioxamine is electrochemically very negative (reported around −468 mV vs. NHE), and the complex is redox-inert under physiological conditions, consistent with its ability to quench iron-mediated ROS production rather than participate in it (Hider & Kong, 2013). In biological systems, DFO preferentially binds non-transferrin-bound iron (NTBI), labile iron pools in hepatocytes/macrophages, and low-molecular-weight iron transiting endosomes/lysosomes after transferrin delivery or ferritin degradation; it can mobilize hepatic stores and iron released by reticuloendothelial cells but cannot effectively strip iron from metalloproteins such as hemoglobin, myoglobin, cytochromes, oxidases, catalase, or peroxidases (Hatcher et al., 2009; Richardson & Poňka, 1998; Faa & Crisponi, 1999). The drug is hydrophilic and generally membrane-impermeant; cellular uptake occurs via fluid-phase endocytosis and pinocytosis, leading to enrichment in endosomal/lysosomal compartments, where it chelates lysosomal labile iron, an activity proposed to block lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis—mechanistically relevant to RPE pathobiology (Zhao et al., 2021; Dayani et al., 2004).

In retina and neural tissues, oxidative damage pathways include iron-dependent conversion of H2O2 to •OH via Fenton chemistry; DFO reduces H2O2-induced damage in retinal ganglion cells and protects against paraquat-generated superoxide by depriving upstream Fenton catalysts of accessible iron (Rohowetz et al., 2018). In RPE and photoreceptors, ferroptosis—a regulated cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation—is closely linked to antioxidant systems centered on glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and, independently, on FSP1-mediated ubiquinol regeneration; DFO’s chelation of FeII/FeIII within lysosomes and cytosolic labile pools acts upstream of these pathways to limit lipid hydroperoxide propagation, preserving GSH–GPX4 and FSP1–CoQ10–NADH defense capacity in ARPE-19 under oxidative stimuli (Zhao et al., 2025; Li et al., 2023). In systemic contexts, DFO detoxifies NTBI, reduces heme-derived ferryl species, removes high-oxidation-state heme iron, and directly scavenges some radical species, converging on inhibition of membrane lipid oxidation; these activities potentially translate to retinal cells where heme/iron-driven oxidative stress is implicated (Hatcher et al., 2009).

Notably, DFO’s hexadentate size and hydrophilicity restrict direct ferritin iron extraction and limit chelation in tightly protein-bound pools; smaller chelators can penetrate cells more readily and directly efflux ferritin iron, whereas DFO works primarily by intercepting low-molecular-weight iron pools and forming kinetically inert FeIII complexes that are then cleared, often via hepatobiliary or renal routes (Sharpe et al., 2011; Richardson & Poňka, 1998). In inflamed or ischemic tissues, the depletion of accessible iron by DFO can blunt downstream ROS and inflammatory cascades; however, prolonged systemic chelation perturbs metal homeostasis and can deplete essential iron pools in sensitive tissues, underlying some adverse effects including RPE toxicity, auditory neuropathy, and bone dysplasia (Zhao et al., 2021; He et al., 2007).

Expected Effect  
For dry AMD models centered on RPE iron-dependent oxidative stress, the expected pharmacologic effect of DFO is to reduce the labile Fe pool in lysosomes and cytosol, thereby attenuating hydroxyl-radical production from H2O2 and limiting iron-driven lipid peroxidation; operationally, this should decrease oxidative damage readouts and preserve mitochondrial and cellular function in RPE assays (Rohowetz et al., 2018; Li et al., 2023). Specifically:

- Lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis markers: In ARPE-19 cells, DFO has been shown to mitigate lipid peroxidation under oxidant (tBH) and blue-light stress and to preserve viability, consistent with suppression of ferroptotic death; thus, in RPE assays one should observe reductions in lipid peroxidation assays (e.g., BODIPY-C11) and maintenance of GPX4 activity/content, with complementary preservation of the FSP1–CoQ10–NADH axis (Zhao et al., 2025). The same studies indicate decreased Fe2+ accumulation after DFO, reflecting lower catalytic Fe at sites of peroxidation; similar decreases in labile Fe can be measured via calcein quenching or analogous probes (Zhao et al., 2025).

- Oxidative DNA damage: Systemic Zn/DFO reduced retinal oxidative DNA damage in vivo in rd10 mice; although these are not AMD models, the result predicts that in RPE oxidative stress assays DFO will reduce accumulation of oxidative DNA lesions, including those arising in mitochondrial DNA, where iron-dependent ROS can be particularly damaging (Zhao et al., 2025). Ex vivo human AMD maculas showed decreased Perls–DAB staining after DFO chelation, supporting the feasibility of reducing chelatable iron burden and downstream oxidative chemistry in diseased human retina (He et al., 2007).

- Mitochondrial bioenergetics: Preservation of RPE oxygen consumption rate (OCR) is plausible under DFO exposure insofar as DFO limits lipid peroxidation and maintains the reductive antioxidant systems (GSH–GPX4, FSP1–CoQ10) that protect inner-mitochondrial membranes and electron-transport components from iron-dependent oxidative injury; in ARPE-19, DFO improved survival and redox balance under stress, suggesting OCR preservation would accompany reduced peroxidation (Zhao et al., 2025). In retinal ganglion cells, DFO lessened H2O2-induced cell death, indirectly supporting improved mitochondrial tolerance to oxidant stress; similar protection can be anticipated in RPE where iron-dependent ROS compromise mitochondrial DNA and respiratory complexes (Rohowetz et al., 2018).

- Tissue iron fingerprints: In tissue sections or organoids, DFO exposure is expected to decrease Perls–DAB positive iron signal in regions with elevated chelatable iron, as observed in AMD macular tissue; in vivo, systemic DFO does not cross the BRB well, so demonstration of retinal iron removal may require enhanced uptake strategies (e.g., Zn/DFO) or local delivery (Li et al., 2023; He et al., 2007; Zhao et al., 2025).

Caveats critical to experimental design include DFO’s lack of blood–retinal barrier penetration and poor cell permeability, which could limit intracellular RPE exposure and lysosomal targeting in vivo after systemic dosing; thus, in vitro or ex vivo assays will show clearer pharmacodynamic effects than systemic models unless delivery is optimized (e.g., Zn-complexation or nanocarriers) (Li et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2025; Jones et al., 2020). Additionally, repeated or high-concentration exposure can be cytotoxic to RPE in vitro and has been associated with clinical retinopathy, so dose–exposure optimization and careful toxicity monitoring are required, especially in chronic paradigms (Charkoudian et al., 2008; He et al., 2007; Zhao et al., 2021).

Overall Evaluation  
Rationale strength and translational plausibility. The mechanistic rationale for iron chelation in dry AMD is strong: AMD retinas accumulate chelatable iron, iron catalyzes hydroxyl-radical generation and lipid peroxidation, and iron-dependent ferroptotic stress has been implicated in RPE dysfunction and photoreceptor degeneration (He et al., 2007; Li et al., 2023). DFO is a high-affinity FeIII chelator with extensive human experience, and it is proven to suppress iron-mediated ROS by forming kinetically inert FeIII complexes, preventing Fenton chemistry (Sharpe et al., 2011; Hider & Kong, 2013). In retinal and RPE models, DFO reduces oxidative injury, limits lipid peroxidation, and preserves cell viability; improved-delivery variants such as Zn/DFO demonstrate in vivo protection of retinal structure and function with concomitant decreases in retinal iron and oxidative DNA damage (Rohowetz et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2025). Ex vivo AMD maculae show DFO-responsive chelatable iron pools, reinforcing target engagement in human disease tissue (He et al., 2007). Mechanistically, enrichment of DFO in lysosomes—an organelle central to RPE iron handling, ferritinophagy, and lipid peroxidation—supports a proximal mode of action relevant to ferroptosis biology in AMD (Zhao et al., 2021).

Key liabilities and translational risks. Despite its compelling mechanism, DFO’s drug properties and ocular safety profile are unfavorable for repurposing in dry AMD without substantial reformulation or targeting:

- Ocular toxicity. DFO has a recognized spectrum of retinal adverse effects in patients, including pigmentary retinopathy, optic neuropathy, ERG/EOG abnormalities, and visual loss; authoritative reviews judge DFO a dubious candidate for AMD because of these safety concerns, and clinical surveillance is recommended when patients receive systemic DFO (He et al., 2007; Li et al., 2023). RPE toxicity is documented in vitro and clinically across age groups, indicating a narrow retinal therapeutic window under repeated exposure (Błasiak et al., 2011; Charkoudian et al., 2008; Zhao et al., 2021).

- Delivery limitations. DFO is hydrophilic, membrane-impermeant, and not blood–neural/retinal barrier penetrant, which undermines retinal exposure after systemic dosing; achieving therapeutic intra-RPE concentrations without systemic toxicity is unlikely with conventional infusion regimens (Li et al., 2023; Birch et al., 2006). The molecule’s very short plasma half-life further complicates exposure in avascular tissues like the outer retina (Sharpe et al., 2011; Jones et al., 2020).

- Systemic chelation risks. Long-term DFO perturbs systemic iron balance, interacts with microbiological iron acquisition in certain pathogens (e.g., Vibrio, S. aureus) that can exploit ferrioxamine, and carries recognized risks of growth retardation, bone changes, and infection, which are unacceptable for a chronic ocular disease with slow progression (Sharpe et al., 2011; Faa & Crisponi, 1999).

- Clinical maturity in ophthalmology. While iron chelators show promise in preclinical retinal models, clinical translation to retinal vascular disease/AMD remains at the basic research stage; challenges include maintaining retinal iron homeostasis, avoiding off-target metal depletion (e.g., Zn2+), and ensuring intracellular chelate stability and localization (Li et al., 2023).

Risk-mitigation strategies and development path. If DFO were to be advanced, development should avoid systemic DFO infusions for dry AMD. Instead:

- Leverage targeted delivery. The Zn/DFO complex that facilitates cellular uptake has shown robust neuroprotection, retinal iron lowering, and oxidative DNA damage reduction in vivo; such conjugate-based or carrier-based strategies could provide intracellular RPE delivery at lower systemic exposure, meriting further evaluation in AMD-relevant models (e.g., light damage, iron-loading, aged mice) with retinal safety readouts (Zhao et al., 2025). Nanocarriers or depot formulations that increase local ocular exposure while minimizing systemic levels could be explored, given DFO’s history of half-life extension in liposomal and multivesicular formulations systemically (Jones et al., 2020; Sharpe et al., 2011).

- Consider chemical alternatives within class. Other chelators with better cell permeability and ocular safety have shown retinal protection without the retinotoxicity seen with DFO; for example, deferiprone protected against iron-induced and light-induced retinal degeneration in multiple mouse models and was not found to be toxic to mouse retina in the contexts studied, suggesting that a more lipophilic, lower-molecular-weight chelator could be a safer starting point than DFO for dry AMD (Song & Dunaief, 2013; Picard et al., 2020).

- Exploit prochelation. Pro-chelators like BSIH that become active only under oxidative conditions were non-toxic with repeated exposure relative to DFO and may offer a way to achieve context-dependent iron chelation within oxidatively stressed RPE while sparing normal iron pools, lowering the risk of retinopathy (Charkoudian et al., 2008).

Decision framework for go/no-go. On balance, while DFO validates the iron-chelation mechanism in retinal tissues and provides useful tool-compound effects in vitro and in ex vivo AMD maculae, its physicochemical properties, delivery constraints, and retinal toxicity profile argue strongly against repurposing native DFO for chronic treatment of dry AMD (Li et al., 2023; He et al., 2007; Zhao et al., 2021). The most credible development path for DFO derivatives would require:

- Demonstrating safe, effective RPE delivery with a DFO-based construct (e.g., Zn/DFO or nanocarrier) that reduces labile iron, lipid peroxidation, and oxidative DNA damage in AMD-relevant in vivo models without ERG/EOG or structural toxicity (Zhao et al., 2025).

- Establishing a therapeutic index in the retina with chronic dosing and appropriate safety pharmacology (ERG, OCT, histopathology), given reported human retinopathies on DFO (He et al., 2007; Li et al., 2023).

- Benchmarking against alternative chelators (e.g., deferiprone) that already demonstrate retinal protection and better permeability/safety in vivo, to justify a DFO-based approach scientifically and clinically (Song & Dunaief, 2013; Picard et al., 2020).

Strengths  
- Mechanistic fit: chelates FeIII with high selectivity and prevents Fenton chemistry; acts in lysosomes where RPE iron and lipid peroxidation intersect with ferroptosis (Sharpe et al., 2011; Hider & Kong, 2013; Zhao et al., 2021).  
- Demonstrated retinal pharmacology: reduces oxidative injury in retinal neurons and RPE, lowers retinal iron and oxidative DNA damage with improved delivery, and removes chelatable iron from AMD macula ex vivo (Rohowetz et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2025; He et al., 2007).  
- Extensive human experience: well-characterized PK/PD, safety liabilities, and clinical management of chelation therapy (Jones et al., 2020; Tam et al., 2003).

Weaknesses  
- Ocular toxicity liability with systemic DFO, including pigmentary retinopathy and electrophysiologic dysfunction, making native DFO a poor risk–benefit prospect for a chronic retinal indication (He et al., 2007; Li et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2021).  
- Poor retinal exposure after systemic dosing due to hydrophilicity and lack of BRB penetration; very short half-life complicates sustained intraocular levels (Li et al., 2023; Birch et al., 2006; Jones et al., 2020).  
- Systemic risks (growth, bone, infection) incompatible with large, elderly dry-AMD populations unless exposure is tightly localized to the eye (Faa & Crisponi, 1999; Sharpe et al., 2011).  
- Field immaturity: iron chelators for retinal disease remain in basic research with unresolved questions about homeostasis and intracellular stability (Li et al., 2023).

Conclusion  
DFO is a mechanistically validated iron chelator with documented ability to reduce iron-driven oxidative injury in retinal systems; however, as a repurposed systemic drug for dry AMD, its delivery barriers and retinal toxicity outweigh potential benefits (Li et al., 2023; He et al., 2007). If iron chelation is pursued for dry AMD within this program, prioritizing better-penetrant, safer chelators (e.g., deferiprone) or context-activated prochelators over native DFO is the higher-probability path; alternately, DFO-based constructs that demonstrably deliver into RPE (e.g., Zn/DFO) with acceptable chronic retinal safety merit targeted preclinical exploration before considering clinical translation (Song & Dunaief, 2013; Charkoudian et al., 2008; Zhao et al., 2025).
