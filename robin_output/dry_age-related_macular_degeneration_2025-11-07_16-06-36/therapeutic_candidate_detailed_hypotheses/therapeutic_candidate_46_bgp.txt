Proposal for BGP-15 (Sigma-Aldrich B726)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
BGP-15 is a synthetic, small-molecule nicotinic amidoxime/hydroxylamine derivative whose full chemical name is O-(3-piperidino-2-hydroxy-1-propyl) nicotinic amidoxime, typically used as a dihydrochloride salt; it was developed by N-Gene (US/Hungary) and has been described as water-soluble and mitochondria-targeting in preclinical studies (including ocular applications) (Takács et al., 2024; Horváth et al., 2021). Chemically and pharmacologically, BGP-15 belongs to the hydroximic/hydroxylamine acid derivative family originally advanced as membrane-interacting heat-shock response (HSR) co-inducers that remodel cholesterol-enriched plasma membrane rafts, with pleiotropic, cytoprotective effects in stressed tissues (membrane-lipid therapy) (Crul et al., 2013; Gombos et al., 2011). In parallel, the compound has been positioned as a nicotinamide-related, non-carboxamide “NAM analogue” with in vitro mixed-type (noncompetitive) PARP-1 inhibition, though the authors emphasize that in vivo protective effects likely involve additional mechanisms beyond direct PARP blockade because the in vitro inhibitory concentration exceeds achievable systemic levels (Crul et al., 2013). Independent biophysical and cellular studies show BGP-15 partitions into membranes, increases the fluidity of negatively charged lipid species, preserves heat-challenged raft integrity, and activates stress signaling linked to HSP induction; imaging and molecular dynamics support a direct membrane-interacting mode of action (Gombos et al., 2011; Crul et al., 2013).

Functionally, BGP-15 is best classified as a multifunctional HSP co-inducer and insulin-sensitizing, mitochondria-targeting cytoprotective agent that remodels membrane rafts, enhances the HSF1–HSP axis, and modulates stress and survival signaling (Akt up, JNK/p38 down), while lowering mitochondrial ROS and stabilizing Δψm; importantly, it preferentially acts in stressed cells, an attribute that underpins its safety rationale (Gombos et al., 2011; Sumegi et al., 2017). The compound concentrates in energized mitochondria driven by its delocalized positive charge, with >85–90% uptake into isolated mitochondria under experimental conditions (Sümegi, 2018). Mitochondrial studies consistently localize the dominant ROS-lowering effect to the respiratory chain at complexes I and III, with minimal direct antioxidant chemistry, and only mild uncoupling at millimolar concentrations that is irrelevant at the micromolar doses used in most cell studies (Sumegi et al., 2017; Sümegi, 2018).

Crucial nomenclature caveat: a distinct “BGP-15” reported by a different group denotes a calcipotriene-derived vitamin D3 analog with anticancer activity; this vitamin D3 analog is mechanistically and structurally unrelated to the nicotinic amidoxime HSP co-inducer described above (both were labeled “BGP-15” by their respective originators) (Sintov et al., 2013). For the current program, the candidate of interest is the nicotinic amidoxime/hydroxylamine HSP co-inducer (often supplied by N-Gene or commercial chemical vendors under the amidoxime name), not the vitamin D3 analog (Takács et al., 2024).

Therapeutic History  
BGP-15 and closely related hydroximic/hydroxylamine acid derivatives have an extensive preclinical history across cardiac, neural, hepatic, renal, skeletal muscle, pulmonary, and metabolic models, with multiple mechanistic readouts converging on membrane remodeling, HSR/HSP co-induction, MAPK and Akt pathway modulation, and mitochondrial protection (Sumegi et al., 2017). Cardiovascular protection has been reported in ischemia–reperfusion, heart failure, and atrial fibrillation, with actions that include reduced ROS, preserved mitochondrial function, and Akt activation with suppression of JNK/p38 (Sumegi et al., 2017). In spontaneously hypertensive rats, BGP-15 increased PGC-1α and mitochondrial mass, decreased interstitial fibrosis, increased MKP-1 and reduced p38/JNK phosphorylation, and activated Akt/GSK-3β signaling (Horváth et al., 2021). BGP-15 prevented imatinib cardiotoxicity, protected against cisplatin nephrotoxicity without impairing antitumor efficacy, and mitigated cisplatin/taxol peripheral neuropathy (Sumegi et al., 2017; Sümegi, 2018). Hepatoprotection against acetaminophen injury via inhibition of caspase-independent cell death has also been documented (Sumegi et al., 2017; Sümegi, 2018). In pulmonary arterial hypertension and severe muscular dystrophy models, BGP-15 preserved mitochondrial structure and function and slowed disease progression; mechanistically, mitochondrial fusion via OPA1 activation and Akt signaling were implicated (Szabó et al., 2018).

Neuroprotection and mitochondrial rescue have been demonstrated in vitro and in vivo, including reduced neuronal death and improved mitochondrial potential/ROS at axonal branch points, coupled to HSP72 elevation, reduced phospho-JNK/p38, and enhanced Akt/IGF1R signaling (Ohlen et al., 2017). Consistent with its membrane-raft mechanism, BGP-15 increased HSP expression during heat challenge and remodeled cholesterol-rich platforms that gate stress signaling (Gombos et al., 2011; Crul et al., 2013).

In ophthalmology, BGP-15 has been applied in multiple retinal disease contexts. It was reported retinoprotective in Goto–Kakizaki rats (type 2 diabetes) with functional preservation on ERG, and later shown to improve survival and retinal function long-term in ZDF rats under uncontrolled diabetes, with increased ocular HSP70 and decreased NFκB (Takács et al., 2024; Wachal et al., 2021). Topical ocular delivery using an SBECD-containing eyedrop improved scotopic/photopic a- and b-waves, restored oscillatory potentials, shortened implicit times, and preserved retinal thickness following global ischemia–reperfusion injury in rats (Takács et al., 2024). These ocular data align with the compound’s mitochondrial and HSP co-inducer mechanisms: accumulation in mitochondria, protection against apoptosis/necrosis in oxidative-stress paradigms, and modulation of stress signaling (Takács et al., 2024).

Clinically, BGP-15 has a proof-of-concept randomized, double-blind trial demonstrating dose-dependent improvement of insulin sensitivity in insulin-resistant patients without apparent short-term toxicity; in parallel, human phase II work in olanzapine-induced metabolic disorder also identified BGP-15 as an insulin sensitizer (Crul et al., 2013; Sümegi, 2018). Safety signals from early human studies and the pharmacology of HSP co-inducers (preferential action in stressed cells) support the translational risk profile; however, systemic serum concentrations reported by the developer are low (≤25 nmol/mL), which has implications for target engagement and favors local ocular delivery for retinal indications (Gombos et al., 2011; Sumegi et al., 2017).

To our knowledge based on the supplied corpus, BGP-15 has not been directly tested in dry age-related macular degeneration (dry AMD) patients or canonical dry AMD animal models; however, its retinoprotective efficacy in diabetic retinopathy models and retinal ischemia–reperfusion suggests relevance to retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)/photoreceptor stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inflammation, which are central to dry AMD biology (Takács et al., 2024).

Mechanism of Action  
Membrane-lipid therapy and HSP co-induction. BGP-15 partitions into and remodels membrane lipid domains, particularly cholesterol-rich plasma membrane rafts that organize stress sensing and signaling (Gombos et al., 2011; Crul et al., 2013). Under heat stress or analogous perturbations, BGP-15 preserves raft integrity and elevates HSP expression; this is associated with activation of Rac1 and modulation of HSF1 acetylation, leading to prolonged HSF1 DNA binding and robust HSP gene induction without causing protein unfolding, a hallmark of HSP “co-inducers” (Gombos et al., 2011; Crul et al., 2013). In HSR-defective melanoma cells, BGP-15 significantly rescued raft domain coverage and shifted the size distribution toward larger platforms, coincident with increased hsp25 expression and Rac1-dependent stress signaling (Gombos et al., 2011). Across cell and tissue contexts, BGP-15 increases HSP70/HSPA1A (HSP72), and this HSP gain is causally linked to improved insulin signaling and cytoprotection in stressed cells (Crul et al., 2013; Gombos et al., 2011).

PARP/NAD+/SIRT1/HSF1 axis. Although BGP-15 demonstrates mixed-type (noncompetitive) inhibition of PARP-1 in vitro, the concentrations required exceed typical in vivo exposures, suggesting that PARP pathway effects in vivo are likely downstream of ROS lowering and reduced DNA single-strand breaks that otherwise drive PARP activation (Crul et al., 2013). In stressed cells, BGP-15 reduces PAR levels, which relieves PARP-mediated suppression of MKP-1/Dusp1, thereby inhibiting JNK/p38 activation; reduced PARP activity also preserves NAD+ and SIRT1 activity, which favors a deacetylated, active HSF1 and a stronger HSR (Crul et al., 2013). This mechanistic loop—less ROS, less PARP activation, more MKP-1, less JNK/p38, and better HSP/SIRT1 tone—aligns with the observed anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective phenotypes (Crul et al., 2013).

Mitochondrial enrichment and respiratory chain modulation. Biophysically, BGP-15 carries a delocalized positive charge, accumulates in energized mitochondria (>85–90% uptake in isolated organelles), and at micromolar doses characteristic of cell studies (1–50 µM) lowers mitochondrial ROS predominantly at complexes I and III without significant baseline depolarization of Δψm (Sümegi, 2018; Sumegi et al., 2017). In oxidative or inflammatory challenges, BGP-15 attenuates H₂O₂- and LPS-induced mitochondrial depolarization and reduces mitochondrial superoxide to near-control levels; MitoTEMPO controls confirm mitochondrial origin of the ROS signal (Sumegi et al., 2017). Importantly, the compound exhibits no meaningful direct antioxidant scavenging in cell-free ROS-generating systems, and only displays mild uncoupling at millimolar concentrations—far above the micromolar exposures used in protective assays—supporting a mechanism of respiratory chain modulation rather than redox scavenging (Sumegi et al., 2017; Sümegi, 2018). Downstream signaling reflects the lowered mitochondrial ROS burden: JNK/p38 activation is reduced, Akt signaling is enhanced, and caspase-independent death pathways are suppressed (Sumegi et al., 2017).

Mitochondrial dynamics, fusion, and biogenesis. Beyond acute ROS control, BGP-15 promotes mitochondrial fusion and preserves cristae architecture by activating OPA1 GTPase and promoting OPA1 polymerization; genetic suppression of OPA1 or Mfn1/Mfn2 abolishes the fusion phenotype, indicating that BGP-15 acts upstream or within the endogenous fusion machinery (Szabó et al., 2018). In vivo, these effects translate into improved mitochondrial ultrastructure and tissue histology under ROS stress (e.g., monocrotaline-induced PAH) (Szabó et al., 2018). In hypertensive cardiomyopathy, BGP-15 increases PGC-1α and mitochondrial mass, with concurrent shifts in CREB/AMPK phosphorylation consistent with improved energetic signaling, reduced p38/JNK via MKP-1 upregulation, and increased Akt/GSK-3β prosurvival signaling (Horváth et al., 2021).

Insulin-sensitizing stress signaling. In skeletal muscle and human studies, BGP-15 increases HSP72 expression, enhances insulin receptor autophosphorylation (blocking JNK inhibitory phosphorylation), activates RAC1, increases AKT phosphorylation, promotes GLUT4 translocation, increases membrane fluidity, raises AMPK activity, and reduces HSF1 acetylation, altogether improving glucose disposal without overt toxicity in a randomized trial (Crul et al., 2013). These metabolic effects are mechanistically congruent with the membrane–HSR–mitochondrial triad described above (Crul et al., 2013).

Ocular and retinal mechanisms. In rat eyes, BGP-15 increased HSP70 and decreased NFκB, consistent with HSR enhancement and anti-inflammatory signaling in ocular tissues (Wachal et al., 2021). In ischemia–reperfusion and diabetic retinopathy models, functional improvements on ERG, restoration of oscillatory potentials, and preservation of retinal thickness are consistent with mitochondrial protection and stress-response reinforcement in retinal cells (Takács et al., 2024). The topical SBECD formulation demonstrated pharmacologic activity in the retina without systemic pharmacokinetic characterization, indicating local ocular delivery as a viable route for targeting mitochondrial and HSP pathways in the posterior segment (Takács et al., 2024).

Expected Effect  
The team’s hypothesis—that BGP-15 stabilizes mitochondrial membrane proteins and co-induces HSF1-driven antioxidant chaperone responses in RPE to mitigate oxidative stress in dry AMD—is aligned with the compound’s demonstrated pharmacology in retinal, cardiac, neural, and pulmonary models: (i) robust mitochondrial enrichment and stabilization of Δψm under oxidative/inflammatory stress, (ii) ROS lowering at complexes I and III without direct radical scavenging, (iii) activation of the HSR via membrane raft remodeling and HSF1 acetylation control, and (iv) suppression of ROS-sensitive injury cascades (JNK/p38) with concurrent activation of pro-survival Akt (Sumegi et al., 2017; Gombos et al., 2011; Crul et al., 2013). In retinal disease models, BGP-15 improved ERG measures and retinal thickness and increased ocular HSP70 while decreasing NFκB, providing a direct ocular precedent likely to translate to stressed RPE, where mitochondria and proteostasis are central to survival under chronic oxidative load (Takács et al., 2024; Wachal et al., 2021).

Mechanistically expected in RPE:  
- Membrane-raft remodeling and HSR co-induction. BGP-15 partitions into RPE membranes and remodels cholesterol-rich rafts, preserving raft integrity under stress, activating Rac1, decreasing HSF1 acetylation, and prolonging HSF1 DNA binding, thereby elevating HSP70/HSP25 and enhancing proteostasis capacity in aged/stressed RPE (Gombos et al., 2011; Crul et al., 2013).  
- Mitochondrial ROS containment and Δψm preservation. BGP-15 should accumulate in RPE mitochondria, reduce ROS production at complexes I/III, and attenuate oxidative- and inflammation-induced depolarization, thereby maintaining ATP production and limiting mitochondrial permeability transition and AIF translocation, as shown in other cell types (Sumegi et al., 2017; Sümegi, 2018).  
- Mitochondrial dynamics and biogenesis. Via OPA1 activation and Akt/GSK-3β signaling, BGP-15 is expected to promote inner-membrane fusion/OPA1 oligomerization and preserve cristae, with possible longer-term increases in mitochondrial biogenesis (PGC-1α), all protective features for RPE faced with chronic oxidative and lipofuscin stress (Szabó et al., 2018; Horváth et al., 2021).  
- Anti-inflammatory signaling rebalancing. By reducing mitochondrial ROS and PARP activation, BGP-15 should relieve PARP-mediated suppression of MKP-1, suppress JNK/p38 activity, decrease NFκB signaling, and bolster SIRT1-HSF1 activity, thereby reducing ROS-amplifying feed-forward loops in RPE (Crul et al., 2013; Wachal et al., 2021).

Assay expectations and endpoints. In standard RPE cell models (primary human RPE, ARPE-19, or iPSC-RPE), BGP-15 at 1–50 µM should (i) reduce H₂O₂- or LPS-induced mitochondrial depolarization (JC-1/TMRM), (ii) lower mitochondrial superoxide (MitoSOX) and total ROS (DHR123) without acting as a direct antioxidant in cell-free assays, (iii) reduce apoptosis/necrosis after oxidative challenge (annexin V/PI), (iv) increase HSP70/HSF1 pathway activation and decrease HSF1 acetylation, (v) activate Akt and downregulate JNK/p38, and (vi) promote OPA1 oligomerization and mitochondrial fusion (Sumegi et al., 2017; Gombos et al., 2011; Szabó et al., 2018). Where possible, complement activation or lipid peroxidation paradigms can be layered to emulate dry AMD stressors; based on the corpus, BGP-15’s effects are strongest under stress, consistent with HSP co-inducer pharmacology (Gombos et al., 2011).

Delivery considerations. Systemic exposures in humans are low (≤25 nmol/mL), suggesting that oral dosing may be insufficient to reach pharmacologic micromolar levels in RPE; conversely, topical ocular delivery with SBECD has demonstrated retinal functional benefits and structural protection, supporting development of a posterior-segment–targeted topical or periocular formulation to enrich the RPE–choroid compartment (Sümegi, 2018; Takács et al., 2024).

Caveats regarding target expression. The corpus documents the relevant targets (HSF1/HSP70, Rac1, OPA1, MKP-1, JNK/p38, Akt) as central to BGP-15’s mechanisms in multiple mammalian tissues and cell types and shows ocular readouts (HSP70 up, NFκB down) in rat eyes; however, it does not provide explicit RPE expression data, which should be confirmed experimentally in our RPE assay systems (Wachal et al., 2021; Gombos et al., 2011; Szabó et al., 2018).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
- Strong mechanistic congruence with dry AMD pathobiology. BGP-15 directly addresses mitochondrial dysfunction and chronic oxidative stress—core drivers of RPE degeneration—by concentrating in mitochondria, lowering ROS at complexes I/III, stabilizing Δψm, and activating survival signaling while suppressing inflammatory stress kinases (Sumegi et al., 2017). Concurrently, it co-induces the HSR by remodeling plasma membrane rafts and modulating HSF1 acetylation, thereby boosting HSP70/HSP25 chaperone capacity and proteostasis—functions that are expected to mitigate protein misfolding and aggregation in aged RPE (Gombos et al., 2011; Crul et al., 2013).  
- Demonstrated ocular efficacy in relevant retinal injury contexts. Topical BGP-15 improved ERG a- and b-waves, preserved oscillatory potentials, reduced implicit times, and rescued retinal thickness after ischemia–reperfusion, and it was retinoprotective in diabetic models, with ocular HSP70 increase and NFκB reduction—outcomes consistent with RPE/retinal resilience under oxidative stress (Takács et al., 2024; Wachal et al., 2021).  
- Pleiotropic yet coherent mechanism spanning membrane, nuclear, and mitochondrial nodes. The membrane–HSR–mitochondrial triad, including PARP/MKP-1/JNK–p38 and Akt/GSK-3β axes, provides multiple intervention points likely to translate into robust protection in the multifactorial environment of dry AMD (Crul et al., 2013; Sumegi et al., 2017; Szabó et al., 2018; Horváth et al., 2021).  
- Favorable translational signals. Proof-of-concept human trials in insulin resistance show pharmacodynamic activity without overt safety signals, and HSP co-inducers are known to preferentially act in stressed cells, potentially limiting off-target effects in quiescent tissues; local ocular delivery further improves the therapeutic index (Crul et al., 2013; Gombos et al., 2011; Takács et al., 2024).

Weaknesses and risks  
- Lack of direct dry AMD evidence. No studies in the corpus directly test BGP-15 in dry AMD models or patients; we infer utility from retinal IR/diabetes models and mechanism (Takács et al., 2024).  
- Exposure–response uncertainties for the posterior segment. Systemic serum concentrations are low relative to micromolar efficacious concentrations in vitro, necessitating optimized local delivery to RPE; although eyedrop formulations show retinal effects, posterior-segment delivery by topical route is often limited, and pharmacokinetics were not measured in the cited ocular study (Sümegi, 2018; Takács et al., 2024).  
- Target ambiguity and pleiotropy. While the membrane–HSR–mitochondrial framework is coherent, BGP-15 lacks a single defined receptor/protein target, and its in vitro PARP-1 inhibition likely does not drive in vivo efficacy; this complicates target engagement assays and regulatory narratives (Sümegi, 2018; Crul et al., 2013).  
- Nomenclature confusion. “BGP-15” also denotes a calcipotriene-derived vitamin D3 analog in the oncology literature; chemical identity must be verified to avoid conflation during procurement and CMC (Sintov et al., 2013; Takács et al., 2024).  
- Evidence base heterogeneity. Some ocular and cardiovascular studies are published in lower–quality venues, emphasizing the need for rigorous, blinded replication and GLP toxicology in ocular models (Horváth et al., 2021; Wachal et al., 2021).

Recommended next steps  
- In vitro RPE validation. In human primary or iPSC-derived RPE under oxidative (H₂O₂/tert-butyl hydroperoxide) and inflammatory (LPS/CpG) stress, test BGP-15 at 0.3–30 µM for effects on Δψm (JC-1/TMRM), mitochondrial superoxide (MitoSOX), total ROS (DHR123), apoptosis/necrosis (annexin V/PI), and mitochondrial respiration; confirm no direct antioxidant scavenging in cell-free systems (Sumegi et al., 2017).  
- Proteostasis and HSR endpoints. Quantify HSP70/HSP25 induction, HSF1 acetylation/binding dynamics, Rac1 activation, and membrane raft integrity using fPEG-cholesterol probes/TOCCSL in RPE; evaluate MKP-1, JNK/p38, and Akt/GSK-3β signaling (Gombos et al., 2011; Crul et al., 2013; Sumegi et al., 2017).  
- Mitochondrial dynamics. Assess OPA1 oligomerization, fusion/fission balance (Mfn1/2 and Drp1 markers), cristae morphology by EM, and PGC-1α/mtDNA copy number to benchmark fusion/biogenesis responses (Szabó et al., 2018; Horváth et al., 2021).  
- Ocular delivery PK/PD. Using the SBECD eyedrop formulation as a starting point, perform ocular pharmacokinetics (vitreous, retina, RPE–choroid) and pharmacodynamics (ocular HSP70, NFκB, mitochondrial markers) to ensure RPE target engagement at tolerated doses (Takács et al., 2024; Wachal et al., 2021).  
- In vivo retinal stress models. Extend to retinal oxidative stress paradigms that capture RPE pathology; leverage ERG, OCT, histology, and mitochondrial ultrastructure endpoints already positive in IR/diabetic models (Takács et al., 2024).  
- Identity control and CMC. Source BGP-15 as O-(3-piperidino-2-hydroxy-1-propyl) nicotinic amidoxime dihydrochloride; confirm identity by NMR/MS to avoid the calcipotriene-derived analog (Takács et al., 2024; Sintov et al., 2013).

Go/no-go considerations for dry AMD  
Given the robust mechanistic alignment—mitochondrial ROS control at complexes I/III, Δψm stabilization, HSR co-induction via membrane raft remodeling and HSF1 acetylation control, OPA1-driven mitochondrial fusion, and suppression of ROS-sensitive inflammatory kinases—plus demonstrable ocular efficacy in retinal injury models, BGP-15 merits advancement to RPE-focused preclinical validation for dry AMD (Sumegi et al., 2017; Gombos et al., 2011; Szabó et al., 2018; Takács et al., 2024). The principal risks are translational: ensuring adequate RPE exposures by ocular delivery, confirming RPE target expression/engagement, and generating rigorous GLP pharmacology/toxicology data in ocular species. On balance, the mechanistic breadth that often complicates development is an advantage in dry AMD, where mitochondrial dysfunction, proteostasis failure, and chronic inflammation coexist.

Conclusion. BGP-15 is a credible repurposing candidate for dry AMD based on its mitochondria-centered, HSP co-inducing, membrane-lipid–modulating pharmacology and encouraging retinal efficacy in vivo; priority should be given to RPE-targeted PK/PD, mitochondrial and HSR biomarker validation, and controlled efficacy studies in retinal oxidative-stress models to de-risk translation (Sumegi et al., 2017; Gombos et al., 2011; Takács et al., 2024).
