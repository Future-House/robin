Proposal for Spermidine (Sigma-Aldrich S2626)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Spermidine is a naturally occurring polyamine that was originally discovered in bovine semen and later shown to be ubiquitous in all forms of life, ranging from bacteria and plants to fungi and mammals. It belongs to the aliphatic polyamine class, characterized by its low molecular weight, linear arrangement, and multiple amino groups that are fully protonated at physiological pH. Its natural origins stem from highly conserved enzymatic biosynthetic pathways—starting either from ornithine via ornithine decarboxylase or from arginine through an agmatine intermediate—resulting in the formation of putrescine, which is subsequently converted to spermidine through the transfer of an aminopropyl group provided by decarboxylated S-adenosylmethionine (dcSAM) (Casero & Marton, 2007; Hobley et al., 2017; Khomutov et al., 2019). Although spermidine can be isolated from natural sources such as food and tissues, it is also available in a chemically defined form commercially (for example, Sigma-Aldrich S2626) and has been used in basic research as well as in some experimental therapeutic approaches. This compound is representative of the polyamine family, and these molecules have traditionally been investigated for their roles in cellular proliferation, nucleic acid stabilization, and modulation of various enzymatic activities, which in turn have broad implications for health and disease (Madeo et al., 2020; Nichugovskiy et al., 2021).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, spermidine’s potential therapeutic applications have been explored extensively in the context of its ability to induce autophagy and affect cellular homeostasis. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that spermidine supplementation can extend lifespan and improve the function of various tissues by enhancing autophagy, contributing to cardioprotection, and promoting neuroprotection in animal models (Han et al., 2022; Madeo et al., 2020). In the ocular domain, several studies have examined its effects on retinal cells: in vitro experiments using ARPE-19 retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells have shown that spermidine can protect against oxidative stress induced by hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) by mitigating intracellular calcium overload and maintaining mitochondrial integrity (Han et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2021). Additionally, there are preclinical observations in which intravitreal administration of spermidine in animal models produced both protective effects—in terms of reducing RPE apoptosis—and, at higher doses, toxic outcomes such as RPE necrosis and photoreceptor degeneration (Ohashi et al., 2017; Han et al., 2022). Although spermidine has not been clinically trialed specifically for dry age-related macular degeneration (dry AMD), its effects on RPE survival and ocular inflammation have been documented in models that mimic some of the pathophysiologic stressors in dry AMD, such as oxidative damage and dysregulated autophagy (Kim et al., 2021; Senekowitsch et al., 2023). In addition, dietary supplementation studies in humans have associated higher spermidine intake with various beneficial health outcomes, including improved endothelial function and cognitive performance; however, the use of spermidine or polyamines more broadly in chronic degenerative ocular conditions remains largely in the preclinical stage (Madeo et al., 2020).

Mechanism of Action:  
The hypothesized mechanism for using spermidine in dry AMD is multifaceted and centers on its ability to modulate cellular quality control processes. At the molecular level, spermidine is known to induce autophagy, a process that mediates the degradation and recycling of damaged or dysfunctional cellular constituents. In model systems ranging from yeast to mammalian cells, spermidine has been shown to increase the conversion of the autophagy marker LC3-I to LC3-II, thereby promoting autophagosome formation (Qin et al., 2021; Han et al., 2022). One important molecular target of spermidine appears to be EP300 (p300), a histone acetyltransferase that, when inhibited, leads to reduced acetylation of several autophagy-related proteins. Inhibition of EP300 has been correlated with enhanced autophagic flux, and several studies suggest that spermidine may exert its autophagy-inducing effects partly by downregulating EP300 expression or activity (Han et al., 2022; Madeo et al., 2020).

In addition to its effects on EP300, spermidine has been implicated in the activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), an evolutionarily conserved energy sensor that modulates metabolic homeostasis and mitochondrial quality control. Activation of AMPK can lead to the phosphorylation and activation of downstream effectors that promote both autophagy and mitophagy (the selective autophagic removal of damaged mitochondria). While direct evidence for AMPK activation by spermidine in retinal cells is still emerging, broader studies in other cell types have described an upregulation of pathways associated with mitochondrial biogenesis and quality control, including the deacetylation of proteins by SIRT1, which often acts in concert with AMPK (Han et al., 2022; Madeo et al., 2020). In the specific context of RPE cells under stress from A2E (a component of lipofuscin) or H₂O₂, the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria and high levels of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) play critical roles in cell loss and dysfunction; thus, induction of mitophagy via enhanced AMPK signaling and subsequent Parkin recruitment to depolarized mitochondria could improve mitochondrial homeostasis and reduce oxidative damage (Kim et al., 2021).

Furthermore, spermidine’s role as an intracellular calcium chelator appears pivotal in its protective effect in RPE cells. Studies have demonstrated that pre-treatment of ARPE-19 cells with 10 µM spermidine prior to oxidative stress prevents intracellular Ca²⁺ overload—a phenomenon that can trigger endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and cell death—thereby maintaining cellular integrity independent of direct ROS scavenging (Han et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2021). In summary, spermidine is posited to enhance the clearance of damaged organelles—particularly mitochondria—by increasing autophagic flux through EP300 inhibition and AMPK activation, leading to enhanced LC3-II conversion and Parkin-mediated mitophagy. This, in turn, is expected to reduce mtROS and protect RPE cell viability under conditions of oxidative and A2E-induced stress (Han et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2021).

Expected Effect:  
Based on its mode of action, the expected effect of spermidine in a dry AMD model would be a pronounced improvement in the quality control mechanisms of RPE cells. The compound is anticipated to induce autophagy, evidenced by a measurable increase in LC3-II levels, alongside enhanced recruitment of the E3 ubiquitin ligase Parkin to dysfunctional mitochondria. This concerted activation of autophagy and mitophagy should facilitate the clearance of damaged mitochondria, thereby reducing the accumulation of mtROS and attenuating oxidative stress. In the context of A2E accumulation—a key driver of RPE dysfunction in dry AMD—spermidine is predicted to restore mitochondrial function and improve cell viability. Experimental assays are expected to show that spermidine pre-treatment leads to a significant reduction in apoptosis markers (e.g., caspase-3 activation, cytochrome c release), along with improved markers of mitochondrial integrity (e.g., stabilized mitochondrial membrane potential) in ARPE-19 cells exposed to H₂O₂ or A2E (Kim et al., 2021).

Overall Evaluation:  
In evaluating spermidine as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD, several strengths and weaknesses emerge from the current literature. One major strength is its natural origin and well-documented role as a global inducer of autophagy and mitophagy. This aligns well with the current understanding of dry AMD pathogenesis, in which impaired autophagic clearance and mitochondrial dysfunction in RPE cells play major roles (Nichugovskiy et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2021). The ability of spermidine to inhibit EP300—a factor whose downregulation encourages autophagy—and to potentially activate AMPK signaling suggests that it could enhance several intracellular quality control processes simultaneously (Han et al., 2022; Madeo et al., 2020). The preclinical data showing that spermidine can protect ARPE-19 cells from H₂O₂-induced oxidative injury by blocking Ca²⁺ overload and reducing apoptotic signaling further bolster its potential utility in ocular applications (Han et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2021).

However, there are notable challenges and weaknesses associated with this therapeutic candidate. First, the dose response appears to be remarkably narrow; while low micromolar concentrations (e.g., 10 µM) exert cytoprotective effects in vitro, higher doses have been associated with cytotoxicity, including RPE cell death and photoreceptor degeneration in animal models following intravitreal injection (Ohashi et al., 2017; Han et al., 2022). This raises concerns about the therapeutic window and the optimal route of delivery for chronic conditions like dry AMD. Another limitation is that, despite promising mechanistic data, direct clinical evidence for the use of spermidine in dry AMD is currently lacking. Most of the studies available have been conducted in vitro or in animal models under conditions of oxidative stress, and while these findings are encouraging, they do not fully recapitulate the complex pathophysiology of dry AMD, which involves not only oxidative damage but also chronic inflammation, immune dysregulation, and drusen accumulation (Kim et al., 2021).

Moreover, while the hypothesis emphasizes effects on EP300 inhibition and AMPK activation leading to enhanced LC3-II conversion and Parkin recruitment, the direct evidence linking these specific molecular events to improved outcomes in dry AMD models remains preliminary. There is a need for more comprehensive studies that directly measure these autophagic and mitophagic markers in RPE cells under conditions that mimic the A2E-induced stress typical of dry AMD (Kim et al., 2021). Additionally, the risk that spermidine’s metabolism by amine oxidases may produce toxic aldehyde byproducts such as acrolein must be carefully considered, as these compounds have been implicated in retinal degeneration in some intravitreal models (Ohashi et al., 2017).

In summary, while spermidine has a compelling mechanistic rationale as a modulator of autophagy and mitochondrial quality control, which are central to the pathogenesis of dry AMD, the current preclinical evidence is mixed and highlights potential safety challenges related to dosing and administration. Its established role in promoting cellular longevity and mitigating oxidative stress in diverse tissues certainly warrants further investigation, but rigorous studies using dry AMD-specific models are essential to ascertain its efficacy and safety in this context. Regulatory considerations will also require detailed pharmacokinetic and toxicological profiling, particularly given its dual potential for both cytoprotection and cytotoxicity depending on concentration and route of administration (Ohashi et al., 2017; Han et al., 2022).

Overall, our evaluation suggests that spermidine represents a promising candidate for repurposing in the treatment of dry AMD based on its intrinsic properties as an autophagy and mitophagy inducer and its ability to protect RPE cells from oxidative damage; however, its clinical translation will depend on resolving key issues such as dose optimization, route of delivery, and comprehensive validation of its proposed molecular mechanisms in relevant models of AMD. The strengths of spermidine lie in its natural occurrence, multi-targeted action on cellular quality control mechanisms, and established preclinical benefits in other models of aging and oxidative stress. Conversely, the main weaknesses are its narrow therapeutic window, the potential for generation of toxic metabolites, and the current lack of direct clinical or robust in vivo data in dry AMD models. Further research, including detailed investigations in RPE models using A2E and oxidative stress conditions as well as careful titration studies in animal models of dry AMD, is warranted to determine its viability as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for this unmet clinical need (Casero & Marton, 2007; Kim et al., 2021; Han et al., 2022; Ohashi et al., 2017).
