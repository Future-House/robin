Proposal for Idebenone (Sigma-Aldrich I7876)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Idebenone is a synthetic short‐chain benzoquinone that was designed as an analog of coenzyme Q10. Its chemical derivation from ubiquinone endows it with increased solubility and significantly enhanced antioxidant properties – reportedly up to ten‐fold greater than natural coenzyme Q10 – which in turn improves its capacity to shuttle electrons through the mitochondrial respiratory chain (Arend et al., 2015, pp. 2, 7–8). Originally synthesized as a potential remedy for mitochondrial dysfunction, idebenone belongs to the class of quinone derivatives that have historically been applied to conditions driven by oxidative stress and defective energy metabolism. The compound was discovered through efforts to mimic and improve on the natural electron carrier function of coenzyme Q10, with modifications engineered to optimize cellular uptake and bioavailability, thereby augmenting its use in various neurodegenerative and ophthalmologic applications (Gueven et al., 2015, pp. 4–5; Arend et al., 2015, pp. 7–8).

Therapeutic History:  
Idebenone’s clinical journey commenced with its use in Leber’s Hereditary Optic Neuropathy (LHON), a mitochondrial disorder resulting in acute or subacute vision loss. In LHON, idebenone has been shown to improve visual function, as evidenced by improvements in photopic electroretinography (ERG) and other visual assessments, and has received regulatory approval in certain regions for this indication (Lyseng-Williamson, 2016, pp. 1–2). Preclinical studies have also demonstrated that idebenone exerts cytoprotective effects on retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells and retinal ganglion cells by preserving mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) and reducing apoptosis in the face of oxidative stress (Clementi et al., 2022, pp. 7–10, 12–13). Although idebenone’s original target was LHON, its biochemical properties—particularly its dual role as an electron shuttle and antioxidant—have spurred interest in repurposing the compound for dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD), a condition likewise characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction and heightened oxidative injury. Several studies have provided evidence from both biochemical assays and animal models that idebenone can modulate pro‐ and anti‐apoptotic protein expression (notably lowering Bax while increasing Bcl-2 levels) and activate the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway in RPE cells, thereby suggesting a potential benefit in retinal diseases beyond LHON (Clementi et al., 2022, pp. 12–13; Arend et al., 2015, pp. 7–8).

Mechanism of Action:  
Idebenone operates through a multifaceted mechanism that addresses the dual challenges of mitochondrial energy impairment and oxidative damage. Fundamentally, idebenone is proposed to bypass defects in mitochondrial complex I by serving as an electron carrier that transfers reducing equivalents directly to complex III. This electron shuttling function is critical for maintaining the mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) and sustaining ATP synthesis when the conventional electron transfer through complex I is compromised due to oxidative damage—a scenario that is hypothesized to occur in the RPE under conditions such as drusen-induced stress (Arend et al., 2015, p. 2; Wang et al., 2025, pp. 5–8). In parallel, idebenone exhibits potent antioxidant activity by scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) and mitigating the lipid peroxidation processes that are thought to contribute to drusen formation in dry AMD (Arend et al., 2015, pp. 7–8).

At the molecular level, idebenone has been shown to influence key cell survival pathways. It modulates the intrinsic mitochondrial apoptotic cascade by upregulating the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 and concurrently downregulating the pro-apoptotic protein Bax. This shift in the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio helps to curb the initiation of apoptosis and contributes to cell survival in oxidative environments (Clementi et al., 2022, pp. 12–13; Arend et al., 2015, pp. 5–6). Furthermore, idebenone stimulates the nuclear translocation of the transcription factor Nrf2, which in turn induces the expression of a suite of antioxidant genes. This activation of the Nrf2 pathway reinforces the cell’s intrinsic defenses against oxidative insult, which is of particular relevance in RPE cells tasked with handling the chronic low-grade oxidative stress present in AMD (Clementi et al., 2022, pp. 7–10, 12–13). However, it is important to note that in certain settings—such as in cells deficient in the enzyme NQO1—idebenone’s efficacy as an electron carrier may be diminished, and higher concentrations may even elicit pro-oxidant or cytotoxic effects (Varricchio et al., 2020, pp. 18–20). These findings underscore the importance of optimizing the dosing strategy and ensuring appropriate cellular context to fully exploit idebenone’s protective actions.

Expected Effect:  
In the context of the proposed application for dry AMD, the hypothesis is that idebenone will act to shuttle electrons to complex III in RPE cells, thereby bypassing complex I defects induced by drusen-associated oxidative stress. This mechanism is expected to preserve the mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) and sustain ATP production, even under conditions of compromised oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). Preclinical data in RPE cell models have shown that idebenone treatment can mitigate the dissipation of Δψm in the face of hydrogen peroxide-induced stress, suggesting that similar protective effects could be harnessed in AMD where RPE cellular damage is a hallmark (Clementi et al., 2022, pp. 7–10, 12–13).

Beyond its electron shuttling capacity, idebenone’s potent antioxidant action is expected to lower intracellular ROS levels and reduce lipid peroxidation, thereby protecting RPE cell membranes from the oxidative effects linked to drusen formation. This reduction in oxidative stress, coupled with the preservation of mitochondrial function, is anticipated to result in decreased activation of apoptotic pathways. The compound is also predicted to trigger Nrf2-dependent transcription of antioxidant genes within RPE cells—a response that is critical for combating the chronic oxidative stress implicated in the pathogenesis of dry AMD (Clementi et al., 2022, pp. 7–10, 12–13). 

Functionally, these molecular effects should translate into measurable improvements in cellular assays of mitochondrial function and viability. In vitro, one would expect to observe maintained or restored Δψm and ATP production in idebenone-treated RPE cells versus controls exposed to oxidative stress. In a more integrated system, such as in animal models or clinical assessments, improvements in photopic ERG responses—previously demonstrated in LHON patients under idebenone therapy—may serve as a surrogate marker for the preservation of retinal neuronal function and integrity (Lyseng-Williamson, 2016, pp. 1–2). Nonetheless, although the mechanistic rationale is robust, these expected effects depend critically on fine-tuning dosage to avoid the potential cytotoxicity observed at supra-therapeutic concentrations and to account for inter-patient variability in enzymes such as NQO1 that activate idebenone (Varricchio et al., 2020, pp. 18–20).

Overall Evaluation:  
The evidence reviewed supports idebenone as a promising repurposed therapeutic candidate for the treatment of dry age-related macular degeneration. Among its key strengths is its dual action: the capacity to serve as an alternative electron shuttle to bypass complex I defects and the potent antioxidant activity that collectively mitigate mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative damage. These properties are especially relevant to dry AMD, where RPE degeneration and mitochondrial impairment form the basis of disease progression (Clementi et al., 2022, pp. 12–13; Arend et al., 2015, pp. 2, 7–8).

Idebenone’s previous clinical approval and widespread use in LHON provide valuable precedent regarding its safety and efficacy in a retinal context. Moreover, preclinical data from in vitro studies in RPE cells offer compelling evidence regarding its ability to maintain mitochondrial membrane potential, sustain ATP synthesis, and modulate apoptotic pathways under oxidative stress conditions—mechanisms that are directly applicable to the pathophysiology of dry AMD (Clementi et al., 2022, pp. 7–10, 12–13; Arend et al., 2015, pp. 5–6). The activation of Nrf2 and subsequent upregulation of antioxidant defenses further consolidate its therapeutic potential (Clementi et al., 2022, pp. 7–10, 12–13).

Nevertheless, several challenges remain. The efficacy of idebenone in bypassing complex I defects may be restricted by factors such as NQO1 expression, and potential pro-oxidant effects at higher concentrations necessitate careful dose optimization. In addition, while data from LHON and certain RPE cell models are promising, direct evidence from robust preclinical models of dry AMD is still limited. Some studies addressing these mechanisms have appeared in journals with variable impact factors, which calls for cautious interpretation of the data and underscores the need for further high-quality, reproducible research (Varricchio et al., 2020, pp. 15–18; Varricchio et al., 2020, pp. 18–20).

In summary, idebenone’s multifaceted mechanism—comprising electron shuttling to complex III, robust ROS scavenging, modulation of apoptotic signaling, and activation of Nrf2-driven antioxidant responses—aligns well with the therapeutic requirements for mitigating the mitochondrial and oxidative stress pathology present in dry AMD. While the compound’s history in LHON provides a strong foundation, the repurposing of idebenone for dry AMD will require additional rigorous preclinical investigations focusing on AMD-relevant models, followed by carefully designed clinical trials to validate its efficacy, safety, and dosing in this context (Clementi et al., 2022, pp. 7–10, 12–13; Lyseng-Williamson, 2016, pp. 1–2; Gueven & Faldu, 2013, pp. 1–2).

Overall, idebenone represents a compelling candidate for further exploration in the therapeutic landscape of dry AMD. Its dual action addresses key pathophysiological mechanisms in the disease, and its established clinical use in a related ocular disorder provides a promising translational bridge. However, careful attention must be paid to the nuances of its mitochondrial interactions, dose-dependent effects, and the heterogeneous responses in different cellular contexts. Future research focusing on these aspects will be critical to determining whether idebenone can be successfully repurposed to prevent retinal degeneration and preserve vision in patients with dry AMD (Clementi et al., 2022, pp. 12–13; Arend et al., 2015, pp. 2, 7–8; Russo et al., 2024, pp. 7–9; Marchesi et al., 2024, pp. 19–20).
