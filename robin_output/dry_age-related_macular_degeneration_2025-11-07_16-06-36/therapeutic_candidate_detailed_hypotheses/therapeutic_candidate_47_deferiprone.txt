Proposal for Deferiprone (Sigma-Aldrich D5721)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Deferiprone is a synthetic iron‐chelating agent originally developed for the treatment of transfusional iron overload disorders, particularly in patients with thalassemia major. It belongs to the hydroxypyridinone class of therapeutic compounds and is characterized by its low molecular weight and lipophilic nature, features that allow it to penetrate cell membranes and cross the blood–brain and blood–retinal barriers (Li et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2025). Chemically, deferiprone is a bidentate ligand that interacts with iron by coordinating via its hydroxypyridinone groups, forming a neutral complex in a 3:1 stoichiometry with ferric iron (Fe3+), although some studies note its affinity for both Fe3+ and Fe2+ under certain conditions (Li et al., 2023). The synthetic origins of deferiprone involve chemical design strategies aimed at generating small, orally active chelators with selective affinity for iron over other metals such as zinc, copper, and aluminium (Li et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2025). As a member of the iron chelator drug class, deferiprone’s established use in treating systemic iron overload syndromes has provided it with a significant clinical track record over several decades, forming the basis for its repurposing in disorders where iron-induced oxidative damage plays a role.  

Therapeutic History:  
Deferiprone has been used clinically as an orally administered iron chelator in the management of transfusional iron overload, notably in patients with thalassemia major. This usage is supported by multiple clinical studies and reviews that address both its efficacy in reducing systemic iron burden as well as its associated hematologic risks, such as agranulocytosis and neutropenia, which occur in approximately 1% of patients (Zhao et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2021; Bhoiwala & Dunaief, 2016). Beyond its systemic application, preclinical models have demonstrated deferiprone’s potential for retinal protection. Several animal studies report that deferiprone, when administered systemically, can reduce retinal and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) iron levels in models of iron overload or light-induced degeneration and produce improvements in oxidative stress markers and retinal functional indices (Zhao et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2021; Bhoiwala & Dunaief, 2016). Although deferiprone has not been widely tested or approved specifically for dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) or similar ocular conditions in clinical trials, its documented experimental benefits in animal retinal models along with its established clinical safety profile in iron overload therapy have prompted its consideration for repurposing in dry AMD where iron-induced oxidative injury is implicated (Shahandeh et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2021; Ueda et al., 2018).  

Mechanism of Action:  
Deferiprone functions primarily by chelating labile iron, thereby limiting the pool of redox active iron that can participate in the Fenton reaction—a process in which ferrous iron (Fe2+) reacts with hydrogen peroxide to produce highly reactive hydroxyl radicals. The molecular mechanism of deferiprone is based on its bidentate hydroxypyridinone structure that binds Fe3+ to form a stable neutral complex in a 3:1 chelator to iron ratio, a coordination chemistry that has been well documented in several studies (Li et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2025). This complex formation effectively reduces the availability of free iron in the intracellular labile iron pool, thereby diminishing iron-catalyzed reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. In addition, some data suggest that deferiprone may also interact with ferrous iron under certain conditions, contributing further to its antioxidant effects by interrupting redox cycling associated with both Fe3+ and Fe2+ species (Zhao et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2021).  

From a biochemical standpoint, iron accumulation in the RPE and surrounding retinal tissues is known to promote lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial oxidative stress, leading to cellular dysfunction and death—a pathomechanism observed in dry AMD (Zhao et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023). Deferiprone’s cell permeability and ability to traverse biological barriers enables it to access the intracellular compartments, including mitochondria. Although direct studies examining deferiprone’s effect specifically on RPE mitochondrial iron pools are still limited, evidence from other systems (such as neuronal and cardiac tissues) demonstrates that reducing mitochondrial iron load can preserve electron transport chain (ETC) integrity by preventing the formation of deleterious hydroxyl radicals that otherwise damage mitochondrial lipids, proteins, and DNA (Li et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2023). Furthermore, preclinical models of retinal injury have shown that deferiprone treatment leads to a reduction in oxidative stress markers, including decreased malondialdehyde (MDA) levels and a concomitant increase in antioxidants such as glutathione (GSH), which supports the hypothesis that iron chelation by deferiprone interrupts the cascade of iron-mediated oxidative damage (Shahandeh et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2025). There is also emerging evidence that iron chelators like deferiprone can mitigate cell death pathways such as ferroptosis and parthanatos in retinal tissues, contributing to neuroprotective outcomes (Villarejo-Zori et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025). In summary, the mechanism of action of deferiprone is multifaceted: it directly reduces the labile iron concentration, thereby lowering the potential for Fenton chemistry, indirectly stabilizes mitochondrial function, and may influence downstream iron-dependent cellular pathways that are implicated in retinal degeneration (Li et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2025).  

Expected Effect:  
The central hypothesis driving the repurposing of deferiprone for dry AMD is that chelation of labile iron in RPE mitochondria will prevent Fenton reaction–mediated ROS formation and maintain the integrity of the ETC. In the proposed assay and eventual clinical application, treatment with deferiprone is expected to significantly reduce intracellular labile iron, thereby attenuating oxidative stress that leads to lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial dysfunction. This, in turn, should result in a lower rate of RPE cell death and preserve photoreceptor viability, thereby potentially slowing the progression of dry AMD (Liu et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023).  

Experimental studies have shown that oral administration of deferiprone in animal models results in a measurable decrease in retinal iron load without causing retinal toxicity—a finding that is pivotal for any drug intended for chronic ophthalmic use (Zhao et al., 2025; Bhoiwala & Dunaief, 2016). In parallel, in vitro studies using retinal cells such as ARPE-19 have demonstrated that deferiprone reduces markers of oxidative stress, including lower MDA levels and preservation of antioxidant enzyme activities (for example, GPX4 upregulation), which are indicative of reduced ROS-mediated damage (Yang et al., 2025; Shahandeh et al., 2020). The proposed mechanism of action implies that by decreasing the availability of iron necessary for Fenton chemistry, deferiprone will decrease the production of hydroxyl radicals, thus protecting mitochondrial membranes and proteins integral to the ETC from oxidative damage (Li et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2023). Moreover, since mitochondrial dysfunction is a known contributor to RPE degeneration in dry AMD, its preservation via iron chelation is expected to sustain cellular ATP production and overall cell viability (Zhao et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023). Given that the RPE expresses iron regulatory proteins and that dysregulated iron metabolism has been observed in AMD retinas (with increased ferritin and altered transferrin receptor expression), the application of deferiprone would ideally restore homeostasis in the labile iron pool and thereby ameliorate the chronic oxidative insult observed in dry AMD (Li et al., 2023; Ashok et al., 2021).  

Overall Evaluation:  
Deferiprone presents several strengths as a candidate for repurposing in dry AMD therapy. Firstly, its well-established clinical use in treating systemic iron overload confers a robust foundation regarding its pharmacokinetics, dosing parameters, and safety profile—despite noted hematologic risks such as agranulocytosis that occur in a small percentage of patients (Zhao et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2021; Bhoiwala & Dunaief, 2016). Its oral bioavailability and ability to cross the blood–retinal barrier make it uniquely suited for targeting intracellular iron in the retina (Li et al., 2023). Secondly, there is compelling preclinical evidence from various retinal degeneration models that iron chelators, including deferiprone, can reduce retinal iron accumulation, diminish oxidative stress, and improve functional outcomes, which supports the rationale for its repurposing in the context of dry AMD (Zhao et al., 2021; Bhoiwala & Dunaief, 2016; Zhao et al., 2025). Thirdly, its mode of action—chelating labile iron to prevent Fenton reaction–mediated ROS production—directly targets a central pathological mechanism implicated in dry AMD. The hypothesis that reducing mitochondrial iron load will preserve ETC integrity is biologically plausible given the importance of iron-catalyzed oxidative stress in mitochondrial dysfunction within the RPE (Li et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2023).  

On the other hand, several weaknesses and uncertainties remain. Although deferiprone’s effectiveness in reducing systemic and retinal iron overload has been demonstrated in animal models, direct evidence of its capacity to specifically target RPE mitochondrial iron and preserve ETC function in human RPE cells is currently limited (Zhao et al., 2025; Li et al., 2023). The precise molecular details regarding how deferiprone modulates mitochondrial iron trafficking, and how this relates to the functional maintenance of the electron transport chain, require further investigation. Additionally, while preclinical data are supportive, deferiprone has not yet been tested in clinical trials specifically for dry AMD, and its long-term safety and efficacy in the ocular context remain to be established (Zhao et al., 2021; Ueda et al., 2018). Another consideration is the risk of adverse systemic effects, such as neutropenia and agranulocytosis, which, although infrequent, could limit its utility in a population of elderly patients with dry AMD who may have co‐morbidities (Bhoiwala & Dunaief, 2016). Moreover, some reports have suggested that under low concentrations or certain conditions, iron chelators might paradoxically participate in free radical formation, necessitating precise dose titration and careful monitoring (Li et al., 2023; Bhoiwala & Dunaief, 2016).  

In summary, deferiprone appears to be a promising therapeutic candidate for dry AMD based on its well-documented ability to chelate labile iron, its favorable pharmacologic properties including oral bioavailability and tissue penetration, and preclinical evidence showing retinal protection. Its mechanism of action—reducing the labile iron pool to limit Fenton reaction–induced oxidative stress—addresses a central pathogenic mechanism of dry AMD, namely, iron accumulation leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and lipid peroxidation in the RPE (Zhao et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023). However, the extrapolation of these preclinical findings to human dry AMD requires additional studies, particularly those that directly measure mitochondrial iron dynamics and ETC activity in RPE cells following deferiprone treatment. Rigorous dose-ranging and safety studies will also be necessary to mitigate the risk of hematologic adverse effects. Therefore, while deferiprone has significant potential as a repurposed drug candidate for dry AMD, further preclinical mechanistic studies and early-phase clinical trials are essential to fully validate its efficacy and safety in this new therapeutic context (Zhao et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2021; Ueda et al., 2018).
