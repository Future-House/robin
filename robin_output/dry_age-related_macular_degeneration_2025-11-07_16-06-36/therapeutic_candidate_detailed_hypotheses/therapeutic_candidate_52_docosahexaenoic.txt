Proposal for Docosahexaenoic acid (DHA; Sigma-Aldrich D2534)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) is a naturally occurring long-chain omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid (LC-PUFA) that has been isolated from marine sources, such as fish oil, and is commercially available in a highly purified form (e.g., Sigma-Aldrich D2534). DHA is synthesized via metabolic elongation and desaturation of dietary alpha-linolenic acid (ALA) in mammals; however, this endogenous conversion is limited, and direct dietary intake is critical for maintaining adequate retinal DHA levels (Figueiredo et al., 2024, pp. 13–14). Belonging to the omega-3 class of fatty acids, DHA has long been recognized for its important roles in maintaining membrane structure and function, particularly in neural tissues and the retina where it is highly concentrated in photoreceptor outer segments and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) membranes (Dziedziak et al., 2021, pp. 7–9; Johnson & Schaefer, 2006, pp. 2–3). Its polyunsaturated nature—with six cis double bonds—confers unique biophysical properties, enabling DHA to increase membrane fluidity and participate in critical signaling cascades. The compound has been used extensively as a nutritional supplement and has been incorporated in various formulations aimed at cardiovascular health, cognitive function, and ocular protection, establishing a robust safety profile over decades of human use (Huang et al., 2020, pp. 5–7; Skowronska-Krawczyk & Chao, 2019, pp. 3–4).

Therapeutic History:  
Over the past two decades, extensive research has explored the role of DHA and related omega-3 fatty acids in maintaining retinal function and potentially preventing age-related macular degeneration (AMD). Epidemiologic studies have consistently demonstrated an inverse association between dietary fish and high omega-3 fatty acid intake and the risk of developing AMD, suggesting that populations with higher DHA consumption tend to exhibit a lower incidence of both early and, to some extent, advanced forms of the disease (Querques & Souied, 2014, pp. 3–5; Huang et al., 2020, pp. 5–7). In clinical settings, large-scale randomized controlled trials, such as AREDS2, incorporated DHA (and EPA) into a multi-nutrient formula designed originally for AMD, though the overall randomized trial outcomes have been mixed, with some studies finding no statistically significant benefit for progression to late AMD (Zeppieri et al., 2024, pp. 4–5; LaFuente et al., 2021, pp. 7–8). Pilot clinical trials and observational cohort studies, including NAT-1 and NAT-2, have assessed DHA supplementation in subjects with drusenoid lesions or early-stage AMD; while some of these studies have shown promising improvements in blood biomarkers (e.g., increased red blood cell DHA levels) and correlations with reduced AMD progression, the clinical efficacy remains under debate (Querques et al., 2016, pp. 2–4; Querques et al., 2011, pp. 7–8). Notably, evidence from animal models suggests that dietary DHA may protect against light-induced retinal degeneration and reduce the accumulation of deleterious lipid peroxidation products, although some preclinical studies also caution that DHA’s susceptibility to oxidation could, under certain conditions, worsen retinal injury (Dziedziak et al., 2021, pp. 7–9; LaFuente et al., 2021, pp. 4–5).

Mechanism of Action:  
The therapeutic hypothesis for using DHA in dry AMD is based on its predicted ability to incorporate into the mitochondrial membranes of RPE cells, thereby enhancing membrane fluidity and protecting against oxidative damage to the electron transport chain (ETC) complexes. Under normal cellular conditions, DHA—owing to its multiple methylene-interrupted cis double bonds—integrates into phospholipid bilayers and modulates biophysical properties such as fluidity, permeability, and thickness (Johnson & Schaefer, 2006, pp. 2–3; Walchuk & Suh, 2020, pp. 30–33). In mitochondrial membranes, this incorporation is thought to replace more saturated fatty acids such as arachidonic acid, ultimately reducing lipid peroxidation and mitigating oxidative stress that is deleterious to ETC function (Dziedziak et al., 2021, pp. 7–9). In vitro studies have shown that DHA supplementation in retinal cell cultures can decrease markers of apoptosis and preserve mitochondrial membrane integrity, with a concomitant increase in anti-apoptotic proteins (e.g., Bcl-2) (Dziedziak et al., 2021, pp. 7–9; Zeppieri et al., 2024, pp. 2–4).  
In addition, DHA is enzymatically converted under conditions of oxidative stress into neuroprotective docosanoids, such as neuroprotectin D1 (NPD1), which can inhibit pro-inflammatory gene expression and reduce cell death in RPE and photoreceptor cells (Longoni & Demontis, 2023, pp. 12–14; LaFuente et al., 2021, p. 13). This conversion not only contributes to countering local inflammation but also supports cellular health by modulating intracellular signaling pathways, particularly those involving NF-κB and other transcription factors critical for managing oxidative stress (Figueiredo et al., 2024, pp. 13–14; Longoni & Demontis, 2023, pp. 12–14). Despite these promising mechanistic insights, it is important to note that direct, quantitative evidence detailing DHA’s incorporation specifically into RPE mitochondrial membranes and its precise effects on mitochondrial cristae structure, malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, or oxygen consumption rate (OCR) remains limited in the current literature (Zeppieri et al., 2024, pp. 4–5; LaFuente et al., 2021, p. 13). Several reviews and preclinical reports acknowledge the theoretical framework while emphasizing the need for more detailed mechanistic studies that directly measure these mitochondrial endpoints (Li et al., 2024, pp. 10–11; Walchuk & Suh, 2020, pp. 30–33).

Expected Effect:  
Based on the mechanistic hypothesis, DHA supplementation is expected to yield several beneficial effects in retinal cells, particularly within the RPE, which are central to the pathogenesis of dry AMD. In an ideal in vitro or ex vivo assay, DHA would incorporate into RPE cell membranes—including the mitochondrial inner membrane—increasing fluidity and enabling better diffusion of substrates and electron carriers necessary for efficient ATP production. Enhanced mitochondrial membrane fluidity should help reduce the susceptibility of the electron transport chain to oxidative damage, thereby lowering the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the subsequent production of lipid peroxidation byproducts such as MDA (Dziedziak et al., 2021, pp. 7–9; LaFuente et al., 2021, pp. 4–5). Furthermore, preservation of mitochondrial cristae structure is anticipated to maintain optimal alignment and function of ETC complexes, leading to an improved oxygen consumption rate (OCR) that is directly linked to cellular energy metabolism and overall cell viability (Zeppieri et al., 2024, pp. 2–4; Souied et al., 2016, p. 8).  
At a molecular level, the increased presence of DHA in mitochondrial membranes is expected to initiate a cascade of protective events: reduced oxidative stress and inflammation via inhibition of NF-κB activation, promotion of the antioxidant response through NFE2L2 (NRF2) pathway activation, and enhanced autophagic clearance of damaged proteins and organelles through upregulation of SQSTM1 (Querques & Souied, 2014, pp. 1–2; Johnson & Schaefer, 2006, p. 3). Consequently, these effects should translate into preservation of RPE cell function, reduced apoptosis, and maintenance of photoreceptor health—an effect that could help slow the progression of dry AMD by stabilizing the retinal environment and preventing drusen accumulation and other degenerative changes (Huang et al., 2020, pp. 5–7; Querques & Souied, 2014, pp. 3–5). Additionally, because genes encoding crucial direct mediators of DHA uptake such as Mfsd2a are expressed in RPE cells and photoreceptors, the target tissue is particularly well-positioned to benefit from increased dietary or supplementary DHA (Fathima et al., 2023, p. 8). However, it is also recognized that despite the strong rationale, direct experimental confirmation of these outcomes—specifically measured improvements in MDA levels, cristae ultrastructure integrity, and OCR in human RPE cells—is still pending, and the observed epidemiologic correlations are not yet unequivocally supported by randomized clinical trial data (Zeppieri et al., 2024, pp. 4–5; Li et al., 2024, pp. 10–11).

Overall Evaluation:  
The proposition to repurpose DHA for dry AMD is underpinned by compelling mechanistic rationale and epidemiologic associations, yet it is not without its challenges and limitations. One clear strength is DHA’s established role as an essential component of retinal cell membranes. Its high concentration in photoreceptors and the RPE is functionally integral to maintaining visual function and cellular homeostasis, lending strong biological plausibility to its protective potential in AMD (Johnson & Schaefer, 2006, pp. 2–3; Walchuk & Suh, 2020, pp. 30–33). Its ability to modulate membrane fluidity, serve as a precursor to neuroprotective mediators like NPD1, and exert anti-inflammatory and antioxidant actions provide multiple mechanistic pathways through which it may mitigate the cellular stressors implicated in dry AMD pathogenesis (Longoni & Demontis, 2023, pp. 12–14; Figueiredo et al., 2024, pp. 13–14).  
From a therapeutic history perspective, DHA is well understood in the context of nutritional supplementation, and numerous epidemiologic studies point toward a lower risk of AMD with higher dietary DHA intake (Querques & Souied, 2014, pp. 3–5; Huang et al., 2020, pp. 5–7). The safety profile of DHA is also well established, making it an attractive candidate for repurposing. However, the clinical trial data to date (including large-scale studies such as AREDS2 and smaller trials like NAT-2) have not consistently demonstrated a clear benefit in altering the progression of advanced AMD, and it remains uncertain whether observed benefits in intermediate AMD or specific cellular endpoints translate into clinically meaningful outcomes in the dry form of the disease (Zeppieri et al., 2024, pp. 4–5; LaFuente et al., 2021, pp. 7–8).  
A significant weakness in the current body of research is the lack of direct mechanistic studies that quantitatively document DHA incorporation into RPE mitochondrial membranes and its subsequent effects on mitochondrial parameters. In our hypothesis, improved mitochondrial function is key, with expected reductions in MDA levels, preservation of cristae structure, and enhancement of OCR serving as biomarkers of efficacy. Yet, while several in vitro studies and animal experiments have suggested that DHA can reduce markers of oxidative stress and improve mitochondrial integrity in retinal models, the literature provided does not include rigorous, high-resolution measurements of these specific parameters in human RPE cells (Li et al., 2024, pp. 10–11; Zeppieri et al., 2024, pp. 4–5; Jiang et al., 2021, pp. 7–8). This gap in the direct evidence would need to be addressed through targeted preclinical assays employing electron microscopy, lipidomics, and mitochondrial respirometry to validate the proposed mechanism.  
Furthermore, the mode of formulation and bioavailability of DHA is a critical factor that may contribute to the mixed findings in clinical trials; DHA administered as an ethyl ester may have different absorption and incorporation profiles compared to its triglyceride-bound form, and the optimal dosing necessary to achieve sufficient retinal or mitochondrial enrichment remains unclear (Souied et al., 2016, pp. 6–7; LaFuente et al., 2021, pp. 7–8). Potential pro-oxidant effects of DHA in settings where oxidation is excessive must also be carefully monitored, as some animal studies have reported that under specific conditions DHA accumulation may even exacerbate oxidative damage (Dziedziak et al., 2021, pp. 7–9; LaFuente et al., 2021, pp. 4–5).  
In summary, while the epidemiologic literature and preclinical studies provide an encouraging backdrop for the repurposing of DHA as a therapeutic agent in dry AMD—by showing that high DHA intake is associated with a reduced risk of AMD and that DHA can confer mitochondrial and anti-oxidative benefits in retinal models—the critical mechanistic endpoints that support our specific hypothesis are not yet fully substantiated in the published literature. The overall evaluation is that DHA has substantial mechanistic promise, safety, and historical use as a nutritional supplement; however, further robust mechanistic studies and carefully designed clinical trials are necessary to validate its specific effects on mitochondrial function in RPE cells and to determine whether these biochemical improvements translate into a clinically significant slowing of dry AMD progression (Huang et al., 2020, pp. 5–7; Querques & Souied, 2014, pp. 3–5; Zeppieri et al., 2024, pp. 4–5).
