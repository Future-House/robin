Proposal for AICAR (5-Aminoimidazole-4-carboxamide ribonucleotide; Sigma-Aldrich A9978)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
AICAR (5‐Aminoimidazole‐4‐carboxamide ribonucleotide) is a synthetic nucleoside analog originally developed as an AMP mimetic agent that is capable of activating AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) across a range of cell types. It is produced by chemical synthesis and is available commercially (e.g., Sigma-Aldrich A9978), having been studied for several decades in the context of cellular energy metabolism and stress signaling. As a member of a class of compounds that mimic the natural intracellular messenger AMP, AICAR is taken up into cells via adenosine transporters and is phosphorylated by adenosine kinase into its active metabolite, ZMP (5‐aminoimidazole‐4‐carboxamide ribonucleotide monophosphate), which then mimics AMP in its interactions with AMPK (Efstathiou et al., 2020). This compound belongs to a well‐established class of metabolic modulators, and similar agents have been used in experimental settings to modulate glucose uptake, fatty acid oxidation, and overall energy homeostasis in both basic science and translational research contexts (Al‐Moujahed et al., 2014). In addition, several preclinical studies have evaluated AICAR’s potential as an “exercise mimetic” due to its ability to enhance energy metabolism in muscle and other tissues, and its synthetic nature allows for reproducible and scalable dosing in research models (Hyttinen et al., 2011).  

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, AICAR was developed primarily as an agent to activate AMPK in metabolic studies and has been investigated in a variety of preclinical models ranging from cancer to cardiovascular disease. In the oncology field, for example, AICAR has been shown to inhibit cancer cell proliferation, induce cell cycle arrest, and promote programmed cell death in several cancer types via AMPK-dependent modulation of pathways such as PI3K-Akt and mTOR (Al‐Moujahed et al., 2014; Rattan et al., 2005). Clinical studies, particularly those in the cardiovascular arena, have also evaluated AICAR’s safety profile in the context of ischemia-reperfusion injury, including human trials during cardiac surgery, demonstrating that it can be administered safely in humans (Rattan et al., 2005). In the context of ocular research, AICAR has been studied for its effects on retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells and other ocular cell types; several studies have demonstrated that AICAR suppresses the production of pro-inflammatory complement components such as complement factor 3 (C3) and complement factor B (CFB) in RPE cells under inflammatory conditions (Efstathiou et al., 2020; Chung et al., 2017). Although there is no established clinical use in dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) to date, these preclinical observations, combined with its well-known pharmacological profile, have spurred interest in repurposing AICAR for treating retinal diseases characterized by oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction (Dang et al., 2021; Efstathiou et al., 2020). Furthermore, studies examining AMPK activators in the eye – including those using AICAR in animal models of uveitis or oxidative stress-induced RPE damage – support the notion that activating this metabolic pathway can protect against retinal degeneration (Hyttinen et al., 2011).  

Mechanism of Action:  
AICAR exerts its biological activity primarily through the activation of AMPK, a master regulator of cellular energy balance that responds to alterations in the AMP/ATP ratio. Once AICAR is imported into cells through adenosine transporters, it is phosphorylated by adenosine kinase to form ZMP, an analog of AMP. ZMP binds to the regulatory γ subunit of AMPK, causing a conformational change that facilitates AMPK phosphorylation at Thr172 by upstream kinases such as LKB1, thereby fully activating the kinase (Efstathiou et al., 2020). Activated AMPK then phosphorylates a series of downstream targets involved in energy metabolism, including acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) and elements of the mTOR signaling pathway. In RPE cells, this activation is particularly important because AMPK phosphorylates and activates ULK1, a kinase critical for initiating autophagy and mitophagy, thereby promoting the clearance of damaged mitochondria (Hyttinen et al., 2011; Chung et al., 2017).  

A central component of the rationale for using AICAR in dry AMD is its ability to stimulate the transcriptional coactivator PGC-1α. AMPK-mediated phosphorylation of PGC-1α enhances its activity and nuclear translocation, which in turn drives the transcription of genes involved in mitochondrial biogenesis, antioxidant defense, and metabolic adaptation (Hu et al., 2024; Nita & Grzybowski, 2023). This mechanism is particularly attractive in dry AMD because mitochondrial dysfunction in RPE cells is thought to contribute significantly to disease pathogenesis by impairing oxidative phosphorylation, increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and triggering cell senescence or death (Tong et al., 2022; Rego, 2023). In addition to these AMPK-dependent effects, some studies have reported that AICAR exerts certain effects independent of AMPK activation. For instance, AICAR has been shown to suppress TNF-α-induced complement factor expression by mechanisms that do not involve AMPK, suggesting that alternative signaling pathways or direct modulation of transcription factor activity might also contribute to its overall activity profile (Chung et al., 2017; Kirchner et al., 2018). These AMPK-independent actions could either broaden the therapeutic potential of AICAR in modulating inflammatory responses or introduce complexity into its activity profile that will need to be carefully isolated and understood (Kirchner et al., 2018; Yu et al., 2024).  

Furthermore, AICAR has been observed to affect mitochondrial dynamics not only by stimulating biogenesis through PGC-1α activation but also by modifying autophagic flux through mTOR inhibition. In models of RPE oxidative stress, where excessive ROS levels lead to mitochondrial fragmentation and accumulation of dysfunctional organelles, AICAR-induced AMPK activation facilitates the clearance of damaged mitochondria via mitophagy, thus restoring mitochondrial quality and energy production (Fisher et al., 2022; Hyttinen et al., 2011; Yu et al., 2024). Moreover, experimental evidence suggests that AICAR can modulate the balance between cellular glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation by shifting metabolism toward a more energy-efficient, oxidative phenotype, thereby increasing the overall oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and potentially improving cellular resilience under oxidative stress (Chan et al., 2020; Fisher, 2022).  

Expected Effect:  
Given its mechanisms of action, the expected effect of AICAR in an assay aimed at treating dry AMD would be multifaceted. In retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells exposed to chronic oxidative stress—a hallmark of dry AMD—AICAR treatment is expected to lead to robust activation of AMPK, as measured by increased phosphorylation of both AMPK and its downstream substrates such as ACC and ULK1 (Guo et al., 2018; Nita & Grzybowski, 2023). This activation should in turn upregulate PGC-1α, resulting in enhanced transcription of genes involved in mitochondrial biogenesis, improved mitochondrial DNA replication, and an increased capacity for ATP production (Hu et al., 2024). As damaged mitochondria are a major source of ROS in stressed RPE cells, the stimulation of autophagy and mitophagy is anticipated to selectively clear these dysfunctional organelles, reducing cellular oxidative stress and preventing the secondary damage that contributes to RPE degeneration (Hyttinen et al., 2011).  

In addition to these metabolic effects, AICAR is also expected to attenuate inflammatory responses within the RPE and adjacent retinal tissues. For instance, by modulating the activity of nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) and reducing the expression of inflammatory mediators such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and complement components (e.g., C3 and CFB), AICAR would help to create a less pro-inflammatory microenvironment that could further protect RPE cells from damage (Efstathiou et al., 2020; Chung et al., 2017; Kirchner et al., 2018). These effects are particularly pertinent given that chronic, low-grade inflammation coupled with complement dysregulation is a recognized component of dry AMD pathogenesis.  

At the cellular level, the outcome of AICAR-induced AMPK activation is expected to manifest as enhanced mitochondrial function, improved energy charge (ATP/AMP ratio), reduced ROS accumulation, and, ultimately, a restoration of normal metabolic and autophagic flux in RPE cells (Fisher et al., 2022; Fisher, 2022). This improved bioenergetic profile should translate into protection against RPE cell death, potentially slowing or halting the progression of geographic atrophy associated with dry AMD (Yu et al., 2024; Si et al., 2023). Moreover, since RPE dysfunction in AMD also involves a reduction in the phagocytic clearance of photoreceptor outer segments, AICAR’s modulatory effects on autophagy may indirectly help to restore the homeostatic functions of RPE cells, reducing the accumulation of damaging lipofuscin and drusen material (Qin & De Vries, 2008; Sung et al., 2015). In summary, the expected effect is a dual-action benefit—mitigation of oxidative and endoplasmic reticulum stresses via improved mitochondrial dynamics and an anti-inflammatory effect stemming from a reduction in complement activation and NF-κB-driven cytokine production (Dang et al., 2021; Efstathiou et al., 2020).  

Overall Evaluation:  
AICAR represents a promising therapeutic candidate for dry age-related macular degeneration owing to its robust and well-characterized mechanism of action via AMPK activation. The strengths of this candidate lie in its dual capacity to modulate critical pathways implicated in AMD pathogenesis: on one hand, AICAR enhances mitochondrial biogenesis and promotes the removal of damaged mitochondria through autophagy/mitophagy, and on the other, it attenuates inflammatory cascades that exacerbate RPE cell damage (Nita & Grzybowski, 2023; Hu et al., 2024). The ability of AICAR to upregulate PGC-1α is particularly compelling because mitochondrial dysfunction is now widely recognized as a central element in dry AMD. Preclinical studies have illustrated that dysregulation of mitochondrial function in RPE cells—a decrease in oxidative phosphorylation capacity, increased ROS production, and impaired clearance of damaged organelles—is pivotal in the initiation and progression of AMD (Tong et al., 2022; Rego, 2023). Thus, a compound like AICAR that can simultaneously restore mitochondrial health and mitigate inflammatory responses offers a multifaceted approach to disease modification.  

From the therapeutic history perspective, AICAR has been extensively studied in various preclinical models, including those of ocular inflammation and oxidative stress in RPE cells. Its ability to safely activate AMPK in vivo has been demonstrated across multiple species and contexts, including settings that mimic some aspects of AMD-related stress, such as endotoxin-induced uveitis and sodium iodate-induced RPE damage (Hyttinen et al., 2011; Chan et al., 2020). Additionally, the compound’s known clinical safety profile in human studies—albeit in contexts outside ophthalmology—provides an encouraging starting point for repurposing it for dry AMD, where no approved therapeutics currently exist (Efstathiou et al., 2020; Chung et al., 2017).  

However, there are notable weaknesses and challenges that must be addressed. First, while AMPK activation is generally beneficial for energy homeostasis and autophagy, certain studies have indicated that AICAR’s effects on RPE cell biology might be context-dependent. For instance, some experimental reports have shown that AICAR can reduce the phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments by RPE cells—a process that is essential for retinal health—which may represent a double-edged sword if not carefully titrated (Qin & De Vries, 2008; Yu et al., 2024). In addition, the existence of AMPK-independent effects—such as the direct inhibition of complement factor expression—introduces complexity into the overall mechanism of action and makes it challenging to predict all downstream outcomes. Differential responses have also been observed between healthy and diseased donor RPE cells, implying that “metabolic memory” or cellular adaptation may influence drug responsiveness (Fisher et al., 2022; Chung et al., 2017). These considerations suggest that while the theoretical underpinnings for using AICAR in dry AMD are solid, the translation of these effects into clinical efficacy may require careful optimization of dosing, delivery, and patient selection.  

Another potential limitation is related to its broad mechanism of action. AICAR activates several metabolic pathways that are intertwined with many aspects of cellular physiology. While this pleiotropy provides the therapeutic benefit of addressing multiple dysregulated pathways in AMD simultaneously, it may also increase the risk of off-target effects or metabolic disturbances in tissues outside the retina (Rattan et al., 2005; Al‐Moujahed et al., 2014). For instance, systemic administration of AICAR has been associated with shifts in glycolytic flux and modifications of lipid metabolism, and it is therefore imperative that strategies for ocular delivery (such as intravitreal injections or topical formulations) be developed to maximize retinal exposure while minimizing systemic exposure (Chan et al., 2020; Kamoshita et al., 2014).  

Moreover, despite the substantial body of preclinical data supporting the role of AICAR in activating AMPK and restoring mitochondrial function, clinical data specific to dry AMD are lacking. Although related studies have shown benefits in models of retinal inflammation and degeneration, the exact therapeutic window, long-term safety, and efficacy in humans with AMD must be thoroughly evaluated in well-designed clinical trials. It is also worth mentioning that other AMPK activators, such as metformin, have independently demonstrated protective effects in AMD models, thereby lending credence to the approach but also raising questions about whether AICAR offers a distinct advantage over these agents (Dang et al., 2021; Efstathiou et al., 2020).  

In summary, the overall evaluation of AICAR as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD is cautiously optimistic. Its well-documented ability to activate AMPK, stimulate PGC-1α-driven mitochondrial biogenesis, and induce autophagy presents a strong mechanistic rationale for protecting RPE cells from the mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress that drive dry AMD pathogenesis (Hu et al., 2024; Nita & Grzybowski, 2023; Hyttinen et al., 2011). The preclinical evidence, gathered from studies in ocular models and other systems, provides encouraging proof-of-concept data that support further investigation (Efstathiou et al., 2020; Hyttinen et al., 2011). Nonetheless, complexities related to AMPK-independent effects, differential responses in healthy versus diseased cells, and potential off-target metabolic changes underscore the need for rigorous preclinical optimization and carefully designed clinical trials before this candidate can be definitively validated for dry AMD treatment (Chung et al., 2017; Fisher et al., 2022; Kirchner et al., 2018).  

Considering all the available literature, the repurposing of AICAR for dry AMD appears to be a promising strategy that merits further investigation. The compound’s ability to simultaneously address mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation positions it as a multifaceted agent with the potential to modify disease progression in a condition that currently lacks effective therapeutic options (Chung et al., 2017; Dang et al., 2021; Efstathiou et al., 2020). Moving forward, it will be critical to further elucidate the detailed cellular and molecular responses to AICAR in human RPE cells, determine the optimal dosing and delivery parameters specific to the ocular environment, and perform in vivo studies that mimic the complex pathobiology of dry AMD. With these additional efforts, AICAR could eventually emerge as a valuable addition to the limited therapeutic arsenal available for managing this debilitating condition.  

In conclusion, AICAR’s robust preclinical profile as an AMPK activator, its well-understood mechanisms of promoting mitochondrial biogenesis and autophagy, and its capacity to mitigate inflammatory responses provide a solid scientific basis for its potential repurposing in dry AMD (Hu et al., 2024; Hyttinen et al., 2011; Nita & Grzybowski, 2023). However, several challenges remain: the dual nature of its effects (both AMPK-dependent and independent), the possibility of adverse effects on key RPE functions such as phagocytosis, and the need for precise ocular drug delivery methods. Therefore, while AICAR is an exciting candidate with considerable mechanistic appeal, its translation from bench to bedside in the treatment of dry AMD will require further detailed studies to fully map its therapeutic window, safety profile, and efficacy in human subjects (Al‐Moujahed et al., 2014; Rego, 2023; Kamoshita et al., 2014). Continued rigorous research and methodical clinical evaluation will determine whether AICAR can be developed into an effective treatment modality for this currently intractable form of macular degeneration.
