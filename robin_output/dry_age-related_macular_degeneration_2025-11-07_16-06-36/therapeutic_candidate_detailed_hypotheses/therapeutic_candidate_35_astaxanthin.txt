Proposal for Astaxanthin (Sigma-Aldrich A9465)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Astaxanthin is a naturally occurring carotenoid belonging to the xanthophyll subclass, first discovered from marine sources such as lobsters and various microalgae, notably Haematococcus pluvialis (Yang & Wang, 2022, pp. 1–2). This red-orange pigment exhibits exceptional antioxidant properties that have been exploited both in natural extraction and through large‐scale synthetic production, with approximately 95% produced synthetically for enhanced economic viability (Giannaccare et al., 2020, pp. 1–3). Its amphipathic structure, characterized by a long polyene chain terminated with polar functional groups, allows it to integrate into both hydrophilic and hydrophobic environments, a property that is fundamental to its ability to span and stabilize cell membranes (D’Angelo et al., 2024, pp. 3–4; Rodella et al., 2023, pp. 11–13). Astaxanthin is a representative member of a broader class of antioxidants that have historically been used as nutraceuticals and dietary supplements, targeting a range of conditions including cardiovascular, dermatologic, and neurodegenerative diseases. Over the decades, the unique biochemical properties of astaxanthin have motivated extensive research into its therapeutic potential, particularly for conditions associated with oxidative stress. Its regulatory approval as a food supplement in the early 1990s, together with subsequent studies demonstrating its anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic capabilities, have established astaxanthin as a promising candidate for further exploration in the context of ocular diseases (Yang & Wang, 2022, pp. 1–2; D’Angelo et al., 2024, pp. 4–5).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, astaxanthin has been investigated and utilized in various biomedical contexts due to its robust antioxidant function. Clinical research and preclinical models have repeatedly shown that astaxanthin improves retinal function and visual acuity in patients with ocular diseases, including both early and nonadvanced stages of age-related macular degeneration (AMD) (D’Angelo et al., 2024, pp. 4–5; Giannaccare et al., 2020, pp. 5–7). In addition to its application in AMD, astaxanthin has been evaluated for therapeutic effects in conditions such as diabetic retinopathy, glaucoma, and dry eye syndrome, demonstrating broad-spectrum protective activities against the oxidative and inflammatory insults commonly underlying these disorders (Lixi et al., 2024, pp. 2–5; Fong, 2023, pp. 7–10). Animal models have provided evidence that dietary supplementation with astaxanthin can mitigate photoreceptor degeneration, reduce choroidal neovascularization (CNV) in models of exudative AMD, and suppress retinal cell apoptosis induced by light or xenobiotic stress (Giannaccare et al., 2020, pp. 3–7; D’Angelo et al., 2024, pp. 4–5). Despite being explored predominantly for ocular applications, astaxanthin’s history is not limited to ophthalmology alone; its neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory properties have been leveraged in studies addressing broader neurodegenerative and systemic conditions, emphasizing a solid profile for safety and translational potential (Lixi et al., 2024, pp. 2–5). Clinical studies have been encouraging in demonstrating that astaxanthin, when administered as a dietary supplement, exhibits an excellent safety profile with minimal adverse effects, thereby facilitating its clinical translation for chronic, age-related ocular conditions (D’Angelo et al., 2024, pp. 4–5, 13–14). Despite these promising reports, it is important to note that while most available studies indicate significant benefits, the literature also highlights the necessity for further research to determine optimal dosages and to overcome challenges related to bioavailability (D’Angelo et al., 2024, pp. 4–5; Lixi et al., 2024, pp. 10–11).

Mechanism of Action:  
The therapeutic potential of astaxanthin in dry age-related macular degeneration (dry AMD) is thought to stem from its unparalleled antioxidant activity, which is mechanistically linked to its lipophilic and hydrophilic properties. On a molecular level, astaxanthin integrates into cellular membranes—including those of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) mitochondria—where it acts as a chain-breaking antioxidant, quenching reactive oxygen species (ROS) and scavenging free radicals efficiently (Giannaccare et al., 2020, pp. 1–5). Its mechanism involves the neutralization of lipid peroxidation, a process wherein polyunsaturated fatty acids in the cell membrane are damaged by ROS, ultimately leading to the formation of malondialdehyde (MDA) and other toxic aldehyde species that compromise membrane integrity (Giannaccare et al., 2020, pp. 3–5; Maiuolo et al., 2022, pp. 8–9). By inhibiting lipid peroxidation, astaxanthin stabilizes the mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm), an essential parameter for maintaining proper mitochondrial function and for preventing the initiation of apoptosis via intrinsic death pathways (Fong, 2023, pp. 7–10; D’Angelo et al., 2024, pp. 4–5).  

At the molecular level, studies indicate that astaxanthin modulates key intracellular signaling pathways. It downregulates inflammatory mediators by suppressing the NF-κB pathway, thereby reducing the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines (Giannaccare et al., 2020, pp. 3–5; Lixi et al., 2024, pp. 3–5). Additionally, astaxanthin has been shown to enhance the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (Giannaccare et al., 2020, pp. 1–3; D’Angelo et al., 2024, pp. 4–5), which together help restore cellular redox balance under chronic oxidative stress conditions. Its role in modulating mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt pathways further contributes to cellular survival by reinforcing anti-apoptotic signaling and inhibiting caspase activation, thereby reducing retinal ganglion cell death (Giannaccare et al., 2020, pp. 3–5).  

The hypothesis under consideration—that astaxanthin integrates into RPE mitochondrial membranes to quench lipid peroxidation and stabilize Δψm—is supported by its molecular configuration, which allows the compound to embed itself at the interface of membrane lipids without disrupting the bilayer structure (Rodella et al., 2023, pp. 11–13; D’Angelo et al., 2024, pp. 3–4). Once incorporated, astaxanthin acts as a “molecular rivet” that not only prevents oxidative damage but also helps maintain membrane fluidity and structural integrity. This is particularly relevant in the context of dry AMD, where chronic oxidative stress leads to the degeneration of the RPE, a critical cell layer whose dysfunction is central to the pathogenesis of the disease (D’Angelo et al., 2024, pp. 3–5). Additionally, the ability of astaxanthin to traverse cellular and mitochondrial membranes enhances its efficacy in neutralizing ROS, thereby preserving both mitochondrial function and cellular viability within the retina (Manresa et al., 2016, p. 5; Rodella et al., 2023, pp. 11–13).  

Expected Effect:  
In the assay proposed for the evaluation of dry AMD treatment, the primary expectation is that astaxanthin supplementation will lead to a marked reduction in oxidative stress markers and preservation of mitochondrial function in RPE cells. Specifically, the anticipated effects include a decrease in MDA formation, indicative of attenuated lipid peroxidation, and stabilization of Δψm, which is critical for preventing loss of mitochondrial integrity and the subsequent cascade of apoptotic signaling (Giannaccare et al., 2020, pp. 3–5). Based on its molecular actions, astaxanthin is expected to enhance cellular antioxidant defenses by upregulating enzymes such as SOD and glutathione peroxidase, ultimately leading to improved cell survival under conditions of chronic oxidative insult typical of dry AMD (Giannaccare et al., 2020, pp. 1–3; D’Angelo et al., 2024, pp. 4–5).  

RPE cells are known to express high levels of antioxidants and genes responsive to oxidative stress, such as those regulated by the nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway (Manresa et al., 2016, p. 5). In vitro assays, therefore, should reveal that astaxanthin treatment results in enhanced Nrf2 activation and subsequent transcription of phase II detoxifying enzymes, which in turn protects against oxidative damage. Furthermore, electrophysiological assessments, such as electroretinograms (ERG), are expected to show functional improvements in retinal activity, corroborating the cytoprotective effects observed at the cellular level (D’Angelo et al., 2024, pp. 4–5; Giannaccare et al., 2020, pp. 5–7).  

In addition to cellular antioxidants, astaxanthin’s anti-inflammatory properties are likely to mitigate the secretion of inflammatory cytokines from RPE cells and reduce the activation of microglial cells in the retina. Such a reduction in the inflammatory milieu is crucial, as chronic inflammation is known to exacerbate retinal degeneration in AMD by promoting pathological angiogenesis and further oxidative damage (Lixi et al., 2024, pp. 2–5). Hence, a combinatorial effect of decreased oxidative stress, improved mitochondrial stability, and reduced inflammation is anticipated to confer significant protection to retinal structures, ultimately delaying or preventing the progression of dry AMD (D’Angelo et al., 2024, pp. 4–5; Lixi et al., 2024, pp. 12–14).

Overall Evaluation:  
The collective body of evidence suggests that astaxanthin holds considerable promise as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD. One of its principal strengths lies in its multifaceted action: as a potent antioxidant, it effectively quenches reactive oxygen species and prevents lipid peroxidation, while its ability to integrate into cellular membranes, particularly those of RPE mitochondria, ensures that mitochondrial membrane potential is preserved even under chronic oxidative conditions (Fong, 2023, pp. 7–10; Rodella et al., 2023, pp. 11–13). The convergence of biochemical, cellular, and clinical data highlights its capacity to improve retinal function, as evidenced by improvements in electroretinogram outcomes and visual acuity measurements in clinical trials (D’Angelo et al., 2024, pp. 4–5; Giannaccare et al., 2020, pp. 5–7). Moreover, its favorable safety profile—as demonstrated through extensive use as a dietary supplement—provides an added advantage in terms of translational potential, facilitating the design and execution of clinical studies aimed at validating its efficacy in dry AMD (D’Angelo et al., 2024, pp. 13–14; Giannaccare et al., 2020, pp. 3–5).

Another strength is the well-documented anti-inflammatory property of astaxanthin, which complements its antioxidant activity. By downregulating NF-κB and associated pro-inflammatory mediators, this compound mitigates the inflammatory processes that contribute to RPE degeneration and retinal neuronal loss (Giannaccare et al., 2020, pp. 3–5; Lixi et al., 2024, pp. 3–5). This dual action is particularly significant given the multifactorial nature of dry AMD pathogenesis, where both oxidative stress and chronic inflammation play aggravating roles. The integration of these effects suggests that astaxanthin may not only slow disease progression but also restore aspects of retinal function, representing a comprehensive therapeutic approach.

However, several challenges and weaknesses also warrant consideration. Despite the promising mechanistic profile, the translational efficacy of astaxanthin in clinical settings remains to be fully established. Although numerous preclinical models have demonstrated cytoprotective effects in vitro and in animal models, the optimal dosing regimen and formulation for achieving sufficient bioavailability in the human retina are still under investigation (D’Angelo et al., 2024, pp. 4–5; Lixi et al., 2024, pp. 10–11). As a lipophilic compound, astaxanthin’s stability and absorption in the gastrointestinal tract can be variable, potentially limiting its effective concentration at the target site. Recent studies have suggested that innovative drug delivery systems, such as nano-based lipophilic carriers, may be necessary to enhance its bioavailability and achieve consistent therapeutic outcomes (Fong, 2023, pp. 7–10; Yang & Wang, 2022, pp. 4–5).

Furthermore, while its safety profile is favorable, long-term clinical trials are required to ensure that chronic administration does not lead to unforeseen adverse effects, particularly when used in high doses over extended periods. Variability in patient response based on genetic factors or baseline antioxidant status may also affect clinical outcomes, necessitating personalized approaches to dosing and supplementation (D’Angelo et al., 2024, pp. 13–14; Lixi et al., 2024, pp. 3–5). Additionally, while the molecular mechanisms such as the stabilization of Δψm and quenching of lipid peroxidation are well-documented in controlled experimental settings, the complexity of dry AMD—characterized by a combination of degenerative, inflammatory, and vascular processes—means that astaxanthin may need to be part of a multifactorial therapeutic strategy rather than a stand-alone treatment (Lixi et al., 2024, pp. 2–5).

In summary, astaxanthin stands out as a compelling therapeutic candidate for dry AMD on several fronts. Its remarkable antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, combined with its ability to stabilize mitochondrial membrane potential and prevent lipid peroxidation, directly address key pathogenic mechanisms implicated in dry AMD (Giannaccare et al., 2020, pp. 1–5; D’Angelo et al., 2024, pp. 4–5). The extensive preclinical and clinical data on its ocular applications, alongside its established safety profile as a dietary supplement, provide a solid foundation for further investigation. Nonetheless, the challenges associated with optimizing its bioavailability, determining the optimal dosing regimen, and confirming its long-term efficacy in diverse patient populations highlight areas where additional research is critically needed (Lixi et al., 2024, pp. 10–11; Giannaccare et al., 2020, pp. 3–5).

From the perspective of a drug development team leader focused on repurposing agents for dry AMD, the evidence supports the incorporation of astaxanthin into further preclinical testing and potential early-phase clinical trials. A multi-pronged approach that includes detailed pharmacokinetic studies, the exploration of enhanced delivery systems, and the evaluation of combination treatments with complementary agents might maximize its therapeutic potential. In conclusion, while certain hurdles remain to be overcome, the overall strengths of astaxanthin—its potent antioxidant activity, favorable safety profile, and mechanistic relevance to RPE mitochondrial stabilization—render it a highly promising candidate for inclusion in the therapeutic armamentarium against dry age-related macular degeneration (D’Angelo et al., 2024, pp. 3–5; Giannaccare et al., 2020, pp. 3–7; Fong, 2023, pp. 7–10).

To recapitulate, astaxanthin’s natural origin as a marine-derived carotenoid, its extensive historical use as a nutraceutical, and its documented molecular mechanisms that include inflammation suppression, ROS scavenging, and stabilization of vital mitochondrial functions all contribute to its strong candidacy for repurposing in dry AMD (Giannaccare et al., 2020, pp. 1–3; Yang & Wang, 2022, pp. 1–2; Maiuolo et al., 2022, pp. 8–9). The expected outcomes of integrating astaxanthin into RPE mitochondrial membranes—particularly the reduction of lipid peroxidation, decrease in MDA formation, and stabilization of Δψm—are directly aligned with the therapeutic needs of dry AMD patients. Despite the necessity for further research to optimize formulation and dosing, the body of evidence suggests that astaxanthin merits serious consideration as a component of future therapeutic regimens aimed at mitigating the multifactorial pathology of dry age-related macular degeneration (D’Angelo et al., 2024, pp. 3–5; Lixi et al., 2024, pp. 10–11).

Overall, the current literature provides a comprehensive rationale for advancing astaxanthin into a translational research pipeline focused on dry AMD. With its robust antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective actions, astaxanthin may fill a critical gap in the current therapeutic landscape, addressing both the symptomatic and pathogenic progression of the disease. The scientific hypothesis that it integrates into RPE mitochondrial membranes to quench lipid peroxidation and stabilize Δψm finds substantial support in the biochemical and clinical studies referenced above (Bakac et al., 2023, pp. 9–10; Lixi et al., 2024, pp. 12–14). While additional investigations are warranted to resolve issues relating to bioavailability and long-term efficacy, the convergence of preclinical, clinical, and biochemical evidence indicates that astaxanthin is a compelling and promising candidate for repurposing in the treatment of dry age-related macular degeneration (D’Angelo et al., 2024, pp. 4–5; Giannaccare et al., 2020, pp. 5–7).

In conclusion, the evaluation of astaxanthin as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD underscores its exceptional potential to mitigate oxidative stress-induced mitochondrial dysfunction in the RPE. Its natural origin, extensive therapeutic history in ocular and other diseases, detailed molecular mechanisms of action, and expected beneficial effects on mitochondrial stability collectively provide a strong scientific basis for further development. The challenges regarding bioavailability and optimal dosing are recognized; however, these are not insurmountable with current advances in pharmaceutical formulation and drug delivery technologies. Based on the extensive literature reviewed, the evidence overwhelmingly supports moving forward with additional targeted preclinical studies and early-phase clinical trials to validate astaxanthin’s efficacy and safety in the context of dry age-related macular degeneration (D’Angelo et al., 2024, pp. 3–5; Giannaccare et al., 2020, pp. 5–7; Fong, 2023, pp. 7–10; D’Angelo et al., 2024, pp. 3–4).
