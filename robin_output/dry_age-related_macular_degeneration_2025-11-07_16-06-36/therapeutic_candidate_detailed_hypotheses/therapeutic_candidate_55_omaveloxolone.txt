Proposal for Omaveloxolone (RTA 408; Reata Pharmaceuticals)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Omaveloxolone (RTA 408) is a semi-synthetic pentacyclic triterpenoid of the cyanoenone (oleanane-derived) chemical class rationally designed to activate the Keap1–Nrf2 pathway by electrophilic modification of redox-sensitive cysteine residues on Keap1, most prominently Cys151 (the key “sensor” cysteine for this chemotype) (Cuadrado et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2016). Within its class, cyanoenone triterpenoids are among the most potent small-molecule NRF2 activators known; they are sensed at KEAP1 C151 and trigger a coordinated induction of antioxidant and cytoprotective programs that normalize cellular metabolism, increase mitochondrial energy production, and reduce ROS (including mtROS), while also exerting anti-inflammatory actions that can include modulation of NF-κB signaling in cells and animal models (Cuadrado et al., 2019). Mechanistically, omaveloxolone forms a covalent adduct with KEAP1 that prevents CUL3–KEAP1–mediated ubiquitination and degradation of Nrf2, thereby allowing Nrf2 stabilization, nuclear translocation, and transcriptional activation via antioxidant response elements (AREs) (Liu et al., 2016; Cai et al., 2021; Bellezza, 2018). In human systems, omaveloxolone induces canonical Nrf2 target genes that collectively bolster mitochondrial and cytosolic antioxidant defenses, including NQO1, HO-1, SOD2, catalase, glutaredoxin-1 (Grx1), and thioredoxin-1 (Trx1) (Liu et al., 2016; Cheng et al., 2021).

Discovery and translational development of omaveloxolone followed extensive preclinical work with synthetic triterpenoids showing robust induction of the phase 2 response and protection against oxidant and inflammatory stress in vitro and in vivo, and early first-in-human experience with this class (e.g., bardoxolone methyl) helped establish on-target pharmacology while also highlighting class-specific safety considerations that informed subsequent designs (e.g., exclusion of patients with incipient heart failure risk in kidney indications) (Nakagami, 2016; Cuadrado et al., 2019). Pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic programs in nonhuman primates and humans show that oral omaveloxolone is bioavailable, exhibits dose-proportional increases in Cmax and AUC, and reaches concentrations capable of inducing Nrf2 target genes in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and multiple tissues; tissue distribution studies in cynomolgus monkeys demonstrated exposure slightly above contemporaneous plasma levels in liver, lung, and brain with measurable mRNA induction of canonical Nrf2 targets after repeat dosing (Reisman et al., 2019). In human clinical development, omaveloxolone advanced through a first-in-human oncology study, a dose-ranging Phase 2 program (MOXIe Part 1) in Friedreich ataxia (FA) with clear pharmacodynamic biomarker changes (e.g., ferritin and GGT) and dose/exposure–response relationships, and a registrational, placebo-controlled, 48-week trial (MOXIe Part 2) showing statistically significant improvement in neurological function by mFARS with an acceptable safety profile characterized principally by reversible transaminase elevations, headaches, nausea, and fatigue (Creelan et al., 2017; Lynch et al., 2019; Lynch et al., 2021).

This pharmacology is intrinsically aligned with a central pathophysiologic axis in dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD): cumulative oxidative stress, sustained mitochondrial dysfunction with increased mtROS, impaired mitophagy, and chronic innate/adaptive inflammation in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and neighboring tissues (Bellezza, 2018; Jarrett & Boulton, 2012). The RPE is enriched in mitochondria and relies on robust antioxidant systems to manage high ROS fluxes from phagocytosis and photo-oxidative challenges; Nrf2 is widely recognized as the “master” antioxidant transcription factor in RPE, transcriptionally controlling the expression of SODs (including mitochondrial SOD2), catalase, NQO1, HO-1, glutathione synthesis enzymes, and thiol-redox enzymes (Trx1/Grx1) (Bellezza, 2018; Lambros & Plafker, 2016; Camelo, 2025). Deficiency of Nrf2 or the mitochondrial SOD2 enzyme in mouse retina/RPE produces AMD-like phenotypes, while exogenous Nrf2 activation in ocular models protects RPE and retinal neurons from oxidative injury, reduces inflammation, and supports mitochondrial homeostasis (Lambros & Plafker, 2016; Jarrett & Boulton, 2012; Chen et al., 2023). Against this mechanistic backdrop, the specific demonstration that omaveloxolone protects primary human RPE from H₂O₂-induced injury via Nrf2—at nanomolar concentrations—by upregulating SOD2, NQO1, HO-1, catalase, Grx1 and Trx1, preserving GSH, lowering ROS, and reducing apoptosis is directly relevant to dry AMD biology (Liu et al., 2016).

Therapeutic History  
Class-wide experience. Cyanoenone triterpenoids have been deployed across multiple preclinical disease models characterized by oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inflammation, with consistent activation of NRF2 target genes and functional improvements in biochemical and physiologic endpoints. For example, omaveloxolone improved renal function and reduced neutrophil infiltration, apoptosis, ROS production, and oxidative injury in renal ischemia–reperfusion models in an Nrf2-dependent manner, and produced sustained neuroprotection in a rat status epilepticus model with concordant increases in tissue glutathione and ATP (Reisman et al., 2019). These effects map onto the known Nrf2 program (e.g., induction of Nqo1, Gsr, Gclc; increased glutathione) and translate to primates and humans as evidenced by PBMC/tissue gene induction and serum biomarkers (ferritin, GGT) (Reisman et al., 2019; Lynch et al., 2019).

First-in-human and oncology safety. In a first-in-human trial in advanced solid tumors, once-daily oral omaveloxolone (2.5–15 mg) was rapidly absorbed (Tmax 2–4 h), exhibited dose-proportional exposure, and had estimated terminal half-lives of ∼9 h on day 1 increasing to ∼20 h by day 28; across a mean exposure of 87 days, no dose-limiting toxicities were observed, adverse events were typically mild/moderate, and increases in select Nrf2 targets were measured in PBMCs (Creelan et al., 2017). The same report and related write-up noted no dose-limiting toxicities attributable to drug, a lack of QTc prolongation, and frequent low-grade laboratory changes (e.g., alkaline phosphatase elevations, anemia) in a small cohort, with PBMC Nrf2 target-gene mRNA serving as a primary pharmacodynamic target-engagement endpoint (Creelan et al., 2017).

Friedreich ataxia (FA) clinical development. Omaveloxolone has been evaluated extensively in FA, a neurodegenerative disease with depressed Nrf2 signaling and mitochondrial dysfunction. In MOXIe Part 1 (12-week, dose-ranging 2.5–300 mg/day), exposure was dose-proportional and PD changes in serum ferritin and GGT were maximal in the 80–160 mg/day range, congruent with improved neurological mFARS scores (placebo-corrected trends) and consistent with an exposure window for maximal biological activity; at 300 mg/day, a nonmonotonic dose–response with reduced Nrf2/mitochondrial effects was observed, suggesting a mid-dose “sweet spot” (Lynch et al., 2019). In MOXIe Part 2, patients treated with 150 mg/day for 48 weeks showed statistically significant improvement in mFARS relative to placebo; safety was acceptable with transient aminotransferase elevations unaccompanied by bilirubin increases, and common adverse events included headache, nausea, and fatigue (Lynch et al., 2021). PK data compiled from FA cohorts show mean terminal half-lives of ∼15–21 h at 80–160 mg/day (supporting once-daily dosing), Cmax in a range predicted by monkey PBMC EC50s to robustly induce Nrf2 targets, and interpatient variability of ∼31–43% for AUC/Cmax (Reisman et al., 2019). Translationally, FA patient biomarkers and platelet isotopologue metabolites indicated dose-dependent modulation of Nrf2-responsive targets (e.g., ferritin, GGT; decreased CK at 80–160 mg) and specific effects on fatty-acid–linked mitochondrial metabolism (reduced ¹³C-palmitate to HMG-CoA conversions with no effect on ¹³C-glucose) (Lynch et al., 2019).

Ophthalmic development context. Within ophthalmology, omaveloxolone has entered topical/ocular clinical testing (e.g., an ophthalmic suspension evaluated for prevention of corneal endothelial cell loss and ocular inflammation post-cataract surgery), and RTA 408 lotions/creams have shown Nrf2 target-gene induction in human skin punch biopsies, demonstrating tissue-level target engagement in humans via local administration (Cheng et al., 2021; Creelan et al., 2017). Although omaveloxolone has not been tested clinically in dry AMD to date, triterpenoid Nrf2 activators (including omaveloxolone) have reproducibly protected human RPE in vitro against oxidative insults (Liu et al., 2016), and related CDDO-derived analogues (e.g., RS9) have improved retinal antioxidant gene expression and function in multiple retinal models (Liu et al., 2016; Cheng et al., 2021).

Use in AMD or similar diseases. There are no published clinical studies of omaveloxolone in dry AMD; however, the drug exhibits precisely the mechanistic activities sought for RPE protection in AMD—Nrf2 activation and induction of SOD2/NQO1/HO-1, preservation of glutathione and antioxidant enzyme activities, and suppression of oxidative injury—in primary human RPE models (Liu et al., 2016). Supportive disease-proximal validation comes from a robust literature demonstrating that genetic loss of Nrf2 or SOD2 in retinal tissues produces AMD-like pathology, and that pharmacologic or genetic Nrf2 activation protects RPE and retina in models of oxidative stress, ischemia–reperfusion injury, and diabetic retinopathy (Lambros & Plafker, 2016; Jarrett & Boulton, 2012; Zhang et al., 2023).

Mechanism of Action  
Primary target: Keap1 Cys151 and Nrf2 stabilization. Omaveloxolone engages KEAP1 through covalent modification of Cys151, which is the electro-sensitive sensor residue controlling the high-affinity ETGE “hinge” and low-affinity DLG “latch” interactions that dock NRF2 to KEAP1 for CUL3-mediated ubiquitination under basal redox states (Cai et al., 2021; Reisman et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2016). Electrophilic adduction at KEAP1 cysteines inhibits the E3 ligase function, stabilizes Nrf2, promotes Ser40 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation, and increases binding of Nrf2–Maf heterodimers to AREs, resulting in transcriptional activation of a broad network of cytoprotective genes (estimated in the hundreds) that neutralize ROS, maintain glutathione and thioredoxin pools, and reprogram metabolism toward redox homeostasis (Das & Imanishi, 2022; Cai et al., 2021; Bellezza, 2018). In primates, quantitative PK/PD modeling relating plasma concentration to PBMC mRNA induction of canonical Nrf2 targets yielded EC50s on the order of tens of ng/mL, permitting translational projection of human exposures likely to achieve robust target engagement; observed FA patient Cmax values at 80–160–300 mg overlapped these EC50 ranges, and tissue distribution studies documented meaningful drug levels in liver, lung, and brain with dose-dependent mRNA induction in those tissues (Reisman et al., 2019).

Downstream antioxidant and mitochondrial programs in RPE. In primary human RPE, nanomolar omaveloxolone activated Nrf2 and increased expression and activity of multiple downstream antioxidant enzymes: HO-1 (heme catabolism; biliverdin/bilirubin production), NQO1 (two-electron reductase that supports NADPH-dependent detoxification), SOD2 (mitochondrial matrix superoxide dismutase), catalase (peroxisomal H₂O₂ scavenger), Grx1 and Trx1 (thiol-disulfide oxidoreductases for protein deglutathionylation/disulfide reduction) (Liu et al., 2016). Functionally, omaveloxolone preserved intracellular GSH under oxidative challenge, attenuated GSSG accumulation and protein glutathionylation, and restored enzyme activity of NQO1, Grx1, and Trx1 following H₂O₂ exposure (Liu et al., 2016). By lowering net ROS and preventing depletion/inactivation of thiol-redox systems, omaveloxolone reduced Bax activation and caspase-3 cleavage while increasing Bcl-2, leading to protection against apoptosis and necrosis in RPE; Nrf2 siRNA abrogated these effects, establishing on-target dependence (Liu et al., 2016).

Inflammation and NF-κB/NOS axes. Triterpenoid NRF2 activators can also influence inflammatory signaling. Omaveloxolone suppressed interferon-γ–mediated nitric oxide signaling in macrophages at nanomolar concentrations and reduced nitrotyrosine production in myeloid-derived suppressor cells and tumor xenografts, pointing to a mechanism that diminishes RNS/ROS-coupled inflammatory tone (Creelan et al., 2017). More broadly, Nrf2 activation antagonizes inflammation by mitigating ROS-dependent NLRP3 inflammasome activation and by competing with NF-κB for limiting transcriptional co-activators (e.g., CBP/p300), ultimately blunting IL-6 and IL-1β transcription; in the triterpenoid class, agents have been shown to modulate both NRF2 and NF-κB in cells and models (Camelo, 2025; Cuadrado et al., 2019). Although direct inhibition of NF-κB by omaveloxolone in human RPE has not been explicitly demonstrated in the provided sources, multiple reviews and trial descriptions describe omaveloxolone as reducing inflammatory mediators (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6) and blocking KEAP1-mediated NF-κB proinflammatory signaling, consistent with the hypothesized dual-action (Clay et al., 2019; Madsen et al., 2020).

Mitochondrial function and disease-relevant cells. In FA-relevant neuronal and fibroblast systems, omaveloxolone restored mitochondrial OXPHOS complex readouts, reduced mitochondrial ROS, prevented lipid peroxidation, increased cellular glutathione, and elevated Nrf2 transcript/protein with increased NQO1, HO-1, and GCL expression at low nanomolar concentrations, indicating a direct link from KEAP1–NRF2 engagement to improved mitochondrial redox and bioenergetic parameters (Tiberi et al., 2023). Platelet isotopologue analyses in FA patients showed reduced conversion of ¹³C-palmitate to HMG-CoA with increasing omaveloxolone dose and no effect on ¹³C-glucose metabolism, consistent with modulation of fatty-acid–linked mitochondrial metabolism by Nrf2 activation in human tissues (Lynch et al., 2019).

Pharmacokinetics, target engagement, and dose–response. Human oral PK shows rapid absorption (Tmax ≈ 1–3.5 h), dose-proportional exposure, and an apparent terminal half-life of ∼15–21 h at 80–160 mg supporting once-daily administration; in the oncology first-in-human trial Tmax was 2–4 h with terminal half-life ranging from ∼9–20 h between day 1 and day 28, consistent with accumulation to steady state across 28-day cycles (Reisman et al., 2019; Creelan et al., 2017). Monotonicity in Nrf2/mitochondrial biomarker response appears dose-limited at very high exposure; in FA, 300 mg/day fell into a concentration range where reduced Nrf2 induction and mitochondrial function were observed in vitro and clinical PD/functional signals were attenuated relative to 80–160 mg (Lynch et al., 2019). Translational PK/PD alignment from primate PBMC EC50s predicts robust induction of canonical Nrf2 targets at human Cmax achieved with ≥ 80 mg dosing, which is consistent with the observed FA biomarker changes and clinical signals in the mid-dose range (Reisman et al., 2019; Lynch et al., 2019).

Expected Effect  
Assay-level expectations in RPE oxidative stress models. The candidate’s cell-autonomous RPE mechanism predicts that, in primary human RPE or iPSC-RPE assays challenged with oxidants (e.g., H₂O₂, t-BHP, photo-oxidative blue light models), omaveloxolone pretreatment at low nanomolar concentrations will:  
- Increase Nrf2 nuclear translocation and upregulate canonical ARE-driven targets including SOD2, NQO1, HO-1, catalase, Grx1, and Trx1, as measured by mRNA, protein, and enzyme activity assays (Liu et al., 2016).  
- Preserve cellular thiol redox status by elevating intracellular GSH, reducing GSSG accumulation, and decreasing protein glutathionylation under oxidative challenge (Liu et al., 2016).  
- Reduce total and mitochondrial ROS burden (by virtue of increased SOD2 and augmented detoxifying capacity), attenuate lipid peroxidation, and maintain activity of antioxidant enzymes otherwise suppressed by oxidant exposure (NQO1, Grx1, Trx1) (Liu et al., 2016).  
- Decrease apoptosis/necrosis readouts (Annexin V/PI, caspase-3 cleavage), with shifts in Bax/Bcl-2 consistent with anti-apoptotic signaling downstream of restored redox homeostasis (Liu et al., 2016).

The RPE expresses all of the above antioxidant effectors (SOD2, NQO1, HO-1, catalase, Grx1, Trx1), and their induction by omaveloxolone has been demonstrated in primary human RPE, making these robust PD endpoints for in vitro validation and mechanistic confirmation of the compound in the exact cell type relevant to dry AMD (Liu et al., 2016). Given compelling preclinical evidence in FA models, we further expect improved mitochondrial bioenergetics (Δψm preservation/OXPHOS complex integrity) and reduced mtROS in RPE with omaveloxolone, although direct Δψm measurements have not been published in RPE for this compound; these endpoints are supported by neuronal/fibroblast data and are mechanistically consistent with Nrf2-driven SOD2/antioxidant induction (Tiberi et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2016).

Inflammation-modulating effects in RPE and retinal milieu. In RPE cultures or co-cultures exposed to pro-inflammatory cytokines or oxidative adducts (e.g., 4-HNE, MDA, CEP), omaveloxolone is expected to reduce ROS-augmented NF-κB–dependent cytokine transcription indirectly via rebalancing redox tone and directly via the triterpenoid class’s interference with NF-κB axes (competition for transcriptional cofactors and proposed KEAP1-linked NF-κB modulation), with measurable decreases in IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α transcription and nitrosative stress markers in settings where these are ROS-coupled (Camelo, 2025; Clay et al., 2019; Madsen et al., 2020; Creelan et al., 2017). In macrophage or microglia co-culture systems, decreased nitrotyrosine and nitric oxide synthase output would be anticipated, consistent with suppression of interferon-γ–driven NO signaling observed at low concentrations (Creelan et al., 2017). These effects target a recognized feed-forward loop in dry AMD in which oxidative damage and inflammatory signaling reciprocally amplify RPE injury (Bellezza, 2018; Camelo, 2025).

In vivo translational expectations and biomarker strategy. For systemic administration, PBMC Nrf2 target mRNA induction has been validated as a sensitive peripheral biomarker for omaveloxolone in primates and humans and should be incorporated as a noninvasive PD readout, with serum ferritin and GGT as additional systemic markers of Nrf2 activation shown to respond in the 80–160 mg range (Reisman et al., 2019; Lynch et al., 2019). Tissue-level target engagement in the eye could be assessed in early clinical studies by surrogate accessible tissues (e.g., skin punch biopsy Nrf2 target induction following topical administration as precedent), tears or conjunctival impression cytology for transcript biomarkers, and functional retinal endpoints; preclinical monkey data showing dose-dependent induction of Nrf2 targets in liver/lung/brain establish that tissue-level engagement is achievable, though ocular distribution has not been reported and remains a data gap (Reisman et al., 2019; Creelan et al., 2017). For local ocular delivery, existing clinical experience with RTA 408 ophthalmic preparations and demonstrated Nrf2 target-gene induction in human skin biopsies support feasibility of achieving tissue PD in the eye without systemic exposure (Cheng et al., 2021; Creelan et al., 2017).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
- Mechanistic precision for AMD biology. The omaveloxolone mechanism—KEAP1 Cys151 modification → Nrf2 stabilization and ARE-driven transcription—directly addresses core deficits in dry AMD: inadequate antioxidant/mitochondrial defenses and redox-driven inflammation in RPE. Upregulation of SOD2, NQO1, HO-1, catalase, and thiol-redox systems in primary human RPE cells has been demonstrated for omaveloxolone at nanomolar potency with rigorous Nrf2-dependence, and these are precisely the enzymes/activities required to reduce mtROS and protect the high-demand RPE from oxidative apoptosis/necrosis (Liu et al., 2016; Bellezza, 2018; Camelo, 2025).  
- Human translational pharmacology. Oral PK is favorable for chronic dosing (once-daily half-life ∼15–21 h at efficacious exposures), with dose-proportional exposure and well-established PD biomarkers (PBMC Nrf2 transcripts; serum ferritin/GGT) that map to mid-dose ranges yielding clinical signals in FA; primate PK/PD modeling provides concentration–effect relationships for Nrf2 targets that align with human Cmax at 80–160 mg (Reisman et al., 2019; Lynch et al., 2019).  
- Tissue distribution and target engagement in vivo. In cynomolgus monkeys, omaveloxolone distributed to major organs, with tissue drug levels at or above plasma and induction of canonical Nrf2 targets in liver and lung; dose-related trends were present in brain, suggesting CNS permeability and supporting the premise of tissue-level engagement beyond blood in primates (Reisman et al., 2019). Topical formulations (RTA 408 lotion/ophthalmic suspension) have induced Nrf2 targets in human skin and advanced into ocular clinical studies, de-risking local ocular delivery modalities for future AMD programs (Creelan et al., 2017; Cheng et al., 2021).  
- Clinical proof of mechanism and safety in a mitochondrial disease. In FA, omaveloxolone produced consistent PD biomarker activation (ferritin↑, GGT changes; CK↓ at 80–160 mg), functional improvement (mFARS benefit at 150–160 mg), and tolerability suitable for long-term administration (48 weeks), with reversible transaminase elevations as the principal laboratory signal; importantly, 300 mg exposures showed attenuated biologic responses, informing an exposure window for maximal effect (Lynch et al., 2019; Lynch et al., 2021).  
- Anti-inflammatory potential that intersects AMD pathophysiology. Nrf2 activation reduces ROS-dependent inflammasome activation and competes with NF-κB for co-activators; omaveloxolone and its class decrease nitrosative stress signaling and inflammatory mediators in immune cells and models, a mechanistic complement to antioxidant effects in RPE that is germane to dry AMD’s chronic para-inflammation (Camelo, 2025; Creelan et al., 2017; Clay et al., 2019; Cuadrado et al., 2019).

Limitations and risks  
- Absence of ocular pharmacokinetics and retinal tissue PD. No data currently demonstrate omaveloxolone exposure in retina/RPE after systemic dosing or local ophthalmic administration in humans; primate tissue distribution did not include ocular tissues in the reported studies, and brain induction was less robust than peripheral organs, raising questions about effective exposure in ocular compartments with systemic dosing (Reisman et al., 2019). While RTA 408 ophthalmic formulations have entered clinical testing, target engagement in human ocular tissues has not been published (Cheng et al., 2021).  
- Clinical evidence gap in AMD. There are no clinical data in dry AMD populations; pathophysiologic rationale is strong, but translation from FA and preclinical RPE models requires careful dose/exposure selection, PD verification in ocular tissues, and demonstration of structural/functional benefit (e.g., slowing GA growth), none of which are available to date in the provided corpus (Lynch et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2016).  
- Nonmonotonic dose–response at high exposure. FA data indicate that very high exposures (e.g., 300 mg/day) can reduce Nrf2 induction/mitochondrial effects, implying a therapeutic window that must be honored to avoid loss of efficacy or off-target effects; AMD trials will need to target mid-dose ranges (80–160 mg) or use local ocular delivery to minimize systemic exposure while maximizing ocular PD (Lynch et al., 2019).  
- Safety nuances in an elderly population. Although omaveloxolone has an acceptable safety profile in FA and oncology settings (no DLTs up to 15 mg in oncology; reversible ALT/AST elevations in FA), dry AMD patients are older with comorbidities; the triterpenoid class has historical cardiovascular risk signals (bardoxolone methyl in CKD), mandating conservative exclusion criteria and vigilant monitoring, particularly if systemic dosing is pursued (Creelan et al., 2017; Cuadrado et al., 2019).  
- Inflammatory pathway claims in RPE remain inferential. While Nrf2 activation is anti-inflammatory, and omaveloxolone suppresses NO/iNOS/nitrotyrosine signaling in myeloid cells, direct evidence of NF-κB pathway repression by omaveloxolone in human RPE is not established in the cited literature; anti-inflammatory benefits in retinal tissues should be verified in appropriate assays (Creelan et al., 2017; Clay et al., 2019; Madsen et al., 2020).

Programmatic recommendations  
- Preclinical ocular PK/PD. Define ocular exposure and tissue PD after systemic and topical dosing in relevant species. For systemic dosing, quantify omaveloxolone in choroid/RPE/retina and assay canonical Nrf2 targets (Nqo1, Hmox1, Srxn1, Txnrd1, Gsr) to confirm dose-dependent tissue engagement; for topical dosing, leverage the human skin precedent (punch biopsy Nrf2 induction) to design ocular surface/conjunctival PD sampling in early clinical studies (Reisman et al., 2019; Creelan et al., 2017).  
- RPE-centric PD biomarker panel. In vitro and ex vivo human RPE studies should be used to finalize a PD panel including SOD2, NQO1, HO-1, catalase, Grx1, Trx1 mRNA/protein/activity, GSH/GSSG ratios, protein glutathionylation, and apoptosis markers, all of which were responsive to omaveloxolone in primary human RPE (Liu et al., 2016).  
- Dose selection anchored to translational PK/PD. For systemic programs, target exposures corresponding to primate PBMC EC50s and FA mid-dose ranges (80–160 mg) that maximize Nrf2 target induction while avoiding the high-exposure attenuation seen at 300 mg; in early AMD studies, incorporate PBMC Nrf2 gene induction, serum ferritin/GGT, and potentially CK as systemic PD anchors (Reisman et al., 2019; Lynch et al., 2019).  
- Anti-inflammatory endpoints. In RPE and myeloid co-cultures, quantify NF-κB–dependent cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and nitrosative stress readouts (iNOS, nitrotyrosine) under oxidative/cytokine challenge with omaveloxolone to confirm expected anti-inflammatory effects; these are relevant given RNS/ROS-driven inflammation in AMD (Creelan et al., 2017; Camelo, 2025).  
- Clinical path. Consider a two-pronged strategy: (i) a topical ocular formulation to deliver drug to RPE/retina with minimal systemic exposure, justified by human skin PD activation and existing ocular formulation experience; (ii) a systemic oral approach with rigorous safety surveillance and mid-dose targeting, using PBMC/serum PD to confirm target engagement while ocular PD is established with surrogate sampling and imaging endpoints. The topical path may be particularly attractive to de-risk systemic safety in an elderly AMD population (Cheng et al., 2021; Creelan et al., 2017; Lynch et al., 2021).

Conclusion  
The totality of mechanistic, cellular, primate, and human clinical pharmacology evidence indicates that omaveloxolone’s core pharmacology—potent Nrf2 activation via KEAP1 Cys151 engagement, coupled with class-associated anti-inflammatory actions—maps directly onto the pathobiology of dry AMD. In primary human RPE, omaveloxolone at nanomolar concentrations induces mitochondrial and cytosolic antioxidant systems (SOD2, NQO1, HO-1, catalase, Grx1, Trx1), preserves GSH and redox enzyme activities, lowers ROS, and prevents apoptosis in an Nrf2-dependent manner, establishing precise on-target activity in the cell type central to AMD (Liu et al., 2016). Clinical development in FA demonstrates that systemic exposures can be achieved safely with pharmacodynamic target engagement (ferritin/GGT; PBMC markers) and functional benefit at mid-range doses (80–160 mg), while primate PK/PD work provides quantitative translational alignment for dose selection (Lynch et al., 2019; Lynch et al., 2021; Reisman et al., 2019). The principal gaps for an AMD program are ocular distribution/PD after systemic dosing, absence of clinical AMD data, and the need to validate anti-inflammatory effects in retinal contexts; each is addressable through staged preclinical and early clinical studies, with topical delivery representing an attractive route to maximize ocular exposure and minimize systemic liability (Reisman et al., 2019; Cheng et al., 2021; Creelan et al., 2017).

On balance, omaveloxolone is a strong mechanistic candidate for dry AMD: it targets the validated Nrf2 axis in RPE with demonstrated induction of SOD2/NQO1/HO-1 and thiol-redox defenses, supports mitochondrial antioxidant capacity, and carries human translational PK/PD tools to manage dose/exposure windows. Careful attention to dose selection (avoid very high exposures that attenuate desired biology), route of administration, and ocular PD verification will be critical to realizing its potential in AMD (Liu et al., 2016; Reisman et al., 2019; Lynch et al., 2019; Lynch et al., 2021).
