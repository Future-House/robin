Proposal for Melatonin (Sigma-Aldrich M5250)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Melatonin (N-acetyl-5-methoxytryptamine) is an endogenous indoleamine neurohormone first identified as a pineal product and now recognized to be synthesized in many extrapineal tissues, including the human eye (retina, retinal pigment epithelium [RPE], lens, ciliary body) where it is also delivered via the circulation and acts locally through cognate receptors (MT1, MT2) and the MT3 binding site (quinone reductase 2) (Lundmark et al., 2006; Lundmark et al., 2007; Ma et al., 2024). Ocular melatonin is under circadian control and is produced primarily by photoreceptors, with additional production reported in inner retina and potentially the RPE, consistent with a paracrine neuromodulatory role in retinal physiology; importantly, melatonin synthesis has been localized to mitochondria, linking its biosynthesis directly to the organelle most relevant to retinal oxidative metabolism (Blasiak et al., 2016; Ma et al., 2024; Rusciano & Russo, 2024). Biosynthesis proceeds from tryptophan via tryptophan hydroxylase and aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase to serotonin, followed by arylalkylamine N-acetyltransferase (AANAT) and hydroxyindole-O-methyltransferase (HIOMT) to generate melatonin, which freely diffuses to act in an autocrine/paracrine manner (Ma et al., 2024). As a chemical class, melatonin belongs to amphiphilic indoleamines; it partitions into both aqueous and lipid compartments, crosses biological barriers (including the blood–retinal barrier), and concentrates in mitochondria, properties that underpin its direct antioxidant, membrane-protective, and mitochondrial-modulatory actions described in multiple ocular contexts (Romeo et al., 2024; Rastmanesh, 2011; Ma et al., 2024).

Melatonin’s pharmacology encompasses receptor-dependent signaling via MT1 and MT2 G protein–coupled receptors (G_i and G_q/11 coupling), and receptor-independent actions as a direct free-radical scavenger with active redox metabolites (e.g., AFMK, AMK) capable of sequential radical quenching (“antioxidant cascade”), allowing a single parent molecule to neutralize multiple reactive species; MT3 corresponds to cytosolic quinone reductase 2 (QR2) and represents a distinct enzymatic interaction relevant to ocular physiology (D’Angelo et al., 2024; Lundmark et al., 2007; Rusciano & Russo, 2024; Rastmanesh, 2011). This dual pharmacology (receptor-mediated signaling plus direct ROS/RNS scavenging) provides a mechanistic basis for melatonin’s pleiotropic antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and mitochondrial-protective effects in the retina and RPE, actions widely reported in preclinical models of oxidative retinal injury, diabetic retinopathy, and nonexudative AMD (Crooke et al., 2017; Diéguez et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2024).

Therapeutic History  
Within ophthalmology, melatonin and melatonergic agents have been explored across inflammatory, neurodegenerative, and vascular retinal diseases. Topical melatonin (0.1%) reduced intraocular inflammation and blood–ocular barrier permeability in rabbit uveitis while increasing tear and aqueous antioxidant activity (supporting tolerability and local pharmacodynamic engagement), and multiple melatonin analogs lowered intraocular pressure in glaucoma, with nanomicelles extending hypotensive and neuroprotective effects (Rusciano & Russo, 2024). In diabetic retinopathy models, melatonin reduced retinal oxidative and nitrosative stress, decreased hypoxia-inducible factor-1α and VEGF signaling, protected the blood–retinal barrier, and improved neural survival, aligning with its redox, anti-inflammatory, and anti-angiogenic pharmacology (Crooke et al., 2017).

For macular degeneration, mechanistic and efficacy signals span in vitro RPE studies, in vivo retinal protection in mice, and human observational datasets. A protected in vivo AMD-relevant signal includes a subcutaneous melatonin pellet that preserved visual function and outer retinal structure and limited oxidative stress in a nonexudative AMD mouse model (Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021). Independently, rodent studies demonstrate intravitreal melatonin prevents acute photoreceptor loss (MNU model) by lowering oxidative damage markers (retinal MDA, 8-OHdG), increasing mitochondrial MnSOD and total SOD activities, and reducing photoreceptor apoptosis—evidence of both antioxidant and anti-apoptotic benefits from local delivery (Li et al., 2020). In RPE cell models (ARPE-19), melatonin consistently protected against H₂O₂-induced oxidative injury by lowering ROS and lipid peroxidation, restoring mitochondrial membrane potential, reducing Bax/cytochrome c/caspase-7 activation, and promoting autophagy (increased LC3-II and Beclin-1, reduced p62), with low-nanomolar protective effects blocked by the melatonin receptor antagonist luzindole, implicating receptor mediation at physiologic ranges (Chang et al., 2018; Blasiak et al., 2016).

Emerging ferroptosis-focused work further reports that melatonin rescues Aβ₁₋₄₀–induced RPE degeneration by curbing lipid peroxidation, restoring GPX4 and SLC7A11, and normalizing iron handling, with loss-of-function of MDM2 abolishing protection and positioning PI3K/AKT→MDM2→P53→SLC7A11 as a key axis; in vivo, melatonin preserved RPE structure and ERG responses in Aβ₁₋₄₀ models (Wu et al., 2025). These mechanistic and efficacy themes converge with a high-quality in vivo finding that melatonin “protects the retina from experimental nonexudative age-related macular degeneration” in mice (Diéguez et al., 2020).

Clinical observations are suggestive but not definitive. A large real-world EHR cohort analysis (TriNetX) associated chronic melatonin use with lower incidence of AMD and reduced progression from nonexudative to exudative AMD after propensity matching, while acknowledging potential residual confounding and calling for randomized trials (Jeong et al., 2024a). An editorialized report in JAMA Ophthalmology similarly noted protective associations and biological plausibility (RPE protection, VEGF reduction) while emphasizing exploratory design and the need for confirmatory prospective studies (Jeong et al., 2024b). Earlier case-series reported that 3 mg oral melatonin nightly for three months stabilized or improved macular changes in many AMD patients, but these uncontrolled data lack modern trial rigor (Lundmark et al., 2007; Martínez-Águila et al., 2021). Across aging and AMD, reduced nocturnal melatonin and urinary aMT6s have been documented, providing a pathophysiologic rationale for supplementation in older adults; however, oral melatonin exhibits variable and often low bioavailability, and aqueous/topical formulations must address photostability and posterior-segment delivery challenges (Čolak & Žorić, 2019; Blasiak et al., 2016; Jeong et al., 2024b; Rusciano & Russo, 2024).

Mechanism of Action  
Melatonin’s ocular actions relevant to dry AMD encompass receptor-mediated signaling, direct and indirect antioxidant effects, mitochondrial protection, modulation of apoptosis and autophagy/mitophagy, ferroptosis suppression, and immune/vascular regulation.

- Receptor-mediated signaling (MT1/MT2/QR2): MT1 and MT2 are expressed in retina and RPE; MT1 is present in photoreceptor inner segments and inner retinal neurons, MT2 shows high retinal expression and is reported in RPE, while the MT3 binding site corresponds to QR2, an oxidoreductase implicated in ocular physiology (Lundmark et al., 2006; Lundmark et al., 2007; Ma et al., 2024). In RPE, low-dose melatonin protection against oxidants is blocked by luzindole, indicating MT receptor involvement at physiologic concentrations (≤10⁻⁸ M), whereas higher concentrations (>10⁻⁶ M) exert receptor-independent antioxidant effects (Blasiak et al., 2016; Chang et al., 2018). MT1-dependent signaling maintains mitochondrial function under oxidative stress in RPE, underscoring receptor linkage to mitochondrial homeostasis in relevant models (Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021).

- Direct and cascade antioxidant activity: Melatonin directly scavenges ROS/RNS, including hydroxyl radicals, and via successive metabolism (3-hydroxymelatonin → AFMK → AMK) neutralizes multiple radicals (“antioxidant cascade”), affording broad-spectrum redox control across aqueous and lipid compartments due to its amphiphilicity (Rastmanesh, 2011; Čolak & Žorić, 2019). In retinal tissues it reduces lipid peroxidation (MDA) and oxidative DNA damage (8-OHdG), and its metabolites independently contribute to radical quenching in ocular systems (Li et al., 2020; Romeo et al., 2024).

- Indirect antioxidant signaling (Nrf2, Sirtuins, AKT): Melatonin modulates redox-sensitive transcriptional programs; it maintains or enhances Nrf2 nuclear activity in several ocular contexts (though Nrf2 responses can be model-dependent), leading to upregulation of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutases and glutathione peroxidases; it also engages AKT phosphorylation and related DNA damage responses (Blasiak et al., 2016; Crooke et al., 2017; Ma et al., 2024). Melatonin regulates sirtuins (SIRT1, SIRT3) which link metabolism to antioxidant gene expression, autophagy, and anti-inflammatory effects, with SIRT1/SIRT3 activation reducing apoptosis and VEGF and promoting autophagy gene programs; modulation is context-dependent but provides additional axes by which melatonin can protect RPE mitochondria and restrain inflammatory signaling (Crooke et al., 2017).

- Mitochondrial protection: Melatonin concentrates in mitochondria, stabilizes the electron transport chain, reduces electron leakage, maintains mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm), inhibits the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, and can activate uncoupling proteins; it reduces mtDNA damage and restores respiratory control, thereby curbing ROS generation at its source (Blasiak et al., 2016; Rusciano & Russo, 2024; Tong et al., 2022). In vivo retina, intravitreal melatonin increased mitochondrial MnSOD (SOD2) activity and total SOD, aligning with enhanced endogenous antioxidant defenses at the organellar level (Li et al., 2020). In RPE cell models, melatonin preserved Δψm (JC-1/TMRM assays) after H₂O₂ challenge (Chang et al., 2018).

- Anti-apoptotic effects: In ARPE-19 cells, melatonin reduced the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio, decreased cytochrome c release and caspase-7 activation, and lowered TUNEL positivity, consistent with inhibition of intrinsic (mitochondrial) apoptosis; this aligns with higher Bcl-2 mRNA and fewer apoptotic photoreceptors in vivo (Chang et al., 2018; Li et al., 2020). Mechanistically, mitochondrial stabilization, reduced ROS, and modulation of NF-κB/JAK-STAT signaling contribute to anti-apoptosis (Crooke et al., 2017; Ma et al., 2024).

- Autophagy/mitophagy regulation: Melatonin increases LC3-II and Beclin-1 and decreases p62 in RPE oxidative stress models, indicating enhanced autophagic flux; MT2-mediated inhibition of mTOR and SIRT1-linked transcriptional control further connect melatonin to autophagy and mitophagy, processes essential for RPE proteostasis and organellar quality control in aging (Chang et al., 2018; Crooke et al., 2017; Ma et al., 2024). Given that impaired mitophagy is a feature of early AMD, pharmacologic reinforcement of autophagy/mitophagy by melatonin is mechanistically aligned with disease biology (Crooke et al., 2017).

- Ferroptosis suppression: Aβ₁₋₄₀–induced ferroptosis in RPE is attenuated by melatonin via PI3K/AKT-driven phosphorylation and activation of MDM2, which inhibits p53; this derepresses SLC7A11, restores cystine import and GSH synthesis, and rescues GPX4 activity, thereby reducing lipid peroxidation, Fe²⁺ accumulation, and mitochondrial ultrastructural damage; MDM2 knockdown abrogates these effects, functionally linking melatonin to the PI3K/AKT/MDM2/p53/SLC7A11 axis (Wu et al., 2025). In vivo, melatonin preserved ERG a-/b-waves and outer retinal integrity after Aβ₁₋₄₀ insult (Wu et al., 2025).

- Anti-inflammatory and immune modulation: Melatonin limits NF-κB activation, reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6), and modulates immune cell phenotypes (e.g., promoting macrophage polarization toward tissue-repair M2), contributing to reduced retinal inflammation; it suppresses nitric oxide synthases and decreases NO, relevant to nitro-oxidative stress (Crooke et al., 2017; Diéguez et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2024). In models of choroidal neovascularization, melatonin attenuated angiogenesis via HIF-1α–VEGF inhibition and RhoA/ROCK-mediated macrophage/microglia polarization, suggesting an adjunctive anti-neovascular role even though dry AMD lacks CNV (Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021; Jeong et al., 2024b).

- Telomere/telomerase and DNA repair: Melatonin modulates telomerase and DNA repair pathways in retinal cells, with reports of stimulated telomerase activity and reduced telomere erosion in senescent contexts, and increased efficacy of oxidative DNA damage removal in RPE, potentially mitigating RPE senescence in AMD (Blasiak et al., 2016; Rastmanesh, 2011).

- Electrophysiologic and circadian effects: Melatonin modulates retinal electrophysiology and ERG amplitude in a dose- and time-dependent manner, interacting with PKC, MAPK/ERK, and cAMP/PKA signaling; while protective effects are seen in nocturnal rodent ERGs, a small human study reported reduced b-wave amplitude after a 10 mg oral dose, highlighting the need for careful dosing and chronotherapy alignment (Lundmark et al., 2006; Rusciano & Russo, 2024).

Collectively, these mechanisms plausibly converge in RPE to reduce mitochondrial ROS, maintain Δψm, prevent cytochrome c release, lower caspase activation, and support autophagy/mitophagy, aligning directly with the pathobiology of dry AMD driven by oxidative/mitochondrial damage and impaired proteostasis (Blasiak et al., 2016; Crooke et al., 2017; Ma et al., 2024).

Expected Effect  
The working hypothesis—melatonin scavenges ROS and upregulates mitochondrial antioxidant enzymes in RPE, preserving Δψm and preventing apoptosis in dry AMD—is consistent with reported assay-level outcomes in RPE and retina and yields clear, testable predictions.

- In RPE oxidative-stress assays (H₂O₂, cigarette-smoke constituents, Aβ₁₋₄₀), melatonin should reduce intracellular ROS (DCF/CellROX), lipid peroxidation (MDA, C11-BODIPY), and Fe²⁺ accumulation (FerroOrange), while increasing reduced glutathione (GSH), restoring mitochondrial membrane potential (JC-1/TMRM), and rescuing viability (MTT/CCK-8/LDH), effects substantiated in ARPE-19 and primary RPE studies (Chang et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2025).

- Mitochondrial antioxidant enzyme readouts are expected to increase: while direct ARPE-19 measures of SOD2/GPx protein can be added to this program, in vivo retinal data show melatonin increases mitochondrial MnSOD (SOD2) activity and total SOD, and multiple reviews report melatonin-induced upregulation of SOD and GPx in ocular tissues via Nrf2 and related signaling, supporting the enzyme-induction arm of the hypothesis (Crooke et al., 2017; Li et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2024).

- Intrinsic apoptosis markers should decline: reduced Bax/Bcl-2 ratio, decreased cytochrome c release, and reduced caspase-7/-3 activation, plus fewer TUNEL-positive nuclei, as already demonstrated in ARPE-19 under H₂O₂ and in photoreceptors in vivo (Chang et al., 2018; Li et al., 2020).

- Autophagy/mitophagy engagement should be evident: increased LC3-II and Beclin-1 and decreased p62, with flux confirmed by lysosomal inhibitors; mitophagy markers (e.g., PINK1/Parkin) should be assessed given melatonin’s reported influence on mitochondrial quality control (Chang et al., 2018; Ma et al., 2024; Tong et al., 2022).

- Ferroptosis control should be demonstrable in Aβ₁₋₄₀ and other ferroptotic stressors: upregulation of SLC7A11 and GPX4, normalization of TFR1/ACSL4/FTH1, reduced lipid peroxidation, and dependence on PI3K/AKT→MDM2→p53 signaling tested by pharmacological inhibitors and siRNA, as shown in recent preclinical reports (Wu et al., 2025).

- Anti-inflammatory and transcriptional endpoints should shift: decreased NF-κB nuclear translocation and pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β), increased Nrf2 nuclear retention and target gene expression, and potential SIRT1/SIRT3 upregulation in appropriate contexts (Crooke et al., 2017; Ma et al., 2024).

- Functional in vivo correlates should include preservation of ERG a-/b-wave amplitudes, photoreceptor/RPE morphology on OCT/H&E, and reduced oxidative tissue biomarkers (MDA, 8-OHdG) under retinal oxidative/AMD-like stressors, consistent with prior animal studies (Diéguez et al., 2020; Li et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2025).

Receptor expression to support target engagement is well documented in retina and RPE, and dependence on MT receptors can be tested with antagonists (luzindole) and MT1/MT2 knockdown, as low-dose protective effects in ARPE-19 are reversibly blocked by luzindole and MT1 knockdown abolishes mitochondrial protective actions under oxidative stress (Blasiak et al., 2016; Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021; Lundmark et al., 2007). Given circadian regulation, repeated dosing aligned with the retinal clock may be necessary to realize full mitochondrial DNA protection and durable antioxidant responses, as suggested by diurnal pretreatment requirements in ARPE-19 (Rastmanesh, 2011).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
- Strong mechanistic convergence on AMD biology: Melatonin’s combined direct scavenging of ROS/RNS, induction of endogenous antioxidant defenses (SODs, GPxs), mitochondrial stabilization (Δψm preservation, mtDNA protection), anti-apoptotic signaling (reduced Bax/cytochrome c/caspases), and autophagy/mitophagy support addresses core pathogenic axes in dry AMD—oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and defective proteostasis in post-mitotic RPE and photoreceptors (Blasiak et al., 2016; Ma et al., 2024; Tong et al., 2022).

- Demonstrated efficacy in relevant preclinical systems: Protection of RPE from peroxide and Aβ₁₋₄₀ ferroptotic stress in vitro, and preservation of retina/RPE structure and function in AMD-like mouse models, including nonexudative AMD and acute degeneration models, with robust biomarker changes (MDA, 8-OHdG, SOD2 activity) and ERG preservation (Chang et al., 2018; Diéguez et al., 2020; Li et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2025).

- Receptor and nonreceptor pharmacology: Dual mode of action enables efficacy at physiologic concentrations via MT1/MT2 and at higher local concentrations via receptor-independent radical scavenging, offering flexibility across dosing ranges and delivery routes; MT1-linked mitochondrial protection in RPE provides a direct mechanistic link between receptor activation and Δψm preservation (Blasiak et al., 2016; Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021).

- Anti-ferroptotic axis in RPE: PI3K/AKT→MDM2→p53→SLC7A11 mechanism positions melatonin within contemporary cell-death biology relevant to AMD (lipid peroxidation, iron handling, GPX4), offering mechanistic biomarkers and combination opportunities with iron chelators or GPX4-preserving agents (Wu et al., 2025).

- Favorable endogenous safety rationale and clinical signal: As an endogenous molecule with extensive human exposure for sleep disorders, melatonin has a comparatively favorable safety backdrop; observational EHR data provides a clinically relevant association signal for AMD incidence and progression reduction that justifies prospective trials (Jeong et al., 2024a; Jeong et al., 2024b).

- Translational feasibility with targeted delivery: Topical ocular and nanocarrier formulations can improve posterior-segment delivery, and intravitreal administration achieves retinal protection with measurable biochemical and functional benefits; nanotechnologies address melatonin’s aqueous instability and enhance tissue penetration (Ma et al., 2024; Rusciano & Russo, 2024; Li et al., 2020).

Weaknesses and Risks  
- Evidence base is predominantly preclinical; clinical efficacy in dry AMD is unproven: Apart from exploratory EHR associations and small uncontrolled case-series, there are no randomized controlled trials in dry AMD demonstrating visual function or structural endpoints with melatonin; reliance on animal models and ARPE-19 limits translatability (Bilbao-Malavé et al., 2021; Jeong et al., 2024a; Jeong et al., 2024b).

- Context- and dose-dependent effects: Nrf2 modulation by melatonin can vary by model, and very high exogenous concentrations can worsen photoreceptor light damage; a small human study showed acute ERG b-wave reduction after 10 mg oral melatonin, indicating potential physiological perturbation at certain doses/times and the importance of chronotherapy and dose finding (Blasiak et al., 2016; Tong et al., 2022; Rusciano & Russo, 2024).

- Vascular tone considerations: MT1-mediated vasoconstriction in retinal endothelium could theoretically exacerbate early perfusion deficits in AMD; dose, receptor selectivity, and local exposure will be critical to avoid adverse vascular modulation (Ma et al., 2024).

- Delivery and pharmacokinetics: Oral melatonin exhibits highly variable and often low bioavailability due to first-pass metabolism, and aqueous topical formulations face photostability and posterior-segment penetration barriers; while nanocarriers and intravitreal delivery overcome these, each introduces manufacturing and safety considerations (Jeong et al., 2024b; Ma et al., 2024; Rusciano & Russo, 2024).

- Aged tissue pharmacodynamics: Age-related declines in retinal melatonin and potential receptor desensitization could diminish responsiveness to exogenous melatonin in elderly AMD populations; some aging-associated retinal rhythms were not rescued by exogenous melatonin in prior studies (Blasiak et al., 2016).

- Safety window for intravitreal dosing: Although protective effects are demonstrated, at least one report cites retinal cell degeneration at high intravitreal doses, underscoring the need for careful dose-range finding and chronic safety assessment (Ma et al., 2024).

- Product-specific gaps: There is insufficient formulation- and impurity-specific information for Sigma-Aldrich M5250 in ocular contexts, and no product-specific ocular PK/PD has been reported in AMD; stability, excipient compatibility, and photostability data must be generated for any clinical candidate (Ma et al., 2024; Romeo et al., 2024; Rusciano & Russo, 2024).

Assessment against the team’s hypothesis  
Supportive evidence is strong that melatonin reduces ROS, preserves mitochondrial function, and prevents apoptosis in RPE/retina. Multiple ARPE-19 studies demonstrate decreased ROS, increased Δψm, and reduced intrinsic apoptosis markers (Bax, cytochrome c, caspases) under oxidative stress with melatonin, consistent with maintained mitochondrial integrity and suppressed apoptotic cascades (Chang et al., 2018). In vivo, raised mitochondrial MnSOD activity and reduced oxidative biomarkers after intravitreal melatonin substantiate mitochondrial antioxidant engagement in retina (Li et al., 2020). Upregulation of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, GPx) by melatonin in ocular tissues via Nrf2, SIRT1/3, and AKT pathways is reported in leading reviews and experimental models, even though direct SOD2/GPX induction has not been quantified in RPE in all cited primary studies; this constitutes a tractable validation gap for our planned assays (Crooke et al., 2017; Ma et al., 2024). Ferroptosis suppression via PI3K/AKT/MDM2/p53 further extends the mitochondrial-protective narrative to lipid ROS and iron metabolism, relevant to RPE stress in AMD (Wu et al., 2025).

Programmatic recommendations  
- Formulation and delivery: Given oral PK variability and ocular delivery constraints, prioritize posterior-segment–targeted strategies: (1) intravitreal melatonin for proof-of-mechanism in large animals (define safety window, ocular distribution, and retinal PD biomarkers); (2) develop stabilized topical nanocarriers (e.g., ethylcellulose, PLGA-PEG, nanomicelles) to improve posterior-segment delivery and residence time for chronic use (Ma et al., 2024; Rusciano & Russo, 2024; Jeong et al., 2024b).

- Pharmacodynamic biomarkers: In RPE/retina, quantify Δψm, mitochondrial respiration (OCR), ROS (DCF, MitoSOX), lipid peroxidation (C11-BODIPY, MDA), mtDNA integrity (long-amplicon qPCR), SOD2 and GPX4 protein/activity, Bax/Bcl-2, cytochrome c release, caspase-3/-7 activity, LC3-II/Beclin-1/p62 flux, Nrf2 nuclear localization and target genes, and ferroptosis markers (SLC7A11, GPX4, TFR1, ACSL4, FTH1) with pharmacologic/siRNA controls for MT receptors and PI3K/AKT/MDM2/p53 (Chang et al., 2018; Ma et al., 2024; Tong et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2025).

- Dosing and chronotherapy: Incorporate diurnal dosing regimens to align with retinal circadian biology; evaluate low-dose receptor-mediated windows versus higher-dose antioxidant windows, avoiding concentrations shown to potentiate phototoxicity (Blasiak et al., 2016; Rastmanesh, 2011; Tong et al., 2022).

- Safety and off-target risks: Monitor ERG under different dosing/time-of-day schedules, retinal/choroidal vascular caliber (given MT1-mediated vasoconstriction), photoreceptor light injury susceptibility under high-dose exposure, and long-term ocular histopathology in repeat-dose studies (Ma et al., 2024; Rusciano & Russo, 2024; Tong et al., 2022).

- Clinical translation: Design an adaptive, randomized, placebo-controlled Phase 2 in intermediate dry AMD with multimodal endpoints (SD-OCT GA growth, mesopic microperimetry, dark adaptation, low-luminance VA) and fluid biomarkers (plasma/urine aMT6s, systemic oxidative markers), stratified by baseline melatonin levels and sleep parameters; consider an intravitreal proof-of-concept in high-risk early GA to validate local PD and safety prior to chronic topical development (Jeong et al., 2024a; Jeong et al., 2024b; Rusciano & Russo, 2024).

Conclusion  
Melatonin is a mechanistically well-justified, low-cost, repurposable candidate for dry AMD: it directly and indirectly controls retinal/RPE redox status, stabilizes mitochondrial function (including Δψm), engages endogenous antioxidant enzymes, prevents intrinsic apoptosis, supports autophagy/mitophagy, and suppresses ferroptosis along a defined PI3K/AKT/MDM2/p53 axis, with concordant protection in RPE and retina across preclinical models (Chang et al., 2018; Li et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025). Epidemiologic data add a clinically relevant signal for reduced AMD risk and progression with melatonin exposure, and ocular delivery is feasible with modern formulations, though oral PK variability and aqueous instability pose development challenges (Jeong et al., 2024a; Jeong et al., 2024b; Rusciano & Russo, 2024). Key risks—dose-dependent phototoxicity, potential vasoconstrictive effects, variability of Nrf2 responses, and uncertain responsiveness in aged retinas—can be managed through judicious dosing, chronotherapy, receptor-aware design, and robust safety pharmacology (Blasiak et al., 2016; Ma et al., 2024; Rusciano & Russo, 2024).

Net assessment: advance melatonin into a formal translational program for dry AMD centered on posterior-segment–targeted delivery and rigorous mitochondrial/RPE pharmacodynamic validation, while preparing for a randomized clinical evaluation; the mechanistic breadth and preclinical efficacy merit this investment, contingent on resolving dosing, delivery, and safety windows in aged ocular tissue (Diéguez et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2024; Rusciano & Russo, 2024).
