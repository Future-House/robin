Proposal for L-Carnitine (Sigma-Aldrich C0283)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
L-Carnitine is a naturally occurring quaternary ammonium compound, chemically defined as β-hydroxy-γ-trimethylaminobutyrate, that is both derived from the diet (predominantly found in animal-based foods such as red meat) and biosynthesized endogenously from the essential amino acids lysine and methionine via a vitamin C–dependent pathway primarily in the liver and kidneys (Pescosolido, Imperatrice, & Karavitis, 2008a; Mollica et al., 2017). Historically, L-carnitine was first identified as a constituent of muscle tissue in the early 20th century and was later linked to its high content in meat, leading to its designation as “vitamin T” in certain insect models (Mollica et al., 2017). As a member of the carnitine family, it belongs to the class of metabolic cofactors or nutraceutical agents whose primary function is to shuttle activated long-chain fatty acids into the mitochondrial matrix, where they undergo β-oxidation, thereby facilitating aerobic energy production and maintaining the intracellular acyl-CoA/CoA balance (Moos et al., 2017; Pescosolido, Imperatrice, & Karavitis, 2008b). This class of compounds has historically been used as dietary supplements to treat primary and secondary carnitine deficiency, as well as in various metabolic and mitochondrial disorders (Flanagan et al., 2010). L-Carnitine is widely formulated in pharmaceutical products both as a standalone supplement and as an ingredient in combination formulations, which underscores its acceptance as a metabolic supportive agent with a favorable safety profile (Flanagan et al., 2010; Surai, 2015).

Therapeutic History:  
Clinically, the carnitine family has a long record of use in addressing conditions where fatty acid oxidation is impaired. L-Carnitine has been employed effectively as a life-saving therapy in primary carnitine deficiency—a condition in which mutations of the carnitine transporter (OCTN2) impair cellular uptake—and also in secondary deficiencies associated with conditions like renal failure, liver disease, and certain inborn errors of metabolism (Flanagan et al., 2010). Beyond classical deficiency syndromes, both L-carnitine and its acetylated derivative (acetyl-L-carnitine) have been investigated in a broad range of clinical contexts, including cardiovascular diseases, diabetic complications, and neurodegenerative disorders. For example, acetyl-L-carnitine has been shown to improve parameters of visual function in patients with age-related macular degeneration (AMD) and to confer neuroprotective effects in Alzheimer’s disease and mild cognitive impairment (Pescosolido, Imperatrice, & Karavitis, 2008a; Calabrese et al., 2009).  
In ophthalmology, while most clinical studies have focused on the use of carnitine derivatives for systemic metabolic or neuroprotective effects, there is preclinical evidence supporting an ocular role. Animal studies have identified high intraocular concentrations of carnitine in the lens and other ocular tissues, hinting at a physiological role in maintaining ocular metabolic balance and osmotic homeostasis (Pescosolido, Imperatrice, & Karavitis, 2008a; Pescosolido, Imperatrice, & Karavitis, 2008b). Investigations into the protective effects of L-carnitine against oxidative damage, including its potential to safeguard retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells and other ocular tissues, provide a rationale for its application in dry AMD, particularly in the context of geographic atrophy where oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction are central features (Pescosolido, Imperatrice, & Karavitis, 2008a; Pescosolido, Imperatrice, & Karavitis, 2008c). Moreover, combination therapy regimens incorporating acetyl-L-carnitine with n-3 fatty acids and coenzyme Q10 have also been trialed in experimental settings to address energy metabolism in retinal tissues (Shamsi et al., 2007). Despite these promising findings, direct clinical use of L-carnitine as monotherapy for dry AMD has not been extensively reported, thus making its repurposing an area that merits focused research to bridge the gap between its established metabolic functions and its potential application in retinal degeneration (Flanagan et al., 2010; Mollica et al., 2017).

Mechanism of Action:  
The central biochemical role of L-carnitine is to facilitate the transport of long-chain fatty acids into the mitochondria via the carnitine shuttle—a process that is critical for β-oxidation and ATP production. This shuttle is mediated by mitochondrial enzymes such as carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 (CPT-1) on the outer mitochondrial membrane, which converts acyl-CoA to acyl-carnitine, and carnitine acyltransferase (also known as carnitine acetyltransferase or CAT) within the mitochondria that re-forms acyl-CoA for oxidation (Moos et al., 2017; Jones et al., 2010). This process not only fuels ATP generation via the Krebs cycle and oxidative phosphorylation but also helps regulate the cellular acyl-CoA/CoA ratio, indirectly influencing multiple metabolic pathways (Surai, 2015).  
Furthermore, L-carnitine exhibits antioxidant properties either directly, through free radical scavenging, or indirectly by preserving mitochondrial integrity and function under oxidative stress conditions (Shamsi et al., 2007; Surai, 2015). It has been shown to reduce lipid peroxidation and prevent the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential—a key step in the cascade leading to cellular apoptosis (Pescosolido, Imperatrice, & Karavitis, 2008a; Pescosolido, Imperatrice, & Karavitis, 2008c). In addition to its classical mitochondrial functions, L-carnitine also acts as an osmolyte, protecting cells against hyperosmolarity by stabilizing proteins and cellular membranes, which could further reduce oxidative stress (Pescosolido, Imperatrice, & Karavitis, 2008b; Mollica et al., 2017).  
At the molecular level in the RPE, the hypothesis is that enhancing mitochondrial fatty acid uptake via L-carnitine supplementation will improve β-oxidation efficiency. This is expected to lead to increased ATP production and a reduction in the accumulation of lipid intermediates that may otherwise result in excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation. The reduction in ROS could mitigate chronic oxidative stress, a well established contributor to the pathophysiology of dry AMD (Jones et al., 2010; Pescosolido, Imperatrice, & Karavitis, 2008b). Notably, disrupted mitochondrial metabolism and energy deficits have been implicated in the progression of RPE cell dysfunction, and L-carnitine’s role in restoring metabolic flexibility has been documented in other cell types such as hepatocytes and neuronal cells (Ates et al., 2008; Surai, 2015). Additionally, the modulation of mitochondrial protein acetylation by L-carnitine derivatives, an effect observed in various models of aging and metabolic disease, may further contribute to a neuroprotective milieu in RPE cells (Wang et al., 2024; Virmani & Cirulli, 2022).

Expected Effect:  
Based on its multifaceted mechanisms of action, the anticipated effect of L-carnitine in an assay designed for dry age-related macular degeneration is twofold. First, by enhancing mitochondrial fatty acid uptake and increasing β-oxidation rates, L-carnitine is expected to boost ATP production in RPE cells, thereby ameliorating the energy deficits that contribute to cellular dysfunction in geographic atrophy (Pescosolido, Imperatrice, & Karavitis, 2008a; Jones et al., 2010). Second, this improved mitochondrial function should lead to a reduction in the generation of ROS that typically arises from lipid overload and subsequent incomplete oxidation, thereby decreasing oxidative damage to critical cellular structures including mitochondrial membranes and nuclear DNA (Shamsi et al., 2007; Pescosolido, Imperatrice, & Karavitis, 2008a).  
In in vitro assays using cultured RPE cells, one would expect that L-carnitine treatment leads to enhanced mitochondrial respiration, as measured by oxygen consumption rate (OCR), and an associated elevation in ATP levels. Moreover, reductions in established markers of oxidative stress, such as malondialdehyde (MDA) levels or increased glutathione (GSH) concentrations, would support its antioxidant action (Ates et al., 2008). Expression studies have shown that key mitochondrial enzymes and transporters, including CPT-1 and OCTN2 (the carnitine transporter), are expressed in RPE cells, providing a molecular substrate for L-carnitine’s action in these cells (Ribas et al., 2014; Jones et al., 2010). Furthermore, since mitochondrial dysfunction is a hallmark of dry AMD pathogenesis, the restoration of mitochondrial efficiency by L-carnitine could be measured by improved RPE cell survival and decreased apoptotic markers under conditions of oxidative stress and lipid oversupply.  
Preclinical studies in other tissues such as neuronal cells and hepatocytes have demonstrated that L-carnitine treatment not only restores mitochondrial function but also reduces lipid-induced ROS production and inflammation. Extrapolating from these findings, it is reasonable to expect that in the context of dry AMD, L-carnitine could counteract the metabolic inflexibility of RPE cells, ultimately slowing the progression of geographic atrophy and preserving retinal structure and function (Surai, 2015; Wang et al., 2024).

Overall Evaluation:  
L-Carnitine stands out as a promising candidate for repurposing in the treatment of dry age-related macular degeneration due to several compelling properties. Its established role in promoting mitochondrial β-oxidation and energy production, combined with demonstrated antioxidant and osmoprotective actions, provides a strong mechanistic rationale for addressing the metabolic and oxidative stress components of geographic atrophy in dry AMD (Pescosolido, Imperatrice, & Karavitis, 2008a; Jones et al., 2010). The compound’s extensive history of use as a dietary supplement and its well-characterized safety profile—in which high oral doses have been administered with minimal toxicity—support its potential for repurposing in an ocular application (Flanagan et al., 2010; Mollica et al., 2017).  
However, there are potential limitations that merit cautious consideration. Direct evidence of L-carnitine’s efficacy in RPE cells specifically within the context of dry AMD is currently limited. While preclinical studies in non-ocular tissues and some ocular models suggest beneficial mitochondrial and antioxidant effects, the precise dosing, ocular bioavailability, and long-term impact on retinal degeneration remain to be rigorously established. Moreover, while L-carnitine can enhance mitochondrial function, the multifactorial nature of dry AMD implies that a monotherapy approach might not be sufficient; a combination therapy strategy—potentially incorporating other mitochondrial or antioxidant agents—may ultimately prove more effective (Shamsi et al., 2007; Pescosolido, Imperatrice, & Karavitis, 2008a).  
In summary, the strengths of L-carnitine include its well-established role in cellular energy metabolism, its safety profile and widespread clinical use in other metabolic conditions, and its potential to mitigate ROS production and oxidative damage through enhanced fatty acid oxidation. The weaknesses primarily lie in the current paucity of direct evidence in ocular models of dry AMD and the challenges associated with ensuring adequate drug delivery to the RPE in vivo. Nonetheless, given the overall mechanistic promise and the favorable tolerability observed in numerous studies, L-carnitine warrants further investigation – with a focus on in vitro RPE assays under condition of lipid overload and oxidative stress, followed by in vivo preclinical models of geographic atrophy – to validate its efficacy as a repurposed therapy for dry AMD (Jones et al., 2010; Pescosolido, Imperatrice, & Karavitis, 2008c; Ates et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2024).  
Overall, I recommend proceeding with targeted experiments to assess mitochondrial respiration, ROS levels, and cell survival in RPE cultures treated with L-carnitine. These studies should be complemented with investigations of tissue distribution following ocular delivery to address bioavailability concerns. This comprehensive evaluation supports the potential of L-carnitine as a metabolic and antioxidant therapeutic candidate for dry AMD, while also highlighting areas where additional data are needed before clinical translation.
