Proposal for Mitoquinone mesylate (MitoQ; Cayman 10012005)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Mitoquinone mesylate (MitoQ) is a synthetic, mitochondria‐targeted antioxidant originally developed as an analogue of coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) to overcome the bioavailability and uptake limitations of naturally occurring CoQ10. Its chemical design is based on modifying decylubiquinone—a CoQ10 analogue in which the long isoprenoid tail is replaced by a saturated decyl hydrocarbon chain to enhance mitochondrial retention—and covalently linking it to a lipophilic triphenylphosphonium (TPP⁺) cation. The inclusion of the TPP⁺ moiety exploits the highly negative membrane potential of the mitochondrial inner membrane, thereby ensuring selective accumulation of MitoQ in the mitochondrial matrix (Cores et al., 2023). This compound belongs to the class of lipophilic cationic antioxidants and is also considered a mitochondria-targeted rechargeable antioxidant (RMA) because, after its active ubiquinone moiety scavenges reactive oxygen species (ROS), it is enzymatically reduced to the active hydroquinone form by components of the electron transport chain. The rechargeable property allows MitoQ to be continuously recycled via the respiratory chain, resulting in sustained antioxidant action even at low doses—a property that distinguishes it from conventional antioxidants (Lukashev et al., 2014; Fields et al., 2023). The synthesis strategy for MitoQ involves the chemical conjugation of decylubiquinone with TPP⁺, and it is formulated as a mesylate salt to improve its stability and facilitate pharmaceutical development (Cores et al., 2023; Kim & Han, 2017). Overall, MitoQ is a well‐defined member of a broader class of mitochondria‐targeted redox active compounds that have been widely investigated for their potential to modulate mitochondrial oxidative stress (Zielonka et al., 2017).

Therapeutic History:  
Over the past two decades, MitoQ has been extensively characterized in a wide range of preclinical studies spanning neurodegenerative diseases, cardiovascular disorders, metabolic syndrome, sepsis, and other conditions associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and elevated oxidative stress. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that MitoQ confers protection against oxidative injury in cellular models and animal models alike. For example, studies in rodent models of cardiac ischemia–reperfusion injury, hypertension, and pressure‐overload heart failure have shown that MitoQ preserves mitochondrial respiration, decreases ROS production (especially H₂O₂ and superoxide) and reduces lipid peroxidation in the heart (Fairley et al., 2023; Kim & Han, 2017). In addition, significant neuroprotective effects have been reported in multiple models of neurodegeneration—ranging from in vitro experiments using Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s disease models to in vivo studies in rodent systems—where MitoQ was able to reduce mitochondrial fragmentation, lower markers of apoptosis, and restore mitochondrial membrane potential in neurons (Fields et al., 2023; Suárez-Rivero et al., 2021). Clinically, MitoQ has advanced into human trials in several indications including studies related to Parkinson’s disease, multiple sclerosis, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and hypertension, with reported safety in over 200 human subjects (Suárez-Rivero et al., 2021; Fairley et al., 2023). Although there is no direct record of its use in dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD), the extensive preclinical history in diseases characterized by oxidative stress and mitochondrial impairment provides a strong precedent for its repurposing. In ocular contexts, early pharmacokinetic studies have demonstrated that MitoQ exhibits measurable retinal uptake (approximately 1% of plasma concentration) after oral or topical administration, suggesting that the compound can reach the retina at therapeutically relevant levels (Apostolova & Victor, 2015; Shinn & Lagalwar, 2021). Given that mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative injury in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells are now increasingly recognized as central to the pathogenesis of dry AMD, the repurposing of MitoQ for this indication is logically supported by its mechanism and prior usage in analogous conditions (Cores et al., 2023; Zielonka et al., 2017).

Mechanism of Action:  
MitoQ’s mechanism of action is rooted in its unique chemical architecture that allows selective mitochondrial targeting and continuous ROS scavenging. The TPP⁺ moiety drives the compound’s accumulation into the mitochondrial matrix in a membrane potential–dependent manner. Once inside the mitochondria, MitoQ’s ubiquinone moiety is rapidly reduced by components of the electron transport chain—most notably by electron flow through complex II—and converted to its active form, mitoquinol (ubiquinol) (Cores et al., 2023; Manolis et al., 2021). In its reduced state, mitoquinol acts as a potent antioxidant that intercepts ROS generated primarily at the sites of electron leakage at complexes I and III. The scavenging of ROS, particularly superoxide and peroxyl radicals, prevents oxidative damage to mitochondrial lipids, proteins, and DNA—all of which are crucial for maintaining mitochondrial function (Zielonka et al., 2017; Modi et al., 2024). In effect, the cycle of reduction and oxidation (redox cycling) enables MitoQ to function catalytically, rather than stoichiometrically, thereby amplifying its antioxidant capacity over time (Lukashev et al., 2014; Modi et al., 2024). Moreover, by reducing mitochondrial ROS, MitoQ preserves the mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm), which is essential for ATP synthesis and overall cellular bioenergetics (Kim & Han, 2017; Gueven et al., 2017). This stabilization of Δψm not only inhibits the initiation of apoptosis but may also dampen downstream inflammatory signaling cascades mediated by ROS-sensitive transcription factors such as NF-κB (Fields et al., 2023; Cores et al., 2023). Such detailed molecular interactions—spanning TPP-driven uptake, rapid reduction at the respiratory chain, catalytic ROS neutralization, and subsequent recycling to the active antioxidant form—underscore the multifaceted protection that MitoQ offers against mitochondrial oxidative injury.

Expected Effect:  
In the context of dry AMD, the underlying hypothesis is that accumulation of oxidative damage in RPE cells leads to mitochondrial dysfunction, loss of Δψm, increased ROS production, chronic inflammation, and ultimately RPE cell degeneration, which is central to disease progression. Based on in vitro studies performed in ARPE-19 cells, treatment with MitoQ has been shown to reduce mitochondrial ROS levels, as measured by MitoSOX fluorescence, and to preserve Δψm as assessed by TMRM assays when cells are challenged with H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress (Cores et al., 2023; Fields et al., 2023). The expected effect of MitoQ in a dry AMD model is that, upon topical or oral administration, it will accumulate in the mitochondria of RPE cells via TPP-mediated uptake. Once there, its ubiquinone moiety will be reduced to the active antioxidant form by the local respiratory chain, which in turn will neutralize superoxide and other ROS at the sites of electron leakage (primarily complex I and III regions). This ROS scavenging is anticipated to limit oxidative damage to mitochondrial membranes and proteins, maintain efficient electron transport and ATP production, and ultimately restore the mitochondrial membrane potential that is compromised in oxidative stress conditions (Zielonka et al., 2017; Lukashev et al., 2014). Additionally, by lowering oxidative stress, MitoQ is expected to reduce inflammatory signaling within the RPE and adjacent retinal layers—potentially decreasing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and mitigating the chronic low-grade inflammation that contributes to dry AMD pathology (Fairley et al., 2023; Gueven et al., 2017). In an in vitro assay using ARPE-19 cells, one would expect to observe a significant decrease in MitoSOX signal intensity along with increased TMRM fluorescence after treatment with MitoQ, indicating reduced mitochondrial ROS and improved Δψm, respectively (Cores et al., 2023; Fields et al., 2023). Given that ARPE-19 is a well‐accepted model for studying RPE function and oxidative stress, these biochemical endpoints provide compelling mechanistic evidence that MitoQ may slow or even reverse aspects of RPE degeneration—a promising therapeutic outcome for dry AMD (Apostolova & Victor, 2015; Shinn & Lagalwar, 2021).

Overall Evaluation:  
MitoQ stands out as a promising therapeutic candidate for dry age-related macular degeneration on the basis of several compelling factors. First, its synthetic origin as a mitochondria-targeted CoQ10 analogue with an appended TPP⁺ group ensures that it concentrates specifically within mitochondria, where oxidative stress is most detrimental (Cores et al., 2023; Zielonka et al., 2017). This targeted delivery is a critical improvement over non-specific antioxidants, which often fail to reach the intracellular compartments where ROS generation is most intense. Second, MitoQ’s well-established mechanism of action—encompassing catalytic ROS scavenging through a continuously rechargeable redox cycle—directly addresses the pathophysiological core of dry AMD, namely the oxidative injury and mitochondrial dysfunction in RPE cells (Lukashev et al., 2014; Fields et al., 2023). Preclinical studies in diverse disease models have robustly demonstrated its capacity to protect mitochondrial structure and function, reduce lipid peroxidation and apoptotic signaling, and ultimately preserve cellular viability (Kim & Han, 2017; Gueven et al., 2017).

On the therapeutic history side, MitoQ has undergone extensive preclinical evaluation in models of neurodegeneration, cardiovascular diseases, and metabolic disorders, and has been tested in several early-phase clinical trials—even if not yet in dry AMD specifically—showing an excellent safety profile in over 200 human subjects (Suárez-Rivero et al., 2021; Fairley et al., 2023). Furthermore, pharmacokinetic studies demonstrating ocular uptake (~1% of plasma levels) provide an encouraging basis for its repurposing in retinal diseases, although the relatively low retinal concentration does suggest that formulation optimization (for instance, via topical delivery systems or enhanced ocular bioavailability formulations) may be required to achieve therapeutic levels in the RPE (Apostolova & Victor, 2015; Shinn & Lagalwar, 2021).

Despite these strengths, several weaknesses need to be considered. Clinical trials in other indications such as Parkinson’s disease have reported mixed outcomes with MitoQ, leading to concerns about its translation from well-controlled preclinical settings to the complex environment of human disease (Suárez-Rivero et al., 2021). Moreover, the potential for pro-oxidant effects at high concentrations—as sometimes observed with redox-active compounds like MitoQ—is a dose optimization challenge that must be carefully managed in any clinical development program (Gueven et al., 2017; Lukashev et al., 2014). The fact that the reported retinal uptake of MitoQ is modest (~1% of plasma levels) may necessitate higher systemic doses or alternative delivery routes to ensure that sufficient drug reaches the RPE, which in turn could raise safety issues if off-target effects occur (Apostolova & Victor, 2015). Additionally, while the biochemical rationale is compelling, there is currently no direct clinical evidence for efficacy in dry AMD; this gap underscores the need for further targeted preclinical investigations using dry AMD-relevant animal models and subsequent early-phase clinical trials.

In summary, the overall evaluation favours exploration of MitoQ as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD. Its strong mechanistic foundation—based on mitochondrial targeting, catalytic ROS scavenging, and preservation of Δψm—aligns well with the known etiopathogenesis of dry AMD, which involves RPE mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress. The extensive preclinical and early clinical data supporting MitoQ’s safety and efficacy in other indications further bolster its candidacy. However, attention must be given to optimizing dosing regimens and delivery strategies to overcome the limitation of modest retinal uptake, as well as rigorously establishing its efficacy specifically in models of dry AMD before advancing to large-scale clinical trials. Given its unique properties and the urgent need for effective treatments in dry AMD, MitoQ merits further investigation as part of a comprehensive strategy to combat RPE degeneration and preserve visual function in affected patients (Cores et al., 2023; Zielonka et al., 2017; Lukashev et al., 2014; Apostolova & Victor, 2015; Shinn & Lagalwar, 2021).
