Proposal for SkQ1 (10-(6′-plastoquinonyl)-decyltriphenylphosphonium; Sigma SML0967)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
SkQ1 (10‐(6′‐plastoquinonyl)‐decyltriphenylphosphonium) is a fully synthetic, mitochondria‐targeted antioxidant constructed by conjugating a plastoquinone antioxidant moiety with a decyl linker that is covalently bound to a triphenylphosphonium (TPP⁺) cation. The plastoquinone scaffold is derived from the natural quinone found in chloroplasts, known for its potent reactive oxygen species (ROS)–removing capabilities, while the TPP⁺ moiety acts as a highly lipophilic vector that drives selective accumulation within mitochondria due to the inner mitochondrial membrane’s large negative potential. In essence, by combining these two structural elements, SkQ1 is designed to exploit electrophoretic uptake to achieve extremely high concentrations in the mitochondrial matrix where oxidative damage is a critical pathogenic factor. This compound belongs to the broader class of mitochondria‐targeted quinone antioxidants, often referred to collectively as “SkQ” compounds. Molecules in this class were originally proposed and developed by Skulachev and colleagues, and they have been used experimentally to achieve mitochondrial protection in contexts ranging from ischemia–reperfusion injury and neurodegeneration to aging‐associated dysfunctions (Apostolova & Victor, 2015; Olovnikov et al., n.d.; Suárez-Rivero et al., 2021). The rational design strategy behind SkQ1—linking a redox‐active quinone to a lipophilic cation—has also inspired the development of analogous compounds such as SkQR1, MitoQ, and related derivatives that are being explored for a variety of biomedical applications.

Therapeutic History:  
The therapeutic history of SkQ1, as well as that of its parent class of mitochondria‐targeted antioxidants, is extensive and spans multiple preclinical and early clinical studies. In numerous in vitro and in vivo models, SkQ1 has demonstrated robust antioxidant effects. For example, it has been shown to protect mitochondria and cellular membranes against lipid peroxidation—key pathogenic processes underlying aging and degenerative diseases—at nanomolar concentrations. In various rodent models, including studies in OXYS rats (a strain that mimics accelerated senescence), chronic administration of SkQ1 prevented or reversed age‐related abnormalities such as cataract, retinopathy, and even systemic markers of mitochondrial dysfunction (Wanagat et al., 2010; Belenichev et al., 2024; Olovnikov et al., n.d.). Moreover, veterinary and preclinical studies have documented SkQ1’s ability to increase lifespan and delay the onset of age‐linked phenotypes, including osteoporosis, immune decline, and neurodegeneration (Apostolova & Victor, 2015; Weissig, 2020). Within the ocular field, SkQ1 has been specifically formulated as eye drops—marketed in certain regions under the trade name Visomitin—to treat dry eye syndrome. These formulations have passed early phase safety trials and have now seen clinical use in Russia. Although SkQ1 has not yet been directly trialed in dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) or geographic atrophy, its documented efficacy in protecting retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) in animal models and its ability to improve electrophysiological outcomes (such as preserved electroretinography [ERG] responses) support its potential repurposing for AMD (Gueven et al., 2017; Olovnikov et al., n.d.; Suárez-Rivero et al., 2021). In several ocular studies in both rodent and primate models, topical administration of SkQ1 has demonstrated the capacity to deliver the drug to the posterior segment with minimal systemic exposure, reducing characteristics like RPE vacuolization and preserving overall retinal function (Weissig, 2020; Shinn & Lagalwar, 2021). The extensive preclinical and limited clinical history of SkQ1 in ocular applications—most notably in dry-eye syndrome and animal models of retinal degeneration—suggests that the therapeutic candidate has a solid track record of mitigating mitochondrial dysfunction in tissues exposed to chronic oxidative stress, a pathophysiological component also seen in dry AMD (Suárez-Rivero et al., 2021; Wanagat et al., 2010).

Mechanism of Action:  
SkQ1 exerts its biological effects via multiple interrelated molecular mechanisms that center on its ability to accumulate rapidly within mitochondria and scavenge deleterious ROS. The TPP⁺ portion of the molecule, owing to its delocalized positive charge and high lipophilicity, drives the electrophoretic uptake of SkQ1 into the mitochondrial matrix along the steep negative membrane potential (Δψm) that is inherent to healthy mitochondria (Apostolova & Victor, 2015; Olovnikov et al., n.d.). Once localized within these organelles, the plastoquinone group is poised to participate in redox cycling: it is reversibly reduced—often by components of the electron transport chain such as complex III—and subsequently reacts with ROS such as superoxide (O₂•⁻) and lipid peroxyl radicals to convert them into less reactive species (Lukashev et al., 2014; Weissig, 2020). Importantly, the antioxidant effect of SkQ1 is not a one-off reaction; rather, it is “rechargeable,” meaning that following its oxidation by ROS, the quinone moiety can be reduced again, thereby enabling repeated cycles of detoxification. This rechargeable mechanism differentiates SkQ1 from conventional antioxidants that are consumed irreversibly during redox reactions. Additionally, SkQ1 has been shown to form complexes with cardiolipin—a key phospholipid in the inner mitochondrial membrane—and thereby stabilize the membrane structure against peroxidation. Some studies also suggest that the TPP⁺ component might mediate a mild uncoupling effect by facilitating proton translocation (often in conjunction with interacting with fatty acid anions), which can reduce mitochondrial ROS generation by dissipating excessive inner membrane potential under conditions of oxidative overload (Lukashev et al., 2014; Weissig, 2020; Suárez-Rivero et al., 2021). This dual mechanism of direct ROS scavenging combined with membrane stabilizing and modest uncoupling effects contributes to the preservation of Δψm and the overall integrity of mitochondrial bioenergetics. In RPE cells stressed by chronic photo-oxidative insult, these properties would translate into effective mitigation of oxidative damage, thereby preventing the collapse of mitochondrial membrane potential—a key event that precipitates cell dysfunction and death in dry AMD (Muraleva et al., 2019; Olovnikov et al., n.d.).

Expected Effect:  
The proposed hypothesis for repurposing SkQ1 in dry age-related macular degeneration is predicated on its capacity to target RPE mitochondria and to neutralize both superoxide radicals and lipid peroxyl radicals that accumulate under conditions of chronic light-induced oxidative stress. In vitro assays using ARPE-19 cells have demonstrated that nanomolar concentrations of SkQ1 elicit potent antioxidant responses—as measured by decreases in fluorescence with DCFDA (a marker of ROS) and stabilization of mitochondrial membrane potential via JC-1 assays—indicating that the compound readily scavenges ocular ROS at very low doses (Gueven et al., 2017; Olovnikov et al., n.d.). This antioxidant activity is expected to prevent or delay the loss of Δψm, a critical parameter for cell survival and function, particularly in RPE cells where mitochondrial dysfunction is implicated in the pathogenesis of drusen formation and geographic atrophy in dry AMD. In vivo, studies conducted in models such as OXYS rats have shown that chronic administration of SkQ1 not only reduces oxidative markers in mitochondrial membranes (e.g., decreasing levels of malondialdehyde and hydroxynonenal-adducts) but also normalizes cardiolipin content and fatty acid composition—key factors involved in the proper assembly and turnover of respiratory complexes (Wanagat et al., 2010; Olovnikov et al., n.d.). Moreover, macaque studies have indicated that topical SkQ1 eye drops achieve significant drug levels in the posterior segment although the dosing remains in the nanomolar range, leading to improvements in ERG function and reduced RPE vacuolization without evidence of systemic toxicity (Olovnikov et al., n.d.; Weissig, 2020). At the molecular level, the expected effect in RPE cells would be a sustained antioxidant environment that preserves mitochondrial function through two primary pathways: first, by directly quenching damaging ROS that would otherwise trigger mitochondrial DNA damage and lipid peroxidation; and second, by maintaining the structural integrity of mitochondrial membranes via stabilization of cardiolipin and prevention of excessive proton leakage. Given that RPE cells express the full complement of mitochondrial electron transport chain proteins, including complex III which is capable of reducing SkQ1 to its active form, these cells are ideal targets for the drug’s mechanism (Muraleva et al., 2019; Olovnikov et al., n.d.). Consequently, when used as a therapeutic intervention in dry AMD, SkQ1 is expected to mitigate mitochondrial dysfunction associated with drusen accumulation and, in turn, slow or potentially reverse the progression of geographic atrophy—one of the major sight-threatening complications of the disease.

Overall Evaluation:  
In summary, SkQ1 represents a promising repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry age-related macular degeneration based on a robust foundation of molecular design, preclinical efficacy, and established ocular safety. One of the major strengths of SkQ1 lies in its rational design: as a rechargeable, mitochondria-targeted antioxidant, it overcomes limitations faced by conventional antioxidants that lack mitochondrial specificity. The ability of the TPP⁺ moiety to drive precise mitochondrial accumulation ensures that the compound is concentrated at the site of maximal ROS production, while its plastoquinone component provides potent scavenging of both superoxide and lipid peroxyl radicals. This dual functionality not only neutralizes damaging oxidative species but also supports mitochondrial membrane integrity by stabilizing cardiolipin and potentially mitigating excessive proton leakage. Such a mechanism is particularly relevant to RPE cells, where chronic photo-oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction are central to the pathogenesis of dry AMD (Apostolova & Victor, 2015; Olovnikov et al., n.d.; Lukashev et al., 2014).

Another significant advantage is the extensive preclinical and early clinical evidence supporting SkQ1’s efficacy in ocular applications. Its use in animal models—spanning species from rodents to macaques—has consistently shown protective effects on retinal structure and function, including preservation of ERG responses and reduced vacuolization of the RPE. Furthermore, the successful clinical trials for dry eye syndrome confirm that topical administration of SkQ1 is not only efficacious but also well-tolerated in humans, thereby reducing the risk related to ocular drug delivery and systemic exposure (Gueven et al., 2017; Weissig, 2020; Suárez-Rivero et al., 2021).

Nevertheless, several challenges and potential weaknesses must be carefully weighed. One concern is the dose-dependent behavior of SkQ1. While nanomolar concentrations are effective and safe, there is evidence that at higher concentrations, the molecule can exhibit prooxidant or detergent-like effects—leading to membrane disruption or uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation. This narrow window between the antioxidant and prooxidant activities necessitates rigorous control of dosing in clinical settings to avoid inadvertent exacerbation of mitochondrial dysfunction (Apostolova & Victor, 2015; Suárez-Rivero et al., 2021; Shinn & Lagalwar, 2021). Additionally, while many studies have focused on the compound’s ability to protect against acute oxidative insults, the etiology of dry AMD is multifactorial and chronic. Although animal models such as the OXYS rat provide valuable insights into age-related ocular changes, the long-term efficacy and safety of SkQ1 in preventing geographic atrophy in the context of chronic, low-grade oxidative stress remain to be fully established in human subjects (Wanagat et al., 2010; Belenichev et al., 2024).

It is also important to note that while the proposed mechanism of action for SkQ1—namely, quenching of lipid peroxyl radicals and superoxide to preserve mitochondrial membrane potential—has been well-documented in vitro and in animal models (particularly using ARPE-19 assays with JC-1 and DCFDA readouts), extrapolating these findings to the complex human pathology of dry AMD will require careful clinical trial design. Key endpoints such as changes in drusen load, progression of geographic atrophy, and improvements in visual function would need to be monitored over extended periods. Moreover, given that oxidative stress and inflammation are part of an intricate network of pathological events in dry AMD, combination therapies that address multiple targets may ultimately yield more significant benefits than a single-agent approach (Olovnikov et al., n.d.; Gueven et al., 2017).

From a pharmacokinetic perspective, the selective accumulation of SkQ1 in the mitochondria is a highly attractive feature, as it maximizes local drug concentrations while minimizing systemic exposure and potential off-target effects. This is especially critical for ocular applications, where local delivery via eye drops can achieve high retinal levels with minimal systemic absorption. Nonetheless, formulation stability, light sensitivity, and proper storage conditions are factors that must be optimized for successful commercial development (Weissig, 2020; Gueven et al., 2017; Ji et al., 2021).

In aggregate, the overall evaluation of SkQ1 as a therapeutic candidate for dry age-related macular degeneration is cautiously optimistic. The compound’s design as a mitochondria-targeted, rechargeable antioxidant offers compelling advantages in addressing mitochondrial dysfunction—a key element in the pathogenesis of dry AMD. Preclinical studies have demonstrated its ability to protect RPE cells, preserve mitochondrial membrane potential, and improve retinal function, while early clinical studies in related ocular conditions confirm its safety profile for topical administration. However, due diligence is warranted to establish the optimal dosing regimen that maximizes beneficial antioxidant effects while avoiding potential prooxidant toxicity. Additionally, further rigorous clinical trials specifically targeting dry AMD endpoints are necessary to confirm that the promising outcomes observed in animal studies translate into measurable clinical benefit in patients with geographic atrophy (Apostolova & Victor, 2015; Gueven et al., 2017; Olovnikov et al., n.d.; Wanagat et al., 2010; Weissig, 2020).

Given the comprehensive body of work underpinning the mechanism of action, the established track record in various preclinical ocular models, and the supporting data from human dry-eye trials, SkQ1 appears to have significant potential as a repurposed drug candidate for dry age-related macular degeneration. Its unique targeting of mitochondria—particularly within RPE cells—and its capacity to prevent ROS-induced mitochondrial collapse make it especially well-suited to counteract the chronic photo-oxidative stress that characterizes this disease. The next steps for validating SkQ1 in this indication should include well-designed in vivo studies in AMD-relevant animal models, followed by early phase clinical trials with careful dose-escalation studies and robust assessment of retinal morphology and function. Overall, SkQ1 stands out as a promising therapeutic candidate that merits further investigation within our drug development pipeline (Apostolova & Victor, 2015; Olovnikov et al., n.d.; Weissig, 2020; Gueven et al., 2017; Wanagat et al., 2010).
