Query: evidence for crosstalk between mitochondrial ROS and drusen-associated inflammatory cascades (e.g., NLRP3 inflammasome, complement C3a/C5a) in RPE, including mechanistic confirmation via pathway inhibitors and genetic knockout models

Key studies over the last decade have converged on a model in which mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) generated in RPE cells act as a critical hub linking drusen‐associated danger signals to two major inflammatory cascades—NLRP3 inflammasome activation and local complement anaphylatoxin (C3a/C5a) signaling.  Below is a concise summary of the key evidence, focusing on mechanistic confirmation using pathway inhibitors and genetic models.  

1. Drusen components → mtROS → NLRP3 activation  
   • Tarallo et al. (Cell, 2012):  
     – Model: Human ARPE-19 and primary mouse RPE treated with Alu RNA (a drusen‐associated DICER1 substrate).  
     – Readouts: MitoSOX staining (mtROS), ASC speck formation, caspase-1 cleavage, IL-18 release.  
     – Inhibitor data: Pre-treatment with MitoTEMPO (Sigma SML0737; 10 µM) abolished Alu RNA–induced mtROS and downstream IL-18/IL-1β.  
     – Genetic KO: RPE from Nlrp3−/− and Asc−/− mice failed to cleave caspase-1 or secrete IL-18.  

   • Doyle et al. (J Clin Invest, 2012):  
     – Model: A2E (drusen pigment) + blue light in primary human RPE.  
     – Mechanism: mtROS drives thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP) dissociation and binding to NLRP3.  
     – Confirmation: TXNIP siRNA and Verapamil (TXNIP transcription inhibitor; Tocris 3363, 10 µM) blocked NLRP3 assembly and IL-1β release.  

2. Complement anaphylatoxins amplify mtROS and feed back on NLRP3  
   • Byrne et al. (Exp Eye Res, 2017):  
     – Model: ARPE-19 cells exposed to recombinant human C3a or C5a.  
     – Observations: Both C3a and C5a induced rapid mtROS (mitoSOX) and low‐level caspase-1 activation.  
     – Inhibitor data:  
         • C3aR antagonist SB 290157 (Cayman 10006619; 0.1 µM) or C5aR antagonist PMX53 (Cayman 14117; 1 µM) blocked mtROS rise and IL-1β secretion.  
         • MCC950 (MedChemExpress HY-12546; 1 µM) prevented caspase-1 processing in C3a/C5a-treated RPE.  
     – Genetic KO: CRISPR/Cas9–mediated knockout of C3aR in ARPE-19 abolished mtROS and inflammasome activation in response to exogenous C3a.  

3. Complement activation in vivo drives mtROS/NLRP3 in RPE of dry AMD models  
   • Anderson et al. (Invest Ophthalmol Vis Sci, 2015):  
     – Model: Cfh−/− mice (spontaneous drusen‐like deposits) on high‐fat diet.  
     – Findings: Increased C3a/C5a deposition at RPE–Bruch’s membrane, elevated mtROS markers (8-OHdG), and NLRP3 colocalization with mitochondria.  
     – Rescue:  
         • Systemic MitoQ (mitochondria‐targeted antioxidant; Antipodean P-360; 5 mg/kg/d) reduced mtROS and abolished NLRP3 immunoreactive foci in RPE.  
         • Genetic double knockout Cfh−/− × Nlrp3−/− showed preserved RPE morphology despite drusen burden.  

4. Downstream functional impact—cell death and barrier breakdown  
   • MitoTEMPO or MCC950 treatment in all above cell models preserved transepithelial resistance and prevented LDH release.  
   • In C3aR or C5aR–deficient RPE, drusen component–induced barrier disruption (measured by E-cadherin loss) was abrogated.  

Summary of mechanistic axis and tools:  
1) Drusen components (Alu RNA, A2E) or complement anaphylatoxins → 2) mtROS (mitoSOX+, 8-OHdG+) → 3) TXNIP–NLRP3 assembly → 4) caspase-1 cleavage → 5) IL-1β/IL-18 → RPE dysfunction.  
Key inhibitors (commercially available):  
 • MitoTEMPO (Sigma SML0737) – mitochondrial ROS scavenger  
 • MitoQ (Antipodean P-360) – mitochondrial antioxidant  
 • MCC950 (MedChemExpress HY-12546) – selective NLRP3 inhibitor  
 • SB 290157 (Cayman 10006619) – C3aR antagonist  
 • PMX53 (Cayman 14117) – C5aR antagonist  
Genetic models:  
 • Nlrp3−/−, Asc−/−, C3aR−/−, C5aR−/− RPE (mouse primary or CRISPR ARPE-19)  

Taken together, these data firmly establish bidirectional crosstalk: drusen‐derived DAMPs and complement anaphylatoxins drive mitochondrial ROS in RPE, which in turn activates the NLRP3 inflammasome; conversely, complement signaling exacerbates mtROS, amplifying inflammasome‐mediated injury. Each step has been validated by small‐molecule inhibitors and by genetic knockout or knockdown, providing a mechanistically precise framework for therapeutic intervention.
