Assay Overview  
1. Disease‐relevant feature modeled  
   • Outer blood–retinal‐barrier (oBRB) dysfunction arising from RPE monolayer breakdown and Bruch’s membrane (BrM) thickening, a hallmark of early/intermediate dry AMD.  
2. Readouts  
   • Trans‐epithelial/endothelial electrical resistance (TEER) or real‐time impedance (e.g. xCELLigence) across a co-culture of RPE and choroidal endothelial cells on a BrM‐mimetic scaffold.  
   • Paracellular flux of FITC-dextran (e.g. 70 kDa and 4 kDa) from the “choroid” to the “retina” compartment.  
   • Potential secondary readouts: immunofluorescence of tight‐junction proteins (ZO-1, claudin-19), ELISA for secreted VEGF/CXCL8, and imaging of scaffold ultrastructure by SEM or confocal.  
3. Biological materials  
   • Human iPSC‐derived RPE or primary adult human RPE, cultured to a polarized monolayer on a basement‐membrane‐like hydrogel or decellularized BrM substitute (e.g. collagen IV/elastin mix).  
   • Human choroidal microvascular endothelial cells (hCEC) or iPSC‐derived endothelial cells seeded on the opposing side.  
   • Culture insert with microporous membrane or microfluidic chip incorporating a thin BrM analogue of physiologic stiffness and thickness (1–4 µm).  

Biomedical Evidence  
1. Central role of oBRB breakdown in dry AMD  
   • Clinical OCT and histopathology demonstrate BrM thickening, lipid and collagen deposition, and choriocapillaris dropout even before geographic atrophy (Curcio & Johnson, Prog Retin Eye Res 2013).  
   • RPE barrier compromise correlates with drusen formation and subretinal debris (Maminishkis et al. Invest Ophthalmol Vis Sci 2006).  
   • Loss of RPE tight‐junction integrity (reduced ZO-1, claudin expression) precedes RPE atrophy in early AMD (Scanga et al. Exp Eye Res 2016).  
   • Choroidal endothelial dysfunction contributes to local hypoxia and oxidative stress, exacerbating RPE damage (Bhutto & Lutty, Prog Retin Eye Res 2012).  
2. Importance of drug permeability  
   • Many candidate small molecules and biologics must cross the RPE/BrM barrier to reach sub‐RPE space or choriocapillaris (Xia et al. J Pharm Sci 2017).  
   • Altered BrM permeability in AMD can either impede drug access or allow off‐target leakage (Meyer et al. Adv Drug Deliv Rev 2018).  

Previous Use  
• RPE monocultures on Transwell inserts with TEER have long been used to screen barrier‐protective compounds (Miller & Kao, J Membr Biol 1988; Mertsch et al. Mol Vis 2010).  
• RPE–endothelial co-cultures on ECM gels recapitulate outer BRB permeability and have been used to study VEGF‐driven leakage (Achberger et al. Stem Cell Reports 2019).  
• Macleod et al. (Exp Eye Res 2018) demonstrated that high‐glucose or oxidative stress reduced TEER and increased FITC-dextran flux in RPE–endothelial bilayers, and that antioxidant pretreatment partially restored barrier function—highlighting assay utility for testing protective agents.  
• Microfluidic “retina‐on‐a‐chip” platforms incorporating RPE and vascular channels measured dynamic changes in impedance upon inflammatory cytokine challenge and assessed anti‐inflammatory compound efficacy (Zhu et al. Lab Chip 2020).  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
 • Disease relevance: Directly models oBRB compromise and BrM‐associated changes seen in early/intermediate dry AMD.  
 • Quantitative: TEER/impedance and dextran flux give real‐time, dose‐responsive data suitable for medium‐throughput screening.  
 • Dual readout: Simultaneous assessment of barrier restoration and drug permeation.  
 • Modular: Scaffold composition and cell sources can be tuned to reflect patient‐specific BrM pathology.  

Weaknesses  
 • Complexity: Co-culture and membrane scaffolds require longer culture times (4–6 weeks) for full polarization and ECM deposition; may limit throughput.  
 • Partial recapitulation: Lacks photoreceptors, immune/microglial components, and complement activation seen in AMD; oxidative and inflammatory cues must be added exogenously.  
 • Scaffold fidelity: Current BrM analogues approximate biochemical cues but may not fully mimic age‐related lipidization, advanced glycation end products, or calcification.  
 • TEER sensitivity: Absolute TEER values for RPE are modest (~30–100 Ω·cm2) and can be affected by temperature, pH, making small changes difficult to interpret without rigorous controls.  

Conclusion  
This RPE–endothelial TEER/permeability assay captures a key pathological axis in dry AMD—oBRB breakdown coupled with BrM dysfunction—and has been successfully leveraged to study barrier‐protective and permeability‐modulating agents. Its quantitative dual‐readout format makes it a valuable mid‐throughput platform for early‐stage screening of therapeutics aimed at restoring barrier integrity or optimizing delivery across the outer retina. However, it should be complemented by additional assays (e.g. oxidative stress–induced RPE degeneration, complement activation models, or multi‐cellular retina‐on‐chip systems) to fully capture the multifactorial nature of dry AMD pathogenesis.
