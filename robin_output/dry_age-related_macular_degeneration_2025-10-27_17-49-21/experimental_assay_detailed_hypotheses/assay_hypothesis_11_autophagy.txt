Assay Overview  
The proposed assay measures autophagy flux in human retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells by quantifying LC3‐II turnover and p62/SQSTM1 clearance in the presence and absence of lysosomal inhibitors (e.g., bafilomycin A1 or chloroquine). RPE cells (commonly ARPE-19 or primary human fetal RPE) are cultured under standard conditions, treated with test compounds or vehicle control, and then exposed to a lysosomal inhibitor for a defined interval (e.g., 4–6 h). Endpoints:  
•  LC3-II levels by Western blot (ratio LC3-II/LC3-I) ± inhibitor to compute flux.  
•  p62 abundance by Western blot or ELISA ± inhibitor.  
•  Optionally: confocal immunofluorescence of mCherry-GFP-LC3 reporter to visualize autophagosome‐to‐autolysosome transition.  
This models the compromised autophagy–lysosomal pathway in dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD), which contributes to lipofuscin and drusen accumulation in RPE.

Biomedical Evidence  
1. Centrality of RPE autophagy in dry AMD A body of histopathological and biochemical studies has linked impaired autophagy/lysosomal clearance in RPE to the hallmark pigment granule (lipofuscin) and drusen deposits seen in early and intermediate dry AMD (Mitter et al. Autophagy 2014; Ferrington et al. J Proteomics 2016). Accumulation of the bisretinoid A2E in RPE lysosomes impairs lysosomal pH and enzymatic activity, leading to defective degradation of photoreceptor outer segments and cellular debris (Eldred and Lasky ’94; Sparrow et al. Prog Retin Eye Res 2012).  
2. Genetic and clinical correlations GWAS have implicated genes regulating phagolysosomal function and oxidative stress response—such as ABCA4, CTSK and CFH—in AMD risk (Fritsche et al. Nat Genet 2016). Clinically, areas of geographic atrophy correlate with decreased expression of key autophagy regulators (ATG5, Beclin-1) in RPE from donor eyes (Golestaneh et al. Aging Cell 2017).  
3. In vivo and ex vivo models Support from animal models: RPE-specific knockout of autophagy genes (Atg5, Atg7) in mice recapitulates features of RPE degeneration and drusen‐like sub‐RPE deposits (Zhang et al. Autophagy 2013). Human donor RPE from AMD patients display reduced autophagic flux relative to age-matched controls (Mitter et al. 2014; Ferrington et al. 2016). Together, these data underscore that restoring autophagy flux is a plausible therapeutic strategy.

Previous Use  
While autophagy flux assays are routine in oncology and neurodegeneration drug screens, their use in AMD‐focused RPE models is emerging:  
•  Mitter et al. (Autophagy 2014) first applied LC3-II/p62 flux measurements in ARPE-19 treated with A2E to demonstrate that A2E impairs autophagy. They showed that pharmacological inducers (e.g., rapamycin, trehalose) partially rescue flux and reduce lipofuscin accumulation.  
•  Xiong et al. (Methods Mol Biol 2018) detailed protocols for mCherry-GFP-LC3 reporter RPE lines to screen compounds that enhance autophagosome maturation.  
•  Golestaneh et al. (Aging Cell 2017) used p62 clearance as a biomarker in primary AMD RPE cultures to validate small molecules that normalize lysosomal pH and proteolysis.  
Collectively, these studies demonstrate feasibility of using the assay to triage and prioritize autophagy‐modulating compounds in an AMD context.

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
•  Disease relevance: Directly models a well‐validated pathogenic mechanism in RPE central to dry AMD.  
•  Quantitative flux readouts: Differentiates between increased autophagosome biogenesis versus improved clearance—a common pitfall of steady‐state LC3 measures (Klionsky et al. Autophagy 2016).  
•  Adaptable to medium throughput: Reporter‐based imaging (mCherry-GFP-LC3) can be scaled to 96- or 384-well plates.  
•  Mechanistic insight: Assay distinguishes compounds acting at the initiation (mTOR, AMPK) versus lysosomal acidification (V-ATPase, TFEB) steps.

Weaknesses  
•  Cell‐line limitations: ARPE-19 may lack full polarity and pigment content of native RPE; primary cells have donor variability and limited lifespan.  
•  In vitro simplification: Does not recapitulate complement activation, choroidal–RPE interactions, or photoreceptor turnover in vivo.  
•  Off‐target autophagy modulation: Many agents affect autophagy indirectly via stress pathways; hits require orthogonal validation of specificity and RPE toxicity.  
•  Endpoint sensitivity: LC3-II and p62 levels can be influenced by overall protein synthesis or degradation pathways; rigorous controls and time‐course analyses are essential.

Conclusion  
An autophagy flux restoration assay in RPE cells offers a mechanistically grounded, experimentally tractable platform for screening dry AMD–relevant therapeutics. Its ability to pinpoint interventions that normalize lysosomal clearance addresses a core defect in early disease stages. However, hits must be validated in more complex models (e.g., polarized iPSC-derived RPE, ex vivo retina explants, or animal models) to confirm translational potential.
