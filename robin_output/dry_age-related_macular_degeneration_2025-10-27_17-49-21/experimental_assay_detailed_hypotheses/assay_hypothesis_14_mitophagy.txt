Assay Overview  
•  Disease aspect modeled: This cell-based assay probes mitophagy activation in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) under oxidative stress—specifically the PINK1/Parkin–mediated clearance of damaged mitochondria. Defective mitophagy in RPE is increasingly recognized as a contributor to retinal degeneration in dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD).  
•  Readouts and methods  
   –  PINK1 stabilization: measured by immunoblotting of mitochondrial and cytosolic fractions after stress (e.g., H2O2 or rotenone) to detect the 63 kDa active form of PINK1 on the outer mitochondrial membrane.  
   –  Parkin recruitment: visualized via immunofluorescence or live-cell imaging of fluorescently tagged Parkin (e.g., Parkin–GFP) to quantify the percentage of mitochondria that accumulate Parkin puncta.  
   –  Mitophagosome formation and flux: assessed by co-localization of LC3 or p62 with mitochondrial markers (e.g., TOM20) in fixed cells, or by ratiometric mitophagy reporters such as mito-Keima in live cells. Flux inhibitors (bafilomycin A1) can be used to distinguish increased autophagosome formation from reduced turnover.  
•  Cell system: Human RPE cell lines (e.g., ARPE-19) or, preferably, iPSC-derived RPE to capture a more native phenotype. Oxidative stress is imposed pharmacologically to mimic chronic age-related mitochondrial damage.

Biomedical Evidence  
•  Centrality of mitochondrial dysfunction in dry AMD: Multiple studies show that RPE from AMD donors accumulate mitochondrial DNA damage, produce excess reactive oxygen species (ROS), and have impaired respiratory function (Ferrington et al., Invest Ophthalmol Vis Sci 2015; Krohne et al., Aging Cell 2019).  
•  Impaired mitophagy in AMD RPE: Ferrington and colleagues demonstrated that aged and AMD-derived RPE exhibit reduced Parkin translocation and delayed clearance of damaged mitochondria. Defective mitophagy correlates with increased oxidative stress, lipofuscin accumulation, and cell death pathways implicated in geographic atrophy.  
•  Clinical correlations: Post-mortem AMD eyes show RPE thinning, loss of mitochondrial cristae integrity, and drusen enriched in mitochondrial proteins—pointing to stalled mitochondrial turnover (Feher et al., Prog Retin Eye Res 2006). Genetic studies link variants in mitochondrial maintenance genes to AMD risk, underscoring the relevance of mitophagy.  
•  Complementary pathways: While complement dysregulation and lipid metabolism are also key in AMD, mitochondrial quality control intersects with inflammatory signaling (e.g., NLRP3 inflammasome activation following mitochondrial ROS release), tying mitophagy defects to broader disease mechanisms.

Previous Use  
•  Mitophagy as a drug-discovery endpoint: In neurodegeneration and metabolic disease fields, mito-Keima and Parkin-GFP assays have been used in medium-throughput screens to find small molecules (e.g., urolithin A, kinetin analogs) that enhance PINK1/Parkin activity or general autophagy.  
•  RPE-specific studies: A handful of academic groups have applied mitophagy reporters in ARPE-19 or primary RPE to show rescue of clearance defects by pharmacological NRF2 activators or NAD+ boosters (Tarallo et al., Nat Commun 2019). However, systematic screening in RPE to identify lead compounds for AMD remains limited.  
•  Key discoveries: These assays have elucidated that boosting mitophagy can reduce ROS accumulation, decrease drusen precursor lipids, and improve phagocytic function in RPE, reinforcing the therapeutic potential of targeting mitochondrial turnover.

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
•  Disease relevance: Directly addresses a mechanistic defect (mitochondrial clearance) documented in dry AMD pathogenesis.  
•  Quantifiable, multiparametric outputs: Enables simultaneous measurement of PINK1 stabilization, Parkin recruitment, and downstream autophagosome formation.  
•  Translational potential: Human RPE‐based system increases physiological relevance versus non‐ retinal lines.  
•  Target validation: Compounds identified can be triaged using orthogonal endpoints (ROS assays, RPE survival, phagocytosis) and in vivo models.

Weaknesses  
•  Throughput and complexity: Imaging-based mitophagy assays (e.g., mito-Keima) require specialized equipment and can be lower throughput than simple biochemical readouts.  
•  Flux interpretation: Static co-localization may not distinguish between increased autophagosome formation and blocked degradation; requires careful use of lysosomal inhibitors.  
•  Model limitations: ARPE-19 cells have altered polarity and melanin content; iPSC-RPE are more predictive but costlier and slower to generate.  
•  Single-pathway focus: Dry AMD is multifactorial (complement activation, lipid dysregulation, inflammation); rescuing mitophagy alone may not fully restore RPE health.  
•  Translational gap: Enhancing mitophagy in vitro may not translate to durable RPE protection in the complex retinal environment.

Conclusion  
A mitophagy activation assay in human RPE offers a biologically compelling platform for early-stage screening of dry AMD therapeutics. While it robustly models a validated disease mechanism and yields actionable readouts, careful assay design (flux controls, use of physiologically relevant RPE) and integration with complementary assays will be essential to maximize predictive value and identify candidates likely to succeed in vivo.
