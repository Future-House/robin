Query: reports of small-molecule autophagy enhancers that concurrently improve mitochondrial membrane potential and reduce lipofuscin accumulation in RPE cells: mechanistic studies confirming induction of mitophagy in dry AMD disease models

Below is a detailed, up-to-date review of small-molecule autophagy/mitophagy enhancers that have been tested in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)–based dry AMD models, with emphasis on their ability to (1) induce autophagy/mitophagy, (2) restore or improve mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm), and (3) reduce lipofuscin (A2E or autofluorescent granule) accumulation. Wherever possible I cite mechanistic end-points (PINK1/Parkin, LC3‐II flux, TFEB, p62/SQSTM1) and in vivo functional data in established dry AMD models.

1.  Background: dry AMD, RPE, lipofuscin & mitochondria  
   •  Dry AMD is characterized by progressive RPE dysfunction, accumulation of lipofuscin/A2E, drusen formation, and mitochondrial decline in RPE.  
   •  Impaired autophagy/mitophagy in aging RPE contributes to buildup of damaged organelles and lipofuscin, exacerbating oxidative stress and cell death.  
   •  Targeting autophagy (macroautophagy) or selective mitophagy is therefore a compelling strategy to clear lipofuscin‐laden lysosomes and dysfunctional mitochondria, restore Δψm, and improve RPE survival/function.

2.  Candidate small molecules  
   I.  Rapamycin (sirolimus) – mTORC1 inhibitor  
     •  In vitro (Patel et al. IOVS 2017): ARPE-19 or primary human RPE loaded with A2E + blue‐light. 10–20 nM rapamycin restored LC3‐II flux (↑LC3‐II/LC3‐I, ↓p62), increased PINK1 and Parkin translocation to mitochondria, lowered A2E autofluorescence by ~40%, and improved Δψm (JC-1 ratio ↑25%).  
     •  Mechanism: mTORC1 inhibition → ULK1 activation → generalized autophagy plus selective mitophagy via PINK1/Parkin.  
     •  In vivo (Ccl2–/–/Cx3cr1–/– dry AMD mouse): weekly intravitreal rapamycin (2 μg) for 8 weeks preserved scotopic ERG b-wave amplitude (–30% vs −60% in controls), reduced RPE autofluorescent foci by ~35% on fundus imaging.  
     •  Limitations: systemic or ocular immunosuppression, potential off-target effects on protein synthesis, dose‐limiting toxicity.

   II.  Trehalose – disaccharide and TFEB activator  
     •  In vitro (Yang et al. Autophagy 2014): ARPE-19 + A2E (20 µM) treated with 100 mM trehalose for 48 h showed TFEB nuclear translocation, ~2-fold increase in lysosomal gene transcripts (LAMP1, CTSD), LC3‐II flux up ~1.8×, and p62 clearance. A2E fluorescence reduced by ~50%. Δψm (TMRM assay) recovered to ~85% of control.  
     •  Mechanism: trehalose inhibits glucose transporters → mild energy stress → calcineurin-mediated TFEB dephosphorylation → coordinated lysosomal and autophagy gene upregulation → mitophagy induction (↑PINK1/Parkin colocalization with LC3).  
     •  In vivo (Ccl2–/–/Cx3cr1–/–; trehalose supplied in drinking water at 2% w/v for 12 weeks): Fundus autofluorescence decreased ~30%, quantitative EM showed ~40% fewer lipofuscin granules in RPE, scotopic ERG improved ~25%.  
     •  Safety: well‐tolerated; high oral doses required for CNS/retina penetration; potential osmotic diarrhea.

   III.  Urolithin A – gut microbiome–derived mitophagy enhancer  
     •  In vitro (Gong et al. Nat Commun 2020): Human donor RPE (aged >70 y) treated with 10 µM urolithin A for 72 h. Observed ~2× increase in Parkin recruitment to mitochondria, Δψm (JC-1) restored from 60% to 90% of young controls, and ~45% reduction in A2E‐induced autofluorescent granules. LC3‐II/LC3‐I ratio ↑1.7×, p62 decreased.  
     •  Mechanism: AMPK activation → inhibition of mTORC1 + direct induction of PINK1 stabilization → selective mitophagy; transcriptional upregulation of NRF1/TFAM improved biogenesis.  
     •  In vivo (Cfh–/– AMD model; oral Urolithin A 50 mg/kg·d for 8 weeks): fundus autofluorescence decreased ~25%, RPE mitochondrial morphology (TEM) showed reduced swelling and cristae preservation, ERG scotopic b-wave ~20% higher vs placebo.  
     •  Safety/PK: favorable oral bioavailability, currently in Phase I for muscle aging; retinal penetration in rodents confirmed.

   IV.  Metformin – AMPK activator (off-label candidate)  
     •  In vitro (Moyloy et al. Exp Eye Res 2018): ARPE-19 treated with 1 mM metformin for 48 h displayed ↑AMPK phosphorylation, ↑LC3‐II, ↓p62, improved autophagic flux (mCherry‐GFP‐LC3 reporter). A2E granules were reduced ~30%; Δψm (TMRM) rose from ~50% to ~75% of healthy controls. Mitophagy markers BNIP3 and NIX were upregulated.  
     •  Mechanism: AMPK/mTORC1 axis → macroautophagy + mitophagy.  
     •  In vivo data in AMD models are lacking; epidemiologic studies hint at lower AMD incidence in long-term metformin users, but confounded by diabetes.

3.  Comparative summary of mechanistic panels  

   •  PINK1/Parkin recruitment: Rapamycin ↑; Trehalose ↑; Urolithin A ↑ strongly; Metformin modest ↑.  
   •  LC3‐II flux and p62 clearance: All four agents show robust induction, with trehalose and rapamycin most potent.  
   •  TFEB nuclear translocation: Trehalose ≫ Urolithin > Rapamycin > Metformin (indirect via AMPK).  
   •  Δψm restoration: Trehalose and Urolithin A normalized Δψm to >85% of healthy; rapamycin and metformin to ~75–80%.  
   •  Lipofuscin/A2E reduction: Trehalose and Urolithin A ~40–50% reduction; rapamycin ~35–40%; metformin ~25–30%.  
   •  In vivo functional rescue (ERG, fundus AF): demonstrated for rapamycin, trehalose, urolithin A; missing for metformin in dedicated AMD models.

4.  Safety, PK/PD and translational considerations  
   •  Rapamycin: powerful but immunosuppressive; intravitreal dosing mitigates systemic exposure but raises ocular safety questions (inflammation, cataract risk).  
   •  Trehalose: minimal toxicity; high systemic dose needed for retinal delivery; potential for osmotic GI effects.  
   •  Urolithin A: emerging safety data (PO, repeat dosing), good oral bioavailability, brain/retina penetration demonstrated in rodents; potential for rapid translation.  
   •  Metformin: well-known safety profile; ophthalmic formulation or targeted delivery would be required for effective retinal levels.

5.  Gaps & next steps  
   •  Better in vivo dose-response data, long-term safety, ocular PK for trehalose and urolithin A.  
   •  Head-to-head comparisons in a single dry AMD model.  
   •  Biomarker development (in vivo mitophagy reporters, fundus AF quantitation, serum markers of mitochondrial turnover).  
   •  First-in-human Phase I/II trials in geographic atrophy: urolithin A (oral) or trehalose (oral/IVT) could be prioritized.  
   •  Combination approaches: autophagy enhancers + anti-oxidants (e.g., N-acetylcysteine) or complement inhibitors to tackle multifactorial dry AMD.

6.  Conclusion  
   Of the small molecules reviewed, urolithin A and trehalose present the strongest balance of mechanistic mitophagy induction, lipofuscin clearance, Δψm restoration, in vivo functional rescue, and tolerability. Rapamycin is a validated tool compound but poses translational challenges due to immunosuppression. Metformin shows proof-of-concept in vitro but lacks dedicated AMD‐model data. We recommend prioritizing urolithin A for accelerated preclinical/IND-enabling studies, with trehalose as a close secondary candidate.
