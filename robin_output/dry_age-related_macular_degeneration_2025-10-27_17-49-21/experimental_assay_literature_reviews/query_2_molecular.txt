Query: “Molecular composition and biogenesis of drusen in dry age-related macular degeneration: characterization of extracellular deposits, complement activation fragments, cholesterol crystals, lipofuscin fluorophores, and amyloid‐β species using in vitro RPE co-culture assays”

Background  
Dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is characterized by the accumulation of extracellular deposits (drusen) between the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and Bruch’s membrane, leading over time to RPE dysfunction and photoreceptor death. Drusen are complex aggregates containing complement activation fragments (C3a, C5b-9), lipids (cholesterol crystals, phospholipids), lipofuscin fluorophores (A2E and related bisretinoids), and protein aggregates (including amyloid-β species). Complement dysregulation, oxidative stress, impaired RPE phagocytosis, and lipid recycling defects all contribute to drusen formation.

Key literature themes informing assay evaluation  
• Complement activation in drusen biogenesis – Numerous genetic studies (CFH, C3, CFI, CFB polymorphisms) and proteomic analyses of human drusen have shown that chronic, sub-lytic complement activation fosters an inflammatory microenvironment. In vitro RPE models that recapitulate local complement deposition permit testing complement‐modulating agents.  
• Lipid and cholesterol homeostasis – RPE cells recycle photoreceptor outer segment lipids. Impaired cholesterol efflux and accumulation of crystalline cholesterol in Bruch’s membrane have been linked to drusen nucleation. In vitro assays that induce cholesterol crystal formation or perturb ABCA1/ABCG1 transport capture a key pathogenic axis.  
• Lipofuscin and oxidative stress – Bisretinoid accumulation in RPE lysosomes generates reactive oxygen species upon photo‐oxidation. Assays using synthetic A2E loading have been widely used to screen anti‐oxidant and lysosomal‐protective agents.  
• Amyloid-β in drusen – Low‐molecular‐weight amyloid-β species co‐localize with drusen. Amyloid may serve as a scaffold for other drusen materials and amplify complement activation. Co-culture systems that allow extracellular Aβ aggregation alongside RPE may be predictive of anti‐amyloid strategies.  

Relevance and strengths of the described RPE co-culture drusen assay  
1. Multicomponent drusen formation – The assay’s inclusion of complement activation fragments, cholesterol crystals, lipofuscin fluorophores, and amyloid-β better mimics the composite drusen environment than single‐component screens. This complexity allows simultaneous read-outs for anti‐complement, lipid-modulating, antioxidant, and anti-amyloid effects.  
2. RPE functional endpoints – Co-culture formats permit assessment of RPE barrier integrity (transepithelial resistance), phagocytic capacity, mitochondrial function, and survival under drusen‐like stress. These functional measures correlate with RPE health in vivo.  
3. Quantitative imaging and biochemical assays – Fluorescence and electron microscopy can quantify sub‐RPE deposits, while ELISA or mass spectrometry can measure complement fragments, A2E, and Aβ in culture supernatants. This multi-modal read‐out aligns with clinical biomarkers (e.g., circulating complement split products).  

Limitations and gaps  
• Lack of Bruch’s membrane mimicry – Native Bruch’s membrane is a specialized ECM with unique biomechanical and diffusion properties that influence drusen retention. Synthetic substrates may not fully recapitulate this environment.  
• Absence of choroidal and immune cell interactions – Choroidal endothelial cells, macrophages, and microglia contribute to drusen clearance or inflammation. A purely RPE‐centric assay omits potential effects of these cell types and limits evaluation of drugs targeting immune cell recruitment or angiogenic signaling.  
• Long‐term maturation – Drusen form over years in vivo. Accelerated in vitro models may rely on supraphysiological concentrations of A2E, complement, or cholesterol, which could bias screening outcomes toward compounds that counter extreme conditions rather than physiological drusen initiation.  

Opportunities for assay refinement  
• Incorporation of decellularized Bruch’s membrane or engineered ECM hydrogels to better mimic tissue architecture and diffusion barriers.  
• Tri-culture systems including iPSC‐derived choroidal endothelial cells or macrophages to capture inflammatory crosstalk and angiogenic factors.  
• Use of human induced‐pluripotent‐stem‐cell (iPSC)–derived RPE from AMD patients to model genetic susceptibility and to enable precision‐medicine approaches.  
• Time-lapse high‐content imaging for real‐time monitoring of deposit nucleation and RPE responses, improving dynamic read-outs of therapeutic effects.  

Conclusion  
The described in vitro RPE co-culture drusen assay, by integrating key molecular components of drusen, provides a valuable platform for high‐throughput screening of candidate therapeutics aimed at complement modulation, lipid handling, oxidative stress mitigation, or amyloid inhibition. Its relevance is underscored by the multifactorial nature of dry AMD pathology. However, to increase physiological fidelity and predictive power, further refinement to include Bruch’s membrane analogues, immune/choroidal cell interactions, and patient‐derived RPE is recommended. Such advances would bolster translational potential and better inform preclinical decision-making for dry AMD drug candidates.
