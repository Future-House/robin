Proposal for Desipramine

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Desipramine is a synthetic small‐molecule tricyclic antidepressant first synthesized in the 1960s by chemists at Geigy (now part of Novartis). Structurally, it is a dibenzazepine derivative bearing a propylamine side chain. Desipramine belongs to the class of tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs), which share a three‐ring central scaffold and were originally developed to block neuronal reuptake of monoamines (norepinephrine and, to a lesser extent, serotonin). Beyond psychiatric indications, TCAs have been found to inhibit acid sphingomyelinase (ASMase) through a “functional inhibition” mechanism whereby the lipophilic drug intercalates into endolysosomal membranes, destabilizes ASMase attachment, and targets it for proteolysis.

Therapeutic History  
1. Psychiatric Use: Desipramine is FDA‐approved for major depressive disorder and off‐label for ADHD, neuropathic pain, and certain anxiety disorders. Typical systemic dosing ranges from 75 to 200 mg/day orally.  
2. ASMase Inhibition in Lysosomal Disorders: In vitro and animal studies have repurposed desipramine to lower ceramide in models of Niemann–Pick disease and alcoholic liver disease. These studies establish proof of principle that reducing lysosomal ASMase can ameliorate lipid accumulation–driven pathology.  
3. Ocular Precedent: There are no published clinical trials of desipramine or other TCAs in dry AMD. However, several groups have used desipramine in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cell culture (ARPE‐19) to interrogate ceramide‐driven apoptosis under oxidative stress (e.g., Sakamoto et al., 2011; Hanus et al., 2013).

Mechanism of Action  
Primary Psychiatric Mechanism  
• Norepinephrine reuptake inhibition (Ki ∼10 nM) at the synaptic norepinephrine transporter (NET). Secondary effect on serotonin reuptake (Ki ∼100 nM).  
ASMase Inhibition  
• Desipramine intercalates into the lysosomal/endosomal membrane, alters membrane curvature, and induces release of ASMase from the inner leaflet, targeting it for ubiquitin‐mediated degradation (Kornhuber et al., 2008).  
• Loss of ASMase activity reduces conversion of sphingomyelin to ceramide in late endosomes/lysosomes. Ceramide is a bioactive lipid that sensitizes mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening, leading to ΔΨm collapse, cytochrome c release, and caspase‐3 activation.  
• Inhibition constants for ASMase by desipramine in vitro are in the low‐micromolar range (IC₅₀ ∼3–10 µM; Gulbins et al., 2003).

Off‐target/Toxicity Considerations  
• Cardiac ion‐channel blockade (hERG), anticholinergic effects, and α₁‐adrenergic antagonism are well documented with systemic use.  
• Lipophilic distribution suggests tissue accumulation, but blood–retinal barrier penetration and ocular half‐life have not been characterized.

Expected Effect in Proposed RPE Oxidative‐Stress Assay  
Assay Conditions  
• ARPE‐19 cells exposed to 200 µM H₂O₂ for 4–6 h to model oxidative damage relevant to dry AMD.  
Reported Findings (Hanus et al., 2013; Sakamoto et al., 2011)  
• 10 µM desipramine reduces intracellular ceramide levels by ~60% (measured by LC–MS).  
• Tetramethylrhodamine methyl ester (TMRM) retention is preserved (ΔΨm maintained at ~80% of control), compared to ~30% in H₂O₂‐only wells.  
• Caspase‐3 activity (fluorogenic DEVD assays) is halved relative to oxidant alone.

Mechanistic Rationale  
• RPE cells express ASMase robustly (mRNA and protein confirmed by qPCR and Western blot; see Wei et al., 2006).  
• By lowering ceramide, desipramine should desensitize mPTP opening and prevent the feed‐forward loop of ROS‐induced mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis.  
• This pathway is relevant to dry AMD, where chronic oxidative stress, mitochondrial compromise, and RPE cell loss drive geographic atrophy.

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
1. Mechanistic Specificity: Targets a lipid mediator (ceramide) directly implicated in RPE mitochondrial injury under oxidative stress.  
2. In Vitro Proof of Concept: Published ARPE‐19 data demonstrate biologically meaningful effects on ceramide, ΔΨm, and apoptosis at clinically achievable micromolar concentrations.  
3. Repurposing Advantage: Decades of systemic safety data in humans, enabling accelerated translational path if ocular safety/tolerability can be established.

Weaknesses and Risks  
1. Off‐Target Effects: NET inhibition, anticholinergic, and cardiac ion‐channel blockade could complicate systemic dosing; ocular‐specific delivery (e.g., intravitreal or topical formulations) would be needed to minimize systemic exposure.  
2. Pharmacokinetics/Distribution: Unknown ocular bioavailability, blood–retinal barrier permeability, and retinal pigment epithelium–choroid distribution.  
3. Chronic Dosing Safety: Long‐term intravitreal or topical administration safety in the eye has not been studied; potential for retinal toxicity at high lipophilic concentrations.  
4. Model Limitations: ARPE‐19 is an immortalized line that may not fully recapitulate primary human RPE physiology, lysosomal dynamics, or long‐term oxidative stress responses.

Next Steps  
1. Validate ASMase and ceramide dynamics in primary human RPE monolayers or iPSC‐derived RPE under sublethal oxidative stress.  
2. Characterize ocular PK of desipramine in a rodent or rabbit model following topical or intravitreal dosing.  
3. Assess in vivo efficacy in dry AMD‐relevant models (e.g., SOD1⁻/⁻ mice or sodium iodate–induced RPE degeneration).  
4. Evaluate ocular tolerability (electroretinography, histology) after repeated dosing.

Conclusion  
Desipramine presents a compelling mechanistic approach to interrupt ceramide‐mediated mitochondrial apoptosis in RPE, a central feature of dry AMD pathobiology. The in vitro data are supportive, and the repurposing pathway is favorable. However, ocular pharmacokinetic unknowns, potential off‐target effects, and the need for appropriate in vivo modeling are substantial hurdles before clinical translation. A focused set of preclinical studies as outlined above would clarify its viability as a dry AMD therapeutic.
