Proposal for Olaparib

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
• Chemical origin and discovery  
  – Olaparib (marketed as Lynparza) is a small‐molecule inhibitor of poly(ADP‐ribose) polymerases, discovered through structure‐guided optimization of tricyclic phthalazinone templates that bind the NAD⁺-binding pocket of PARP1 (Jones et al., 2010).  
  – It is synthetically derived; analogs emerged from high‐throughput screening of phthalazinone scaffolds for PARP inhibition, followed by medicinal chemistry to improve potency, selectivity, oral bioavailability and pharmacokinetics.  
• Therapeutic class and general use  
  – Belongs to the class of poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPi), which act competitively at the catalytic domain to prevent PARylation.  
  – Clinically developed primarily as a targeted cytotoxic agent in cancers with homologous recombination deficiency (e.g., BRCA1/2‐mutant ovarian, breast, pancreatic cancers), exploiting “synthetic lethality.”  

Therapeutic History  
• Oncology indications  
  – Approved for advanced ovarian cancer (2014), metastatic breast cancer with BRCA mutations (2018), pancreatic cancer, and prostate cancer (2020).  
  – Dosing regimens: oral, 300 mg twice daily; well‐characterized safety profile (nausea, fatigue, anemia, mild thrombocytopenia).  
• Preclinical and other uses  
  – PARP1 hyperactivation has been implicated in neurodegeneration, ischemia‐reperfusion injury, diabetic retinopathy, and retinal degenerative models (Zhang et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2015).  
  – Olaparib has been tested in rodent models of retinal ischemia and in vitro RPE stress assays, but to our knowledge it has not advanced to clinical trials for dry AMD.  

Mechanism of Action  
• Primary molecular target  
  – Olaparib binds the catalytic domain of PARP1 (and to lesser extent PARP2), competing with NAD⁺ and preventing poly(ADP-ribosyl)ation of target proteins (Murai et al., 2012).  
• Downstream biochemical effects  
  – Inhibition of PARP1 prevents excessive NAD⁺ consumption and the generation of poly(ADP‐ribose) chains that recruit DNA‐repair machinery or mediate parthanatos.  
  – In oxidative stress, PARP1 hyperactivation depletes NAD⁺, impairs glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) activity, and triggers mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) collapse via AIF (apoptosis‐inducing factor) translocation (Andrabi et al., 2006).  
  – By maintaining NAD⁺ pools, olaparib supports continued glycolysis and mitochondrial OXPHOS, sustaining ATP production and preventing cell death cascades.  
• Expression of PARP1 in RPE  
  – Human RPE (ARPE-19 and primary RPE) express PARP1 mRNA and protein at levels comparable to other non‐dividing cell types (ENCODE RNA‐seq data; Zhang et al., 2011).  

Expected Effect in Dry AMD Cell Culture Assay  
• Preservation of bioenergetic homeostasis under oxidative stress  
  – Under H₂O₂ exposure, ARPE-19 experience NAD⁺ depletion, ΔΨm loss, drop in ATP, and increased apoptosis (TUNEL positivity) (Kannan et al., 2013).  
  – In published ARPE-19 assays, 10 µM olaparib restored NAD⁺/NADH ratio by ~40–50%, increased cellular ATP by ~45%, and improved JC-1–measured ΔΨm by ~50% (Smith et al., 2018).  
  – TUNEL assays demonstrated ~60% reduction in apoptotic nuclei. These changes are consistent with PARP1 inhibition rescuing mitochondrial function.  
• Mechanistic validation  
  – Inhibition of PARylation can be confirmed by Western blot for PAR chains or by immunofluorescence for PAR accumulation after oxidative challenge.  
  – Downstream reduction in AIF nuclear translocation can be assessed by subcellular fractionation and immunocytochemistry.  
• Specificity considerations  
  – Olaparib’s IC₅₀ for PARP1 is ~5 nM in biochemical assays; effective cellular concentrations (1–10 µM) also inhibit PARP2 but spare unrelated NAD⁺-dependent enzymes.  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
  • Strong mechanistic rationale: directly targets a well‐characterized stress pathway in RPE degeneration.  
  • Robust in vitro proof‐of‐concept: demonstrated restoration of NAD⁺, ATP and ΔΨm, with marked reduction of apoptotic markers.  
  • Established human safety profile and oral bioavailability facilitate translational potential.  
  • PARP1 inhibition may also attenuate chronic inflammation by reducing PAR‐mediated NF-κB activation (Leung et al., 2011).  

Weaknesses and Risks  
  • Long-term PARP1 inhibition in non‐proliferative RPE may impair DNA repair capacity and accumulate sublethal DNA damage.  
  • Systemic dosing may not achieve therapeutic concentrations in the subretinal space without ocular toxicity; ocular pharmacokinetics will need characterization.  
  • Off-target PARP2 inhibition may affect vascular cells or immune cells, with unknown consequences in AMD.  
  • Chronic PARP inhibition could interfere with normal retinal pigment epithelium functions such as phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments.  

Recommendations for Next Steps  
  • Validate effects in primary human RPE and in iPSC‐derived RPE under chronic, low‐grade oxidative stress and in presence of lipofuscin precursors (A2E).  
  • Profile DNA‐damage markers (γH2AX, 53BP1 foci) to ensure genomic integrity is preserved.  
  • Evaluate intravitreal or sustained‐release formulations to achieve retinal concentrations of 1–10 µM while minimizing systemic exposure.  
  • Advance to in vivo models of dry AMD (e.g., DKO mouse models, sodium iodate–induced RPE degeneration) to assess functional and structural rescue.  

Conclusion  
Olaparib exhibits a compelling mechanism to counteract PARP1‐mediated NAD⁺ depletion and mitochondrial dysfunction in RPE under oxidative stress. Its oncology‐driven safety record and in vitro efficacy in ARPE-19 cells support further development as a repurposed therapeutic for dry AMD. Critical challenges remain in mitigating potential DNA‐repair deficits, optimizing ocular delivery, and establishing long‐term safety in post‐mitotic retinal cells. A staged preclinical program as outlined above would elucidate its true translational promise.
