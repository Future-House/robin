Proposal for Elamipretide (SS-31)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
• Compound identity and origin  
  – Elamipretide (also known as SS-31 or Bendavia) is a fully synthetic, cell-permeable tetrapeptide with sequence D-Arg–2′,6′-dimethylTyr–Lys–Phe-NH₂.  
  – It was discovered in the Szeto–Schiller series of aromatic–cationic peptides designed to target mitochondria. Initial structure–activity work by Szeto and colleagues showed that alternating aromatic and basic residues promote mitochondrial uptake and cardiolipin binding.  
  – Elamipretide is manufactured by standard solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS), followed by purification via reverse-phase HPLC.  

• Therapeutic class and prior general use  
  – Belongs to the class of mitochondria-targeted peptides (MTPs), specifically the Szeto–Schiller peptides, which accumulate in the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) independent of membrane potential.  
  – MTPs have been explored broadly as cytoprotective agents in models of ischemia–reperfusion injury, heart failure, renal injury, sepsis, and neurodegeneration owing to their ability to stabilize electron transport chain (ETC) supercomplexes and reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS).  

Therapeutic History  
• Preclinical studies  
  – Cardiovascular: In rodent models of myocardial ischemia–reperfusion, SS-31 reduced infarct size and preserved left ventricular function (Birk et al., 2013).  
  – Neuroprotection: Demonstrated reduced infarct volumes in stroke models (Mao et al., 2012).  
  – Renal and hepatic injury: Protective in models of acute kidney injury and acetaminophen-induced liver toxicity.  

• Clinical studies  
  – Phase I–II trials in primary mitochondrial myopathy, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, and Barth syndrome have established safety and tolerability at systemic doses up to several milligrams per kilogram per day (Karaa et al., 2018; Chatfield et al., 2017).  
  – No registered clinical trial to date has investigated SS-31 in dry AMD patients, though systemic safety data support ocular repurposing.  

• Ocular preclinical work  
  – Two groups have shown that intravitreal SS-31 in rodents (10–100 µg/eye) accumulates in the retina (Cmax ~5 µg/g) and has a vitreous half-life of ~12 h (Smith et al., 2020).  
  – In blue-light and NaIO₃-induced RPE degeneration models, intravitreal SS-31 preserved outer nuclear layer (ONL) thickness and electroretinogram (ERG) amplitudes (Zhao et al., 2021).  

Mechanism of Action  
• Mitochondrial targeting and cardiolipin binding  
  – SS-31’s alternating aromatic (dimethylTyr, Phe) and cationic (Arg, Lys) residues favor insertion into the IMM via electrostatic attraction to cardiolipin (CL), a negatively charged phospholipid enriched in cristae. Surface plasmon resonance and NMR studies confirm low-micromolar affinity (K_D ~1–5 µM) for CL (Birk et al., 2014).  

• Supercomplex stabilization  
  – By clustering around CL, SS-31 promotes assembly of ETC supercomplexes (Complexes I+III+IV), which minimizes electron leak at CoQ and cytochrome c sites. Blue-native PAGE of peptide-treated fibroblasts shows increased respirasome abundance (Petrosillo et al., 2015).  

• Preservation of ΔΨm and reduction of ROS  
  – In oxidative stress models, SS-31 prevents collapse of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) as measured by JC-1 or TMRM fluorescence, and lowers mitochondrial superoxide (MitoSOX or Amplex Red assays) by 40–70% (Moraes et al., 2016).  
  – SS-31 also inhibits peroxidase activity of cytochrome c–cardiolipin complexes, interrupting a self-amplifying cycle of lipid peroxidation (Cheng et al., 2018).  

• Downstream bioenergetic effects  
  – Restoration of ATP levels in stressed cells (luminescent ATP assays) and reduced release of pro-apoptotic factors (cytochrome c, AIF) have been reported in cardiomyocytes, neurons, and renal tubular cells.  

Expected Effect in RPE Cell‐Based Assay  
• Target cell and model validation  
  – ARPE-19 and iPSC-derived RPE express high levels of cardiolipin synthase (CRLS1), respiratory complexes, and key mitochondrial fusion/fission regulators (MFN1/2, OPA1), confirming the machinery for SS-31 action is present (Voigt et al., 2020).  

• Oxidative and light stress paradigms  
  – Published protocols use 200–500 µM H₂O₂ for 4–24 h or 450–500 nm blue light (1–5 mW/cm²) to induce mitochondrial depolarization, ROS generation, lipid peroxidation, and cell death in ARPE-19 (Kannan et al., 2006; Golestaneh et al., 2017).  

• Predicted assay outcomes  
  – ΔΨm assays: Restoration to ≥85% of control fluorescence signal (JC-1 red/green ratio or TMRM intensity) at SS-31 concentrations of 0.1–1 µM.  
  – Mitochondrial ROS: 40–60% reduction in MitoSOX red fluorescence compared to stressed, untreated cells.  
  – ATP production: 1.5- to 2-fold increase over stressed controls (CellTiter-Glo).  
  – Cell viability: 20–40% increase by MTT or Calcein-AM assays; reduced Annexin V/PI staining.  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
  • Mechanistic alignment: Mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative damage in RPE are central to dry AMD pathogenesis (Ferrington et al., 2017). SS-31 directly targets these pathways.  
  • Preclinical ocular efficacy: Rodent light-damage and NaIO₃ models demonstrate morphological (ONL thickness) and functional (ERG) rescue.  
  • Favorable ocular pharmacokinetics and safety: Intravitreal dosing achieves retinal exposure in the therapeutic range without detectable toxicity up to 100 µg/eye in rats.  
  • Human safety data: Extensive systemic trial experience accelerates IND-enabling work for ocular indications.  

Weaknesses and Remaining Questions  
  • Translatability of ARPE-19 data: This immortalized line displays altered mitochondrial biogenesis and lower p53, potentially underestimating stress responses. iPSC-RPE or primary human RPE may show differing sensitivity or uptake kinetics.  
  • Delivery and dosing regimen: Intravitreal half-life (12 h) suggests the need for sustained-release formulation or frequent injections. Subretinal or topical routes remain untested.  
  • Chronic dosing effects: AMD is a slowly progressive disease; long-term safety of repeated intravitreal SS-31 is unknown. Potential immune responses to a peptide must be evaluated.  
  • Off-target actions: Although highly mitochondria-selective, SS-31 may modulate cytosolic signaling or inflammation; comprehensive transcriptomic profiling in RPE is needed.  

Conclusion  
Elamipretide (SS-31) features a strong mechanistic rationale for dry AMD: it restores mitochondrial integrity, reduces ROS, and preserves RPE viability under oxidative and light stress. Preclinical ocular models support its efficacy, and existing human safety data facilitate rapid translation. Key next steps include: (1) repeating in vitro assays in primary or iPSC-derived RPE under chronic stress, (2) developing a long-acting intravitreal formulation, and (3) conducting GLP toxicology and immunogenicity testing in relevant species. If these succeed, SS-31 merits advancement to Phase I/II proof-of-concept trials in geographic atrophy patients.
