Below is a structured, mechanism-focused review of in vitro assay components commonly used to model dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD, “geographic atrophy” phenotype) and to validate target engagement or phenotypic rescue by novel therapeutics.  Each section cites key findings in the literature to help you decide which readouts, cell sources and culture formats best recapitulate disease‐relevant biology.   

1. Pathogenic axes in dry AMD and their in vitro correlates  
  A. Drusen biogenesis and lipid accumulation  
    • In vivo drusen are lipid‐ and complement‐rich deposits between RPE and Bruch’s membrane.  Key drusen constituents include esterified cholesterol, ApoE, vitronectin and complement fragments (C3b, C5b-9).  (Curcio et al., Prog Retin Eye Res 2011)  
    • In vitro, iPSC- or primary RPE treated chronically with oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL), hydroxyapatite nanoparticles or advanced glycation end products (AGEs) accumulate cytoplasmic lipid droplets and secrete ApoE.^1  These models permit screening for compounds that reduce lipid load or drusen-like deposits.  

  B. RPE dysfunction (phagocytosis & barrier integrity)  
    • The RPE daily phagocytoses ~10% of photoreceptor outer segments (POS); impairment leads to lipofuscin (A2E) build-up and cell stress.  (Radu et al., Prog Retin Eye Res 2011)  
    • iPSC-RPE monolayers express tight junction proteins (ZO-1, occludin) and exhibit transepithelial resistance (TEER).  Young et al. (Stem Cell Reports 2017) used TEER and FITC-dextran flux to quantify barrier rescue by small molecules.  
    • Phagocytic flux readouts: pHrodo-labeled POS uptake over time by live-cell imaging or high-content analysis (HCA) of rhodopsin-positive phagosomes.  (Mazzoni et al., J Neurosci 2011)  

  C. Complement activation  
    • Genetic studies implicate CFH, C3, CFI and C9 in AMD risk.  Locally produced RPE complement can amplify lamp1-positive endolysosomal stress and trigger MAC deposition.  (Anderson et al., Invest Ophthalmol Vis Sci 2010)  
    • In vitro addition of human serum or purified C3/C5 induces C5b-9 on RPE; cytotoxicity measured by LDH release or propidium iodide uptake.  (Zhou et al., Hum Mol Genet 2009)  
    • ELISA-based assays for C3a/C5a in conditioned medium track complement turnover and therapeutic modulation.  

  D. Oxidative stress & lipid peroxidation  
    • RPE are highly susceptible to photo-oxidation and mitochondrial ROS.  (Beatty et al., Surv Ophthalmol 2000)  
    • Common in vitro insults: H2O2 pulses, t-butyl hydroperoxide, A2E + blue light.  Readouts include CellROX or DCFDA fluorescence, MitoSOX for mitochondrial ROS, and quantification of 4-HNE or malondialdehyde by ELISA or immunostaining.  (Ferrington et al., Invest Ophthalmol Vis Sci 2005)  

  E. Mitochondrial dysfunction  
    • Aged human donor RPE show fragmented mitochondria and lower spare respiratory capacity.  Seahorse XF assays reveal decreased basal and maximal respiration in AMD iPSC-RPE vs controls.  (Golestaneh et al., Stem Cells 2017)  

  F. Inflammasome activation  
    • RPE express NLRP3; chronic stress (A2E, drusen components, complement) drives IL-1β and IL-18 release.  (Tarallo et al., Cell 2012)  
    • In vitro readouts: IL-1β/IL-18 by ELISA; ASC speck formation by immunofluorescence or ASC-GFP reporters.  

  G. Lipofuscin/A2E accumulation  
    • Accumulates in RPE with age and impairs autophagy.  (Finnemann and Rodriguez-Boulan, J Cell Biol 1999)  
    • A2E can be added exogenously to mimic lipofuscin; quantifiable by HPLC or fluorescence (excitation ~430 nm).  

2. In vitro model systems – pros, cons and disease relevance  
  A. Primary human RPE  
    • Pros: mature phenotype, native Bruch’s membrane attachment.  
    • Cons: donor variability, limited passages, low throughput.  

  B. iPSC-derived RPE monolayers  
    • Pros: patient-specific genetics (CFH risk haplotypes, ARMS2 variants), unlimited supply, amenable to CRISPR.  (Meyer et al., Stem Cell Reports 2011)  
    • Cons: maturation takes 6–8 weeks; some protocols yield immature tight junctions or incomplete pigmentation.  

  C. Co-cultures with choroidal endothelial cells or microglia  
    • Enable study of RPE–choroid crosstalk, complement trafficking, and immune cell recruitment.  (Chen et al., JCI Insight 2018)  
    • More complex readouts (transwell inserts; cytokine panels) but better mimic in vivo milieu.  

  D. 3D retinal organoids  
    • Contain RPE–photoreceptor interactions; can develop subretinal-like deposits.  (Hallam et al., Nat Commun 2018)  
    • Low throughput, variable differentiation, long culture times (months).  

3. Mechanistic, high-content readouts for target validation  
  1. High-content imaging  
    • Multiparametric phenotyping: cell shape, pigmentation, ZO-1 continuity, lysosomal size (LAMP1), lipid droplet number (BODIPY).  Automated image analysis pipelines correlate with RPE health.  (Falk et al., Sci Rep 2019)  
  2. Phagocytic flux assay  
    • Time-lapse imaging of pHrodo-POS uptake and acidification kinetics.  Useful to detect partial rescue of phagosome maturation.  
  3. Barrier integrity  
    • TEER measurements over time; FITC-dextran or Lucifer Yellow paracellular leakage.  
  4. ROS quantification  
    • Live dyes (CellROX, MitoSOX) plus HCA to detect heterogeneity in oxidative burden under compound treatment.  
  5. Autophagy flux  
    • GFP-LC3 or mCherry-GFP-LC3 reporters to distinguish autophagosome vs autolysosome accumulation.  p62/SQSTM1 immunoblot or ELISA as orthogonal readout.  
  6. Complement-mediated cytotoxicity  
    • Incubate RPE with 10–20% human serum (± inhibitors), then stain for C5b-9 and quantify cell death (PI uptake, LDH release).  

4. Biochemical & phenotypic biomarkers for screening and validation  
  • Secreted cytokines: IL-18, IL-1β, TNFα—monitor inflammasome engagement.  
  • Complement fragments in medium: C3a, Bb, C5a by ELISA.  
  • Oxidative damage markers: 8-OHdG (DNA), 4-HNE–protein adducts.  
  • Lipid peroxidation: TBARS assay, BODIPY C11 staining.  
  • Mitochondrial health: JC-1 or TMRM membrane potential dyes; Seahorse metabolic profiling.  

5. Key considerations & recommendations  
  • Disease relevance: No single assay recapitulates full dry AMD pathogenesis.  A tiered strategy—begin with high-throughput oxidative stress or phagocytosis assays in iPSC-RPE, then progress hits into co-culture or complement challenge models—maximizes biological fidelity while managing throughput.  
  • Cell source: Use patient-derived iPSC-RPE carrying high‐risk genotypes for target validation; complement with gene-edited isogenic controls.  
  • Assay validation: Include positive controls (e.g., antioxidants like lutein, complement inhibitors such as anti-C5 antibodies) and negative controls to define dynamic range.  
  • Readout multiplexing: Combine live‐cell imaging dyes to measure ROS, mitochondrial potential and phagocytosis concurrently, reducing well‐to‐well variability.  
  • Biomarker alignment: Whenever possible, correlate in vitro biomarker changes (e.g., reduced C5a, restored TEER, lowered IL-18) with clinical endpoints (retinal thickness by OCT, GA lesion growth) to strengthen translational value.  

Conclusion  
An integrated in vitro platform employing iPSC-RPE monolayers (with CRISPR-defined risk alleles), mechanistic phenotypic assays (phagocytic flux, barrier integrity, ROS, autophagy, complement-cytotoxicity) and multiplexed biochemical biomarkers can recapitulate key facets of dry AMD pathogenesis.  This tiered approach allows early identification of compounds that (1) enhance RPE resilience to oxidative and inflammatory stress, (2) correct fundamental RPE functions (phagocytosis, barrier), and (3) suppress complement or inflammasome‐mediated injury—all critical for geographic atrophy therapeutics.  Such a workflow aligns with recent literature consensus and offers a robust strategy for early‐stage drug discovery in dry AMD.
