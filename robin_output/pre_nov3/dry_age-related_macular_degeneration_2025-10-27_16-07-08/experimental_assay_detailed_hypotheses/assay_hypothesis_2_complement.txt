Assay Overview  
• Rationale and disease aspect modeled  
  – Dry AMD, especially geographic atrophy (GA), is strongly linked to local complement overactivation at the basal side of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). Genetic variants in CFH, C3, CFI, CFB/CFD and histological detection of membrane attack complex (MAC; C5b-9) in drusen and RPE–choroid interfaces point to complement‐mediated RPE injury as a key driver of progression to GA.  
  – This assay recreates sublytic or lytic complement attack on human RPE by exposing a polarized RPE monolayer to normal human serum (NHS), thereby triggering the terminal complement pathway and deposition of C5b-9 on the basal RPE surface.  

• Key steps and readouts  
  1. Culture: Human iPSC-derived RPE cells (or well-characterized primary human RPE) are matured on Transwell inserts or tissue culture plates to a pigmented, polarized monolayer expressing tight junctions and complement regulators (e.g., CD46, CD59).  
  2. Complement challenge: Incubate monolayers with 10–20% NHS (as a source of native complement) for 30 min–4 hr at 37 °C. Heat‐inactivated serum (56 °C, 30 min) is used as a negative control.  
  3. Detection of complement deposition:  
     – Immunofluorescence or Western blot for C5b-9 (anti-C9 neoepitope) to quantify MAC assembly on the basal surface.  
     – Co-stain with propidium iodide (PI) or 7-AAD to identify membrane‐compromised (dying) cells.  
  4. Cytotoxicity measurement:  
     – Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay in supernatants for quantitative cell lysis.  
     – Alternative viability assay (e.g., MTS, ATP‐based) for corroboration.  
  5. Test compounds: Pre-incubate serum or RPE with candidate inhibitors (e.g., complement pathway blockers, receptor antagonists) and assess reduction in C5b-9 deposition and cell death markers versus controls.  

Biomedical Evidence  
• Genetic and histopathological support  
  – Genome-wide and candidate-gene studies have consistently implicated the alternative complement pathway in AMD susceptibility (CFH Y402H, C3 R102G, CFI, CFB/CFD). Individuals carrying risk alleles show elevated systemic and ocular complement activation fragments (C3a, C5a, Bb).  
  – Post-mortem analyses of AMD eyes reveal extensive C5b-9 deposits in drusen, Bruch’s membrane, and on the basal RPE face, correlating with areas of RPE atrophy in GA.  

• Mechanistic links in RPE biology  
  – RPE cells under chronic oxidative stress downregulate complement regulators (e.g., CD46, CD59), sensitizing them to complement attack.  
  – Sublytic MAC deposition on RPE mitochondria triggers inflammasome activation and pro‐inflammatory cytokine release (IL-1β, IL-18), exacerbating local tissue damage.  
  – Animal models (CFH-deficient mice, alternative‐pathway hyperactivation models) develop drusen-like deposits and RPE atrophy, which are ameliorated by complement inhibition.  

• Clinical correlation  
  – Elevated plasma and ocular fluid levels of complement activation fragments are associated with AMD progression rates.  
  – Complement inhibitors (anti-C5, anti-Factor D, C3 inhibitors) have entered clinical trials for GA; while systemic anti-C5 (eculizumab) failed to slow GA, intravitreal C3 and Factor D blockade showed promising lesion growth reduction in early‐phase studies.  

Previous Use  
• Foundational publications  
  – Zhou et al. (Hum Mol Genet 2009): Demonstrated 10–20% NHS induces MAC deposition and sublytic injury in primary human RPE; established immunostaining for C5b-9 as a quantitative readout.  
  – Anderson et al. (Invest Ophthalmol Vis Sci 2010): Extended this to iPSC-derived RPE, validated LDH release and PI uptake as functional cytotoxicity measures; correlated complement‐mediated injury with expression levels of CD46 and CD59.  

• Application in compound screening  
  – Several academic and industry groups have used similar RPE-serum assays to rank complement inhibitors by potency in blocking MAC deposition and preventing RPE death.  
  – Key insights include identification of small-molecule C3 convertase inhibitors and monoclonal antibodies that protect RPE from complement‐mediated lysis in vitro.  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
  1. Disease relevance: Directly models a core pathogenic mechanism—complement-mediated RPE injury—validated by genetics, histology, and clinical biomarkers.  
  2. Human-cell based: Uses human iPSC-RPE or primary RPE, bypassing species differences in complement regulation.  
  3. Quantitative, multiplexable: Endpoints (C5b-9 staining, LDH release, PI uptake) are amenable to plate-based assays and high-content imaging.  
  4. Flexibility: Can be used to profile inhibitors targeting different nodes (C3, Factor D, C5 convertase, MAC assembly) by varying serum source, complement‐deficient sera, or reconstitution experiments.  

Weaknesses  
  1. Serum variability: Donor-to-donor differences in complement activity and regulatory protein levels may affect assay robustness; batch standardization is required.  
  2. Simplified microenvironment: Lacks Bruch’s membrane, choroidal endothelial cells, photoreceptors, and drusen components that modulate complement activation in vivo.  
  3. Acute vs. chronic modeling: The short-term complement challenge may not capture slow, sublytic, pro-inflammatory effects leading to chronic GA progression.  
  4. Polarization and maturity: iPSC-RPE can exhibit immature phenotypes; differences in complement regulator expression may impact sensitivity.  

Recommendations  
  – Standardize NHS pools or use reconstituted defined complement mixes to reduce variability.  
  – Validate RPE monolayer maturity (tight junctions, polarization markers) before assay.  
  – Consider a co-culture or transwell format with choroidal endothelial cells or drusen‐matrix components to better recapitulate tissue architecture.  
  – Incorporate longitudinal, sublytic readouts (e.g., cytokine secretion, mitochondrial function, inflammasome activation) to capture non‐lytic complement effects.  

Conclusion  
The complement-mediated cytotoxicity assay provides a physiologically pertinent, human-cell–based platform to evaluate candidate therapeutics targeting a genetically and histologically validated driver of dry AMD. While it excels at rapidly screening complement inhibitors for their ability to prevent MAC assembly and RPE death, care must be taken to control serum variability, ensure RPE maturity, and, where possible, augment the assay with complementary models that address chronic, sublytic, and tissue‐architectural aspects of geographic atrophy.
