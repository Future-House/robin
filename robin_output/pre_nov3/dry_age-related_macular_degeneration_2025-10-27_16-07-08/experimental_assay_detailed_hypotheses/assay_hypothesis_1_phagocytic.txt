Assay Overview  
• Biological question modeled  
 – In healthy retina, RPE cells daily phagocytose and degrade spent photoreceptor outer segments (POS). In dry AMD, accumulating evidence (autopsy, imaging, genetic models) points to progressive failure of this RPE “house‐keeping” function, leading to lipofuscin build-up, oxidative damage, drusen formation and eventual RPE atrophy.  
• Assay design  
 – Cell type: human iPSC-derived RPE monolayers (either control or patient-derived lines, 6–8 weeks post-differentiation, validated by ZO-1, RPE65, bestrophin expression).  
 – Challenge reagent: purified bovine/porcine POS labeled with pHrodo™—a pH-sensitive dye that is essentially nonfluorescent at neutral pH (extracellular) but becomes fluorescent in the acidic lumen of late phagosomes/lysosomes.  
 – Readout: live‐cell, high-content time-lapse imaging (or end-point plate reader) of pHrodo fluorescence. Kinetic curves (fluorescence vs. time) report on composite steps of POS binding, internalization and phagosome acidification (“phagocytic flux”). Endpoint metrics can include total fluorescent area per cell, average fluorescence intensity per phagosome, and time to half-maximal fluorescence.  
• Equipment and throughput  
 – 96- or 384-well plates coated for RPE polarity; automated confocal imager. Assay duration ~6–24 h post-POS addition; amenable to small‐molecule screening at ~100–1,000 compounds per run.  

Biomedical Evidence  
• Centrality of RPE phagocytosis in dry AMD pathogenesis  
 – Histopathology and in vivo imaging: early RPE dysfunction and lipofuscin accumulation precede clinical geographic atrophy (Curcio et al., Prog Retin Eye Res 2009).  
 – Genetic and animal models: MERTK-deficient animals accumulate outer segment debris, develop RPE atrophy; variants in phagocytosis-related genes (e.g., MERTK, ITGB5, MFGE8) have been linked to retinal degenerations with features overlapping AMD (D'Cruz et al., Nature 2000; Finnemann et al., J Cell Biol 1997).  
 – Biochemical studies: impaired lysosomal acidification exacerbates A2E/lipofuscin toxicity (Sparrow et al., Adv Exp Med Biol 2003). Oxidized POS further slow clearance and trigger inflammasome activation in RPE (Tseng et al., Nat Commun 2013).  
• Clinical correlations  
 – Reduced expression of phagocytosis receptors (e.g. MERTK) and decreased autophagy markers reported in RPE from donor AMD eyes versus age-matched controls (Mitter et al., Autophagy 2014).  
 – OCT and fundus autofluorescence studies in early dry AMD patients show areas of hyperautofluorescence corresponding to lipofuscin-rich RPE that later progress to atrophy.  

Previous Use  
• Foundational publications  
 – Mazzoni et al., J Neurosci 2011: first demonstrated that human iPSC-RPE can internalize FITC-labeled POS and degrade rhodopsin in vitro, recapitulating key RPE functions.  
 – Young et al., Stem Cell Reports 2017: used patient-derived iPSC-RPE from AMD donors to show significant deficits in POS uptake and processing relative to gene-edited isogenic controls.  
• Drug discovery/screening reports  
 – While no clinical candidates have yet emerged directly from this assay, several groups have used high-content POS uptake assays in ARPE-19 or primary RPE to identify small-molecule enhancers of lysosomal function (e.g. TFEB activators) that accelerate acidification and reduce lipofuscin‐like autofluorescence (Yang et al., Mol Pharmacol 2019; Kim et al., Autophagy 2020).  
• Key insights gained  
 – Demonstrated that boosting lysosomal acidification alone restores phagocytic flux in “diseased” iPSC-RPE (Young et al.).  
 – Provided evidence that patient genetic background can be linked to measurable deficits in a core RPE function, validating the assay’s disease relevance.  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
 1. Disease relevance: directly models a fundamental RPE function whose failure is well documented in dry AMD pathology.  
 2. Human-cell based: avoids species differences in phagocytosis machinery; iPSC platform allows comparison of healthy vs. patient backgrounds or isogenic gene corrections.  
 3. Quantitative, kinetic: provides mechanistic resolution (uptake vs. acidification) and high-content image metrics for robust screening and dose–response.  
 4. Throughput: adaptable to medium‐throughput screening of small‐molecule libraries with 96/384‐well formats and automation.  
Weaknesses  
 1. Limited scope: focuses on RPE–POS axis but does not address complement dysregulation, chronic inflammation, or drusen formation, all of which also drive dry AMD progression.  
 2. Cell variability: iPSC differentiation protocols yield variable RPE maturity; batch‐to‐batch reproducibility requires rigorous QC.  
 3. POS source and labeling: isolated animal POS can vary in composition and purity; pHrodo labeling efficiency must be tightly controlled to avoid assay drift.  
 4. Absence of multi-cell interactions: no contributions from Bruch’s membrane changes, choroidal endothelial cells, or microglia, which also participate in disease pathogenesis.  

Conclusion  
The phagocytic flux assay in iPSC-derived RPE represents a well‐validated, physiologically pertinent platform to identify compounds that restore or enhance RPE clearance of POS—a defect mechanistically upstream of lipofuscin buildup and RPE atrophy in dry AMD. As a focused, medium‐throughput in vitro assay, it is ideally suited for early target validation and lead optimization of modulators of RPE endolysosomal function. Downstream assays or co-culture systems will be needed, however, to capture AMD’s full complexity (inflammation, complement, extracellular matrix changes) before clinical translation.
