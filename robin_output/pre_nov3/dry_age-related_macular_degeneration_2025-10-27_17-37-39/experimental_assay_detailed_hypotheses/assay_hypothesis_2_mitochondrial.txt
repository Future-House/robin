Assay Overview:  
This assay models one of the earliest and most reproducible cellular phenotypes observed in dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD)—retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) mitochondrial dysfunction under oxidative challenge. Human RPE (ARPE-19 or, preferably, patient-derived iPSC‐RPE) are plated in a standard 96-well Seahorse XF microplate. After cells reach confluence and exhibit characteristic cobblestone morphology (in the case of ARPE-19, following extended low-glucose, serum-reduced differentiation for ≥4 weeks; in the case of iPSC‐RPE, after full polarization and pigmentation by ~6–8 weeks), wells are treated with sublethal oxidative stress (e.g., 200–400 µM H2O2 for 1–2 h or blue-light exposure at 450–480 nm for 30–60 min).  
Measurements:  
• Oxygen Consumption Rate (OCR): basal respiration, ATP-linked respiration, proton leak, maximal respiration (following FCCP), spare respiratory capacity and non-mitochondrial respiration (following rotenone/antimycin A).  
• Extracellular Acidification Rate (ECAR): glycolytic rate and glycolytic reserve (via sequential injection of glucose, oligomycin, and 2-deoxyglucose).  
• Mitochondrial Membrane Potential: dyes such as TMRE or JC-1 quantified in parallel by high-content imaging or plate reader.  
• Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS): mitochondrial superoxide via MitoSOX Red, total cellular ROS via DCFDA.  
• ATP Content: luminescent or fluorescence assays to confirm changes in cellular energy charge.  
This multiplexed metabolic and redox profiling yields functional endpoints—ATP production rate, spare respiratory capacity, proton leak, ROS generation—allowing rapid triage of putative antioxidant, mitophagy-inducing or bioenergetic-enhancing small molecules or biologics.

Biomedical Evidence:  
1. Centrality of mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress in dry AMD pathogenesis is well established. In human donor RPE from AMD eyes, mitochondrial DNA damage, ultrastructural abnormalities (swollen cristae, reduced number) and reduced respiratory chain complex I and IV activities are observed (Feher et al., 2006; Kaarniranta et al., 2019).  
2. Oxidative stress induces RPE senescence, inflammasome activation, and complement dysregulation—key drivers of drusen formation and geographic atrophy (Jarrett & Boulton, 2012; Tarallo et al., 2012).  
3. iPSC-derived RPE from AMD patients display reduced basal and maximal OCR, decreased spare respiratory capacity and increased ROS relative to controls (Golestaneh et al., 2016; Ferrington et al., 2017). These bioenergetic deficits correlate with impaired phagocytosis, lysosomal dysfunction and increased secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines.  
4. Clinically, early AMD patients exhibit subtle functional deficits in dark adaptation and multifocal ERG that may reflect subclinical RPE energetic failure (Owsley et al., 2000; Wu et al., 2014).  
5. Interventions that bolster mitochondrial function (e.g., Nrf2 activators, NAD+ precursors) rescue RPE survival and function in animal models of dry AMD (Roh et al., 2017; Xiong et al., 2015), underscoring relevance of mitochondrial health as a surrogate for disease modification.

Previous Use:  
• Seahorse XF profiling of ARPE-19 under oxidative stress has been used to screen antioxidants (e.g., resveratrol, mitochondrial-targeted coenzyme Q analogs) and to characterize mechanistic pathways of RPE injury (Beharry et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2014).  
• iPSC-RPE bioenergetic profiling revealed that patient-derived lines bearing high-risk AMD genotypes (e.g., CFH Y402H) have compromised mitochondrial respiration prior to overt phenotypes, providing a stratified screening platform (Golestaneh et al., 2016).  
• Studies of mitophagy enhancers (e.g., urolithin A) used Seahorse assays alongside live-cell imaging of Parkin translocation to demonstrate enhanced clearance of damaged mitochondria in RPE (Xiao et al., 2018).  
• Key discoveries include demonstration that complement activation fragments (C3a, C5a) directly impair mitochondrial respiration in RPE, suggesting a feed-forward loop in AMD (Lakkaraju et al., 2016).

Overall Evaluation:  
Strengths  
• Disease relevance: directly measures the most robust early cellular defect in dry AMD RPE.  
• Quantitative, multiplexable: Seahorse plus membrane potential, ROS and ATP readouts provide a comprehensive energy/redox phenotype.  
• Predictive capacity: bioenergetic endpoints correlate with downstream RPE functions (phagocytosis, barrier integrity, cytokine profile).  
• Throughput: 96-well format enables moderate‐throughput screening and dose–response assessments.  
• Flexibility: can compare ARPE-19 with patient-derived iPSC-RPE, or RPE from gene-edited isogenic lines, enabling genetic target validation.

Weaknesses  
• ARPE-19 differentiation state: widely used but may not fully recapitulate polarized, pigmented RPE; requires long differentiation protocols or use of primary/iPSC-RPE for most disease-relevant data.  
• Oxidative insult model: H2O2 or blue-light exposures are acute stressors that may not perfectly mimic chronic, cumulative oxidative damage in vivo.  
• Lacks in vivo microenvironment: no Bruch’s membrane, choriocapillaris, photoreceptor interactions or immune cell components, all of which contribute to AMD progression.  
• Potential artifacts: high-concentration oxidative insults can cause rapid necrosis unrelated to mitochondrial pathways; careful titration is essential.  
• Cost and expertise: Seahorse instrumentation and multiparametric readouts require specialized equipment and expertise in assay development and data deconvolution.

Conclusion  
The mitochondrial health assay described provides a robust, mechanistically grounded platform to triage candidate therapeutics targeting early RPE dysfunction in dry AMD. When executed with appropriately differentiated RPE (ideally patient-derived iPSC lines), carefully titrated oxidative stress and complementary functional assays (phagocytosis, cytokine profiling), it can accelerate identification of agents that restore RPE bioenergetic resilience and slow progression toward geographic atrophy.
