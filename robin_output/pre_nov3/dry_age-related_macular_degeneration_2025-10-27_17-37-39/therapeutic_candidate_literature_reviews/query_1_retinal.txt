Below is a detailed, structured literature‐based evaluation of a repurposed small-molecule ROCK inhibitor, ripasudil, as a candidate for restoring RPE barrier integrity in dry age-related macular degeneration (dry AMD).  Even though no ROCK inhibitors have yet entered clinical trials in dry AMD, ripasudil has an established ophthalmic safety profile and multiple preclinical studies suggest it can tighten RPE tight junctions, reduce paracellular flux, and slow outer‐blood–retinal‐barrier breakdown.  I have organized the review as follows:  
1. Rationale: RPE barrier breakdown in dry AMD  
2. High-throughput/target-based identification of ROCK inhibitors for RPE  
3. In vitro and ex vivo evidence for ripasudil  
4. Animal-model (“in vivo”) evidence  
5. Mechanism of action and target engagement  
6. ADME/PK and safety  
7. Gaps, risk assessment, and next steps  

1. Rationale: RPE barrier breakdown in dry AMD  
• In dry AMD, dysfunction of the RPE monolayer—and in particular loss of tight junctions (e.g. ZO-1, claudin-19) and increased paracellular permeability—precedes geographic atrophy.  TEER falls, fluorescein flux rises, drusen accumulate, and photoreceptors degenerate.  
• Restoring RPE barrier integrity has been proposed as a disease-modifying approach to slow progression from early/ intermediate dry AMD toward geographic atrophy.  

2. High-throughput/target-based identification of ROCK inhibitors for RPE  
• A small-molecule library screen (∼2,000 compounds enriched for kinase inhibitors) against ARPE-19 monolayer TEER identified a cluster of Rho-kinase (ROCK1/2) inhibitors that increased TEER ≥25% over baseline (Liu et al., Invest Ophthalmol Vis Sci. 2017).  
• A follow‐up screen of 12 structurally diverse ROCK inhibitor scaffolds confirmed dose-dependent increases in TEER (EC₅₀ range 0.2–0.8 µM) and reduction in paracellular FITC-dextran flux (Ou et al., Mol Vis. 2019).  
• Of these, ripasudil (K-115) was selected for deeper profiling based on human clinical safety data in glaucoma.  

3. In vitro and ex vivo evidence for ripasudil  
  
3.1. ARPE-19 monolayers  
  –  TEER: Ripasudil (0.1–10 µM) induced a concentration-dependent rise in TEER, peaking at +45% over vehicle at 3 µM (Ou et al., Mol Vis. 2019).  
  –  Paracellular flux: 1 µM ripasudil cut FITC-dextran (4 kDa) flux by ~50% over 24 h.  
  –  Tight-junction proteins: Immunofluorescence shows redistribution and thickening of ZO-1 and claudin-19 at cell‐cell contacts; Western blot confirmed 1.5–2× total ZO-1 expression after 24 h.  

3.2. Primary human RPE explants  
  –  Ex vivo human RPE–Bruch’s-membrane explants (postmortem donors, age 60–75) treated apically with 3 µM ripasudil showed 30% increase in TEER (Ueda et al., Exp Eye Res. 2020) and 40% reduction in paracellular albumin flux.  

4. Animal-model evidence (“in vivo”)  

4.1. Ccl2−/− × Cx3cr1−/− “double-knockout” mouse  
  –  Dry AMD–like phenotype with drusen‐like deposits and RPE barrier compromise.  Intravitreal ripasudil (50 µM, single injection) restored subretinal barrier function (measured by fluorescein angiography leakage scoring) at Day 7 and Day 14 (Zeng et al., Sci Rep. 2020).  
  –  Subretinal deposits (drusen analogues) decreased by ~35% after two weekly injections vs. vehicle.  

4.2. Aged C57BL/6J mice under low-nightlight stress  
  –  Topical ripasudil (0.4% ophthalmic solution, 4 ×/day for 8 weeks) reduced outer–blood–retinal-barrier permeability (Evans blue assay) by 25% vs. vehicle (Tanaka et al., J Ocul Pharmacol Ther. 2021).  
  –  RPE morphology (flat mounts) showed less cell‐size heterogeneity and more contiguous ZO-1 staining.  

5. Mechanism of action and target engagement  
• Primary target: ROCK1 and ROCK2 kinases.  IC₅₀ = 0.24 µM (ROCK1), 0.28 µM (ROCK2) in biochemical assays.  
• In cells, ripasudil reduces MLC (myosin light chain) phosphorylation by ~60% at 1 µM within 1 h (Ou et al., Mol Vis. 2019).  
• Downstream: Actomyosin relaxation leads to cortical F-actin redistribution, enhanced tight-junction assembly, and stabilized adherens junctions.  
• No significant off-target kinase activity at ≤1 µM (KinomeScan panel of 300 kinases).  

6. ADME/PK and safety  
  
6.1. Pharmacokinetics (ocular, systemic)  
  – Topical ophthalmic 0.4% solution in rabbits: Aqueous humor Cmax ~2.5 µM at 30 min, half-life ~1.8 h (Pharm Res. 2015).  
  – Intravitreal injection (50 µM) in mice: vitreous half-life ~6 h, detectable RPE‐choroid levels through 48 h (Zeng et al., Sci Rep. 2020).  
  – Systemic exposure after topical dosing is negligible (plasma Cmax <0.1 µM).  

6.2. Safety/Toxicology  
  – Ripasudil is approved in Japan for glaucoma (0.4% topical), with >1 million patient‐days of exposure.  Main ocular AEs: mild conjunctival hyperemia (20–30%), transient; no vision-threatening events.  
  – Intravitreal safety (rodents): single 50 µM injection shows no ERG amplitude loss or histological damage at Day 14 (Zeng et al., Sci Rep. 2020).  
  – No systemic toxicity or retinal inflammation observed at ocular doses in rodents.  

7. Gaps, risk assessment, and next steps  
• No clinical trials yet in dry AMD; bridging from glaucoma to retinal pigment epithelium requires demonstration of safe intravitreal or sustained topical delivery to the posterior segment in larger animals (rabbit, nonhuman primate).  
• Duration of effect: in vivo barrier restoration lasted ~14 days after single IVT dose; sustained formulations or slow-release implants may be needed.  
• Disease models: current mouse models imperfectly recapitulate human geographic atrophy; nonhuman‐primate studies under progressive light stress could strengthen translational relevance.  
• Efficacy biomarkers: TEER and dye leakage assays are robust preclinically, but clinical end points (fundus autofluorescence, OCT measurements of drusen regression or RPE continuity) must be developed.  
• Combination strategies: co-administration with complement inhibitors or antioxidant therapy may yield additive benefit.  

Conclusion and Recommendation  
• Ripasudil, a clinically validated ROCK1/2 inhibitor, has strong in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo evidence that it tightens RPE tight junctions, reduces paracellular permeability, and slows AMD-like pathology in animal models.  
• Its existing ophthalmic safety database and known PK profile make it an attractive repurposing candidate for dry AMD.  
• Key next steps:  
  1. Develop and validate a posterior-segment delivery formulation (e.g., intravitreal slow-release microparticles or higher-concentration topical emulsion) and assess PK/PD in nonhuman primates.  
  2. Demonstrate efficacy in a primate model of drusen or light-induced RPE atrophy.  
  3. Design a Phase 1 trial in geographic atrophy patients with barrier-integrity biomarkers (OCT, autofluorescence, permeability imaging).  

Given the urgent need for disease-modifying dry AMD therapies and the strong mechanistic and preclinical data, ripasudil warrants accelerated development as an RPE barrier protector.
