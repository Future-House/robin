Below is a structured, mechanism‐focused review of whether a cell‐based assay built around retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) culture would meaningfully capture key pathogenic features of dry age‐related macular degeneration (dry AMD, including geographic atrophy) and thus serve as a useful platform for early‐stage therapeutic screening.  

1. Pathobiological Rationale  
  1.1. Central Role of RPE Dysfunction  
   • RPE cells provide trophic support to photoreceptors, handle outer segment phagocytosis, maintain the blood–retina barrier and regulate local immune responses. In dry AMD, chronic stressors overwhelm RPE homeostasis, leading to cell death and secondary photoreceptor loss.  
  1.2. Key Molecular Drivers in Dry AMD  
   – Oxidative stress: high metabolic rate, constant light exposure and phagocytosis of polyunsaturated outer segment lipids generate reactive oxygen species (ROS).  
   – Mitochondrial dysfunction: AMD‐associated RPE show mitochondrial DNA damage, loss of membrane potential and impaired biogenesis.  
   – Lipofuscin/Bisretinoid accumulation: incomplete degradation of outer segments leads to fluorescent aggregates (A2E) that sensitize cells to blue‐light–mediated phototoxicity.  
   – Complement activation: drusen deposits beneath RPE are rich in complement factors (C3a, C5b-9), fueling chronic, low‐grade inflammation.  
   – Inflammasome signaling: NLRP3 inflammasome activation has been detected in RPE from AMD donors, driving IL-1β and IL-18 release.  
   – Impaired autophagy: age and stress decrease autophagic flux, exacerbating accumulation of damaged proteins and organelles.  
   – Lipid metabolism dysregulation & extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling: altered processing of phospholipids and Bruch’s membrane thickening contribute to drusen formation and impaired nutrient/waste exchange.  

2. In Vitro RPE Models in Dry AMD Research  
  2.1. Immortalized RPE Lines (e.g., ARPE-19)  
   • Pros: easy to expand, amenable to high‐throughput.  
   • Cons: reduced pigmentation, variable expression of key RPE markers (RPE65, BEST1), lower phagocytic capacity and stress‐response fidelity compared with native RPE.  
  2.2. Primary Human RPE Cultures  
   • Pros: closer to in vivo physiology, retain native morphology and gene expression.  
   • Cons: donor variability, limited proliferation, higher cost.  
  2.3. iPSC‐Derived RPE  
   • Pros: patient‐specific genetic background (including AMD risk‐alleles in CFH, ARMS2), robust pigmentation, polarized monolayers, realistic phagocytosis rates.  
   • Cons: differentiation protocols are time‐consuming and can be variable across lines; costlier than immortalized lines.  
  2.4. 3D Organoids & Co‐culture Systems  
   • Incorporate photoreceptors, choroidal endothelial cells, microglia for cell–cell interaction studies.  
   • Still in early development for dry AMD—greater complexity but lower throughput.  

3. Assay Readouts Aligned to Dry AMD Pathways  
  3.1. Oxidative Stress & Mitochondrial Health  
   • ROS quantification (DCFDA, CellROX) under basal and induced (e.g., H₂O₂ or blue‐light) conditions.  
   • Mitochondrial membrane potential (TMRE, JC-1), ATP production assays, Seahorse XF analysis of oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR).  
  3.2. Lipofuscin/Bisretinoid Accumulation  
   • Autofluorescence imaging (488 nm excitation; 580–620 nm emission) to quantify A2E‐like pigments.  
   • HPLC or mass spectrometry for bisretinoid species.  
  3.3. Complement Deposition & Activation  
   • Immunostaining or ELISA for C3b/iC3b and membrane attack complex (C5b-9) on RPE.  
   • Co-culture with serum or purified complement to model sublytic vs. lytic injury.  
  3.4. Inflammasome Activation  
   • Caspase-1 activity assays, IL-1β/IL-18 secretion measured by multiplex immunoassay.  
   • ASC speck formation via high‐content microscopy.  
  3.5. Autophagic Flux  
   • LC3-II/I and p62 levels by western blot or immunofluorescence, in the presence/absence of lysosomal inhibitors (bafilomycin A1).  
   • mCherry-GFP-LC3 reporter systems for dynamic flux measurement.  
  3.6. Lipid Metabolism & ECM Remodeling  
   • Lipid droplet staining (BODIPY), cholesterol assays.  
   • MMP activity (gelatin zymography), ECM deposition markers (fibronectin, collagen IV) by immunostaining or ELISA.  
  3.7. Cell Viability & Morphology  
   • ATP‐based viability (CellTiter-Glo), apoptosis (caspase-3/7 activity, Annexin V/PI) and senescence (β-galactosidase) assays.  
   • Tight‐junction integrity (ZO-1 staining), transepithelial resistance (TEER) measurements in polarized monolayers.  

4. Strengths and Limitations for Drug Screening  
  4.1. Strengths  
   – Multifaceted readouts enable phenotypic screening for modulators of oxidative stress, inflammation, phagocytosis, etc.  
   – iPSC‐RPE platforms allow incorporation of patient risk genotypes for target validation and stratified approaches.  
   – High‐content imaging and automated liquid handling can drive moderate‐ to high‐throughput screening (HTS).  
  4.2. Limitations  
   – 2D monocultures lack choroidal endothelium and immune cells, which modulate complement and inflammatory cascades in vivo.  
   – Some phenotypes (e.g. drusen‐like ECM deposits) take weeks–months to develop in vitro, limiting throughput.  
   – Variability across RPE lines/differentiation batches necessitates rigorous controls and assay standardization.  
   – Translating compound effects on single‐cell RPE to functional visual outcomes remains a major hurdle.  

5. Recommendations for Assay Development  
  • Choose an RPE model that balances throughput with physiological relevance—e.g. iPSC‐RPE for lead validation, ARPE-19 for initial pilot screens.  
  • Layer orthogonal readouts: couple ROS/mitochondrial assays with viability and inflammasome readouts to triage off‐target cytotoxicity vs. specific pathway modulation.  
  • Incorporate sublethal complement challenge or drusen‐mimetic lipoprotein particles to probe complement inhibitors or lipid‐modulating agents.  
  • Leverage high‐content imaging to quantify multiple phenotypes (autophagy, tight‐junction integrity, lipid droplets) in the same well.  
  • Validate hits in more complex co‐culture or organoid models to gauge effects on RPE–photoreceptor and RPE–immune crosstalk.  

6. Conclusion  
A well‐designed RPE cell‐culture assay that integrates oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, lipofuscin accumulation, complement/inflammasome activation and autophagy metrics can recapitulate core features of dry AMD pathogenesis. While no single in vitro model fully captures the multicellular environment of the subretinal space, a tiered screening cascade—beginning with immortalized or iPSC‐derived RPE monolayers equipped with multiplexed phenotypic readouts and progressing to co-culture and organoid formats—provides a pragmatic path for small‐molecule and biologic discovery. Such platforms, combined with genetic risk backgrounds and orthogonal validation assays, should accelerate identification and prioritization of therapeutic candidates for dry AMD.
