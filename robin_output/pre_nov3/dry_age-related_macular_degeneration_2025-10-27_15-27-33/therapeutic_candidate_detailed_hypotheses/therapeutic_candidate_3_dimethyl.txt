Proposal for Dimethyl fumarate (DMF; Sigma-Aldrich, cat. D9774)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Dimethyl fumarate (DMF) is the dimethyl ester of fumaric acid, a naturally occurring Krebs‐cycle intermediate. First synthesized in the 1950s, DMF belongs to the class of fumaric acid esters (FAEs), small electrophilic molecules capable of modifying cysteine thiols on target proteins. FAEs were initially developed for psoriasis (marketed as Fumaderm®) and later repurposed for relapsing–remitting multiple sclerosis (Tecfidera®, 240 mg twice daily). DMF’s favorable oral bioavailability, well‐characterized safety profile, and electrophilic reactivity underpin its interest in redox‐modulating indications beyond dermatology and neurology.

Therapeutic History  
• Psoriasis: FAEs (including DMF) were introduced in Germany in the 1990s to treat moderate‐to‐severe plaque psoriasis, demonstrating efficacy through anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant effects (Mrowietz et al., 2007).  
• Multiple Sclerosis: In 2013 the FDA approved DMF (Tecfidera®) for relapsing–remitting MS based on phase III DEFINE/CONFIRM trials showing reduced annualized relapse rates and MRI lesion burden (Gold et al., 2012).  
• Other Preclinical/Ocular Uses: DMF has been explored in models of optic neuritis and retinal ischemia–reperfusion, where it mitigates neuronal death and preserves retinal function via Nrf2‐dependent pathways (Li et al., 2017). To date, no clinical trials have evaluated DMF in age-related macular degeneration (AMD) or other primary retinal degenerations, though oxidative stress and complement dysregulation are shared pathomechanisms.

Mechanism of Action  
1. Keap1–Nrf2 Activation  
   • DMF is a Michael acceptor that covalently modifies reactive cysteines (notably Cys151, Cys273, Cys288) on Keap1 (Scannevin et al., 2012).  
   • Modification disrupts Keap1’s E3‐ubiquitin ligase adaptor function, stabilizing Nrf2, which accumulates and translocates into the nucleus.  
   • Nuclear Nrf2 binds antioxidant response elements (AREs) in promoters of genes such as HMOX1 (HO-1), NQO1, GCLM/GCLC, and GSR, driving antioxidant and phase II detoxification enzyme expression.  
2. Anti-Inflammatory Effects  
   • Nrf2 induction attenuates NF-κB signaling via upregulated HO-1 and modulation of intracellular redox state (Ahmed et al., 2017).  
   • DMF has been reported to engage the hydroxycarboxylic acid receptor 2 (HCAR2), leading to a shift in microglial/macrophage polarization (Chen et al., 2014).  
3. Inflammasome Suppression  
   • Nrf2 activation limits NLRP3 inflammasome priming by lowering ROS and pro-IL-1β transcription (Zhou et al., 2018).  
4. Complement Modulation  
   • Although indirect, by reducing oxidative damage and inflammasome activity, DMF may lower complement component C3 expression in RPE (Shi et al., 2018).

Expected Effect in Dry AMD–Relevant Assay  
• Oxidative Stress Model (H₂O₂, 200 µM) in Primary Human RPE  
   – Nrf2 nuclear localization ↑ (immunofluorescence), HO-1/NQO1 mRNA >5-fold (qPCR) at 5–20 µM DMF (Shi et al., 2018).  
   – Intracellular ROS ↓ by ~70% (DCFDA assay).  
   – IL-1β secretion ↓ by ~60% (ELISA), cell viability rescued (MTT assay). Knockdown of Nrf2 abrogates protection, confirming target engagement.  
• Inflammasome Priming and Complement Expression  
   – DMF–treated RPE show reduced NLRP3, pro-caspase-1, and mature IL-18 levels (Western blot), and lower C3 and CFB mRNA (qPCR) compared to vehicle (Shi et al., 2018; Barhoumi et al., 2019).  
• In Vivo (SOD1−/− Mouse Model)  
   – Oral DMF (30 mg/kg/day) preserves RPE ultrastructure, reduces drusen-like deposits (TEM), and maintains scotopic ERG amplitudes versus controls (Li et al., 2019).  
   – Ocular PK studies in rodents demonstrate posterior-segment penetration, with vitreous/plasma ratios approaching 0.2–0.3 at steady state (Zhang et al., 2020).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
• Mechanistic alignment: DMF directly addresses two central dry AMD drivers—oxidative stress and inflammasome/complement priming—through well‐characterized Nrf2 activation.  
• Target validation: Robust in vitro data in primary human RPE demonstrate on‐target effects (Nrf2 dependence) for ROS reduction, inflammasome suppression, and improved viability under oxidative challenge.  
• In vivo proof of concept: Efficacy in an oxidative stress–driven murine model (SOD1−/−) with functional and morphological readouts relevant to human dry AMD.  
• Clinical readiness: Decades of human safety data, known dosing regimens, oral administration, and documented posterior-segment bioavailability accelerate translational potential.

Weaknesses and Risks  
• Ocular exposure: Human posterior-segment concentrations following approved dosing (240 mg BID) may be lower than efficacious in vitro concentrations (5–20 µM). Dedicated ocular PK in humans will be critical.  
• Systemic adverse effects: Gastrointestinal intolerance and flushing common in MS patients; elderly AMD patients may have comorbidities impacting tolerability.  
• Off-target immunomodulation: HCAR2-mediated effects on systemic myeloid cells could influence systemic immunity.  
• Disease heterogeneity: Dry AMD involves lipid dysregulation, RPE–Bruch’s membrane interactions, and outer retinal inflammation; DMF may not address all pathogenic axes (e.g., lipofuscin accumulation).

Conclusion  
DMF presents a compelling, mechanism-driven repurposing opportunity for dry AMD. Its dual antioxidant and anti-inflammatory actions via Nrf2 activation directly target established pathogenic pathways in RPE. The combination of robust preclinical efficacy, human safety history, and oral dosing supports rapid progression into proof-of-concept clinical trials. Primary challenges lie in confirming adequate ocular exposure at tolerable systemic doses and ensuring comprehensive coverage of dry AMD’s multifactorial pathology.
