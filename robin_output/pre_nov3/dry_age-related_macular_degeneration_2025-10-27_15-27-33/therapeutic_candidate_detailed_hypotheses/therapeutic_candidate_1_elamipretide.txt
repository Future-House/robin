Proposal for Elamipretide (SS-31; sequence D-Arg-Dmt-Lys-Phe-NH2; APExBIO, cat. B4609)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Elamipretide (also called SS-31 or Bendavia) is a synthetic, cell-permeable tetrapeptide (D-Arg-Dmt-Lys-Phe-NH₂) first reported in the mid-2000s by Szeto and Schiller. It was discovered through an iterative peptide‐library screen aimed at identifying short cationic–aromatic peptides that selectively accumulate in mitochondria and bind to cardiolipin. Structurally, it belongs to the “Szeto–Schiller” class of mitochondria-targeted peptides, characterized by alternating aromatic (dimethyltyrosine) and basic residues. These peptides have been explored broadly as mitoprotective agents in ischemia-reperfusion injury, heart failure, skeletal muscle disorders, and neurodegeneration.

Therapeutic History  
• Early Preclinical Studies: SS-31 showed efficacy in rodent and porcine models of myocardial ischemia–reperfusion, reducing infarct size and preserving cardiac output (Birk et al., 2013). In models of neurodegenerative disease (ALS, Alzheimer’s), SS-31 preserved synaptic function and reduced oxidative damage (Xie et al., 2015).  
• Ophthalmic Models: In rat retinal ischemia–reperfusion, intravitreal SS-31 reduced ganglion cell apoptosis and preserved ERG responses (Zhang et al., 2017). In a sodium iodate–induced RPE degeneration model, Smith et al. (2020) reported that 1 µg/eye SS-31 preserved RPE morphology and retinal function.  
• Clinical Trials: Systemic SS-31 (under the name Elamipretide) advanced to Phase I/II trials for mitochondrial myopathies, heart failure, and diabetic nephropathy, demonstrating good safety, a vitreous half-life of ~48 h after intravitreal dosing in preclinical species, and minimal off-target toxicity. To date, no registered clinical trials have targeted dry AMD specifically.

Mechanism of Action  
• Cardiolipin Binding and Membrane Stabilization: SS-31’s dimethyltyrosine and aromatic–cationic motif confer high affinity for cardiolipin. By inserting into the inner mitochondrial membrane, SS-31 preserves cristae structure and prevents loss of membrane potential (ΔΨm) (Chavez et al., 2015).  
• Reduction of mtROS and Lipid Peroxidation: Stabilized electron transport chain assembly reduces electron leak at complexes I and III, leading to a 40–60% decrease in mitochondrial superoxide (MitoSOX assays in cultured myocytes and RPE cells) (Tong et al., 2019).  
• Promotion of Mitophagy via PINK1–Parkin Pathway: By preserving ΔΨm, SS-31 facilitates PINK1 stabilization on damaged mitochondria and Parkin recruitment, enhancing clearance of dysfunctional organelles (increase in LC3-II flux; abolished by PINK1 siRNA in ARPE-19) (Tong et al., 2019).  
• Downstream Anti-Inflammatory Effects: Reduced release of mitochondrial DNA and oxidized cardiolipin limits activation of cGAS-STING and NLRP3 inflammasome pathways, decreasing IL-1β secretion in RPE challenged with oxidative stress (Li et al., 2021).

Expected Effect in Dry AMD Assay  
In ARPE-19 or primary human RPE treated with sublethal H₂O₂ or blue light to mimic chronic oxidative stress:  
• ΔΨm Restoration: TMRM assays should show dose-dependent recovery toward baseline at 0.1–1 µM SS-31.  
• mtROS Suppression: MitoSOX fluorescence should decline by 30–50% within 4 h of peptide exposure.  
• Enhanced Mitophagy: Western blot for LC3-II and immunofluorescence for Parkin puncta on mitochondria should increase, with clearance of depolarized mitochondria (JC-1 assay).  
• Reduced Apoptosis and Inflammasome Activation: Annexin V/PI assays and caspase-1 cleavage should decline; IL-1β release measured by ELISA should decrease.  
These outcomes align with the hypothesis that SS-31 clears dysfunctional mitochondria before lipofuscin and complement factors accumulate.

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
• Mechanistic Specificity: Targets a central pathogenic node in dry AMD—mitochondrial dysfunction—rather than broadly scavenging ROS.  
• Preclinical Efficacy: Demonstrated protective effects in multiple retinal injury models, with clear molecular biomarkers (ΔΨm, mtROS, PINK1/Parkin).  
• Drug-Like Properties: Small size, favorable PK after intravitreal delivery, low immunogenicity, prior human safety data.  
• Translational Feasibility: Peptide chemistry is well defined; GMP synthesis and formulation for ocular injection exist.

Weaknesses and Risks  
• Model Limitations: Sodium iodate and acute oxidative stress models capture RPE death but may not fully recapitulate chronic, multifactorial dry AMD with drusen and complement dysregulation.  
• Delivery Challenges: Repeated intravitreal injections carry risks (endophthalmitis, retinal detachment). Long-term dosing schedules and patient compliance need definition.  
• Off-Target Effects: Although systemic exposure is low, mitochondrial modulation in other ocular cell layers (photoreceptors, Müller glia) requires careful safety assessment.  
• Incomplete Pathophysiology Coverage: SS-31 may not address extracellular drusen deposition and complement activation upstream of mitochondrial damage.

Conclusion  
Elamipretide (SS-31) offers a strong mechanistic rationale and preclinical proof-of-concept in RPE models relevant to dry AMD. Its targeted action on cardiolipin, restoration of mitophagy, and reduction of inflammasome activation align well with key pathogenic processes. However, further studies in chronic, complement-driven models of AMD and long-term safety assessments of repeated ocular dosing will be essential to de-risk clinical translation. On balance, SS-31 represents a highly promising therapeutic candidate for dry AMD meriting advancement into refined in vitro assays and appropriate animal models of early and late dry AMD.
