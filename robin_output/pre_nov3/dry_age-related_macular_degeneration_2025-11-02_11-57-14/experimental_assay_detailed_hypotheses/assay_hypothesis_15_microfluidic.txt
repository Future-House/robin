Assay Overview  
The proposed assay is a microfluidic “RPE-on-a-chip” perfusion platform that models key features of dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) at the level of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)–Bruch’s membrane interface.  
• Disease aspect modeled: chronic RPE barrier dysfunction, altered trans-epithelial transport across Bruch’s membrane, and low-grade inflammation driven by RPE stress. These processes underlie drusen deposition, RPE atrophy, and geographic atrophy in dry AMD.  
• Measurements:  
  – Transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) in real time to quantify barrier integrity under flow.  
  – Molecular permeability assays (e.g., fluorescent dextrans or small tracer molecules) to assess Bruch’s-membrane-like barrier function.  
  – Flow-mediated shear stress effects on RPE morphology and survival via live/dead staining and high-content imaging.  
  – Basal and apical cytokine and complement factor secretion (e.g., IL-6, IL-8, complement C3a) by multiplex immunoassay of perfusates.  
  – Oxidative-stress readouts (ROS generation via DCFDA) and lipofuscin autofluorescence over prolonged culture.  
• Cell types and materials:  
  – Human iPSC-derived RPE monolayers differentiated to a polarized phenotype on a synthetic or decellularized Bruch’s-like extracellular matrix.  
  – Microfluidic chips composed of optically clear polymers (e.g., PDMS) with integrated electrodes for TEER.  
  – Optionally co-culture with choroidal endothelial cells in a parallel channel to model cross-talk.  

Biomedical Evidence  
1. Centrality of RPE dysfunction in dry AMD: Pathologic studies show that early dry AMD is characterized by RPE barrier breakdown, accumulation of sub-RPE deposits (drusen), and local inflammation (Lopez et al., Invest. Ophth. Vis. Sci. 1995; Strauss, Prog Retin Eye Res. 2005). Loss of RPE pump and barrier functions leads to photoreceptor starvation and geographic atrophy, the primary driver of vision loss in dry AMD (Curcio et al., Prog Retin Eye Res. 2011).  
2. Role of shear and fluid dynamics at Bruch’s membrane: Although ocular shear stresses are low compared with vascular beds, fluid flow across Bruch’s influences nutrient transport and waste clearance. Aging thickening of Bruch’s impedes this exchange; a dynamic perfusion model can recapitulate early transport deficits (Fisher & Ferrington, Exp. Eye Res. 2018).  
3. Inflammation and complement activation: Clinical samples from dry AMD patients show elevated local levels of complement components (C3a, C5a) and cytokines (IL-6, IL-8) in the RPE–choroid complex (Johnson et al., JCI Insight 2019). Measuring perfusate cytokines under stress in vitro provides a direct functional correlate.  

Previous Use  
• Musah et al. (Lab Chip 2017) first described a hiPSC-derived RPE-on-a-chip under laminar flow. They demonstrated physiologic TEER levels (~200 Ω·cm2), polarization marker expression (RPE65, CRALBP), and drug permeability correlating with in vivo data.  
• Achberger et al. (Nat. Commun. 2019) expanded this concept into a multilayer “retina-on-a-chip,” integrating RPE, photoreceptors, and microglia to model diabetic retinopathy and test anti-VEGF agents; they showed dynamic cytokine responses to oxidative stress.  
• Other microfluidic RPE models have been used to study complement-mediated cytotoxicity (Kim et al., Lab Chip 2020) and to screen antioxidants targeting lipofuscin accumulation (Wang et al., Front. Cell Dev. Biol. 2021).  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths:  
– Physiologic relevance: flow recreates nutrient/waste exchange across Bruch’s-like barrier, more closely modeling in vivo microenvironment than static Transwell cultures.  
– Real-time functional readouts (TEER, permeability, cytokine secretion) enable kinetic assessment of drug action and toxicity.  
– Amenable to human iPSC-derived RPE, capturing patient-specific genetics and disease phenotypes (e.g., CFH polymorphisms).  
– Modular design allows incorporation of choroidal endothelial cells or microglia for multicellular interactions.  

Weaknesses:  
– Throughput: microfluidic setup is lower throughput than 96-well plate assays, limiting large-scale screening.  
– Absence of photoreceptors in simple RPE-only designs; full disease modeling may require more complex co-cultures.  
– Technical complexity and cost: requires expertise in microfabrication, fluidics, and live-cell electrophysiology.  
– Limited recapitulation of drusen biogenesis: no long-term ECM remodeling or lipid deposition unless supplemented with damaged BM components.  

Conclusion  
A microfluidic RPE-on-a-chip perfusion assay offers a highly physiologic, dynamic platform to interrogate RPE barrier integrity, transport, and inflammatory responses central to dry AMD. While technically demanding and lower throughput, its ability to generate real-time multiparametric data makes it a valuable tool for mechanism-based target validation and for the testing of candidate therapeutics aimed at preserving or restoring RPE function in dry AMD.
