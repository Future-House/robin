Assay Overview  
• Rationale and disease aspect modeled  
  – Dry AMD is characterized by RPE dysfunction, imbalance of trophic factors, and progressive choriocapillaris atrophy. Healthy RPE secrete VEGF basally to maintain choroidal endothelial cells (CECs) and PEDF apically to support photoreceptors; in dry AMD, this balance shifts, impairing choriocapillaris survival.  
  – The RPE–endothelial co-culture assay on Transwells directly models this paracrine “cross-talk,” barrier integrity, and endothelial survival signals.  
• Assay format and readouts  
  – Primary or iPSC-derived human RPE are grown to confluence on Transwell inserts; human choroidal endothelial cells are seeded on the basolateral side (underside) of the same membrane.  
  – Readouts include:  
    • VEGF and PEDF secretion (ELISA of apical vs. basolateral media)  
    • Trans-epithelial electrical resistance (TEER) as a measure of RPE barrier integrity  
    • Endothelial viability/proliferation (e.g., calcein AM, Ki-67 immunostaining)  
    • Endothelial tube-forming capacity on recovery from conditioned medium  
    • Gene expression of angiogenic, inflammatory, and complement factors (qPCR, RNA-Seq)  
• Biological materials  
  – Human primary or iPSC-derived RPE (validated for pigmentation, TER >200 Ω·cm2)  
  – Human choroidal microvascular endothelial cells (commercial or primary)  
  – Standard ECM coating (collagen IV, laminin) or decellularized Bruch’s membrane  

Biomedical Evidence  
• RPE–Choriocapillaris interdependence  
  – Clinical imaging (OCT angiography) shows choriocapillaris dropout correlates with RPE atrophy (Miller et al., Invest. Ophth. Vis. Sci. 2017).  
  – RPE-derived VEGF is critical for choriocapillaris maintenance; mice with RPE-specific VEGF knockout develop choriocapillaris loss and AMD-like changes (Kurihara et al., J. Clin. Invest. 2012).  
  – PEDF levels decline in aged/dry AMD eyes, tilting the pro- vs. anti-angiogenic balance (Anand-Apte et al., Exp. Eye Res. 1998).  
• Inflammation and complement  
  – Dysregulated RPE complement factor expression (CFH, C3) contributes to low-level chronic inflammation, further stressing both RPE and endothelium (Anderson et al., Prog. Retin. Eye Res. 2010).  
  – Pro-inflammatory milieu (IL-1β, TNF-α) in dry AMD alters RPE barrier and paracrine signaling (Tseng et al., Front. Immunol. 2019).  
• Oxidative stress  
  – RPE under oxidative challenge secretes altered cytokine profiles that promote endothelial apoptosis (Beatty et al., Surv. Ophthalmol. 2000).  

Overall, multiple lines of biomedical and clinical literature underscore that RPE–CEC cross-talk, through VEGF/PEDF balance, barrier function, and inflammatory signaling, is central to dry AMD pathogenesis.  

Previous Use  
• Drug discovery and mechanistic studies  
  – Shen et al. (Tissue Eng. A 2014) described an RPE–endothelial co-culture to test anti-angiogenic compounds: they demonstrated that certain small-molecule VEGFR inhibitors normalized VEGF/PEDF ratios and preserved endothelial viability under stress.  
  – Tsapakis et al. (J. Oph. Res. 2017) used a similar co-culture to show that carotenoid antioxidants (lutein, zeaxanthin) restored TER and shifted secretion toward PEDF in oxidatively challenged RPE, with secondary benefit to endothelial survival.  
  – Peterson et al. (Sci. Rep. 2019) incorporated inflammatory cytokines to probe how IL-1β modulation by candidate drugs affected RPE barrier and endothelial tube formation.  
• Key insights gained  
  – Quantitative linkage between RPE health and choriocapillaris support via paracrine factors.  
  – Proof of concept that targeting RPE secretome can secondarily rescue endothelial cell integrity.  
  – Demonstration that TEER is a sensitive readout for RPE barrier–centric therapies.  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
1. Disease relevance: Directly models a core dry AMD mechanism—RPE-dependent trophic support of choriocapillaris.  
2. Multiparametric: Combines barrier measurement (TEER), secreted factor quantification, cell viability, and gene expression in one system.  
3. Human cells: iPSC or primary RPE and choroidal ECs increase translational fidelity.  
4. Adaptable: Can add oxidative stressors, complement components, cytokines, or drusen-mimetic ECM to mimic disease milieu.  
5. Moderate throughput: Transwell formats can be scaled in 24- or 96-well plates for screening.  

Weaknesses  
1. Missing complexity: Lacks photoreceptors, Bruch’s membrane ultrastructure, microglia/macrophages, and full complement cascade.  
2. Phenotypic drift: Primary choroidal ECs can lose in vivo–like phenotype over passages; iPSC-derived RPE vary by differentiation protocol.  
3. End‐stage events: Does not model drusen accumulation, geographic atrophy expansion, or photoreceptor death directly.  
4. Standardization: TEER and secretion baselines vary lab to lab; requires rigorous QC of cell identity and barrier tightness.  

Conclusion  
The RPE–endothelial co-culture Transwell assay is a well‐validated, mechanistically focused in vitro platform that recapitulates a central paracrine axis of dry AMD pathogenesis. It is particularly suited to screening agents that restore RPE barrier integrity and normalize VEGF/PEDF balance, thereby preserving choriocapillaris health. To enhance disease fidelity, complement components, drusen-like deposits, or microglial co-culture modules can be incorporated in follow‐up studies.
