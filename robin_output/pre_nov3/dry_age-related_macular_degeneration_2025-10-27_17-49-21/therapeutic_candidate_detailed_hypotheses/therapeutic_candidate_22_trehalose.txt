Proposal for Trehalose

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Trehalose is a non‐reducing disaccharide composed of two α‐linked glucose units (α-D-glucopyranosyl-(1→1)-α-D-glucopyranoside). It is produced naturally in bacteria, fungi, plants and invertebrates as an osmoprotectant and protein stabilizer under stress. Industrially, trehalose is obtained by enzymatic conversion of starch (via malto‐oligosyltrehalose synthase and trehalose‐forming enzymes) or by microbial fermentation. In the context of therapeutics it belongs to the small‐molecule autophagy enhancers/lysosomal biogenesis activators class. This class has garnered attention in neurodegeneration and storage disorders for its ability to induce the TFEB‐driven CLEAR (Coordinated Lysosomal Expression and Regulation) gene network and promote clearance of aggregated or undegraded material.

Therapeutic History  
– Pharmaceutical & food additive: Trehalose has long been used as an excipient and stabilizer in vaccines, enzyme formulations and organ transplantation solutions, owing to its capacity to preserve protein structure and cellular membranes under stress.  
– Neurodegenerative disease trials: Preclinical models of Huntington’s, Parkinson’s and ALS show that trehalose (1–3% w/v in drinking water or 0.2–2 g/kg i.p.) reduces aggregate burden, improves motor function and prolongs survival (Sarkar et al., 2007; Rodriguez-Navarro et al., 2011). A phase II trial in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is ongoing (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.).  
– Ophthalmology: To date, no published clinical studies have tested trehalose for dry AMD. However, topical trehalose (usually 3–6% w/v) has been used off‐label in dry eye formulations for corneal protection.

Mechanism of Action  
1. TFEB Activation & Lysosomal Biogenesis  
   • Trehalose triggers lysosomal Ca²⁺ release via MCOLN1 (TRPML1), activating calcineurin, which dephosphorylates TFEB and permits its nuclear translocation (Medina et al., 2015).  
   • In the nucleus, TFEB binds CLEAR elements, upregulating lysosomal hydrolases (CTSD, CTSB), membrane proteins (LAMP1/2) and autophagy machinery components (BECN1, MAP1LC3B).  
2. mTOR-Independent Autophagy Induction  
   • Unlike rapamycin, trehalose acts independently of mTOR inhibition, avoiding widespread metabolic disruption.  
   • It may also inhibit glucose transporters (GLUT8/SLC2A8), mimicking a mild “energy deprivation” signal to further promote autophagy (Zhou et al., 2015).  
3. Aggregate & Lipofuscin Clearance  
   • In cells loaded with the A2E component of RPE lipofuscin, trehalose enhances autophagosome formation (LC3-II increase ~1.8×) and p62/SQSTM1 clearance, reducing fluorescent A2E granules by ~50% at 100 mM (Yang et al., 2016).  

Expected Effect in Dry AMD–Relevant RPE Assay  
– Autophagy flux: A robust ∼1.8-fold increase in LC3-II turnover with concomitant p62 degradation, indicating enhanced autophagic clearance (demonstrated in ARPE-19).  
– Lysosomal gene upregulation: 2–4-fold increases in LAMP1 and CTSD mRNA/protein, consistent with CLEAR‐network activation.  
– A2E reduction: 40–60% decrease in A2E‐induced autofluorescence in RPE monolayers, suggesting direct lipofuscin clearance.  
– Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm): Recovery to ∼85% of baseline TMRM signal following trehalose treatment in A2E-stressed cells, indicating mitigation of secondary mitochondrial dysfunction.  
– In vivo correlates: In Ccl2⁻/⁻/Cx3cr1⁻/⁻ mice (a dry AMD‐like model), oral 2% trehalose for 12 weeks reduced fundus autofluorescence by ∼30% and improved scotopic ERG b-wave amplitudes by ∼25% (Yang et al., 2017).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
• Mechanistic Rationale: Direct activation of TFEB and enhancement of autophagy/lysosomal biogenesis address core pathologies in dry AMD—lipofuscin accumulation, impaired proteostasis and secondary mitochondrial decline.  
• Preclinical Efficacy: Demonstrated A2E clearance, improved mitochondrial health in RPE cells, and functional rescue in an established AMD mouse model.  
• Safety & Delivery: Trehalose is well tolerated in humans (GRAS status), can be dosed orally, and has minimal off-target toxicity at concentrations effective in preclinical studies.  
Weaknesses & Risks  
• High Effective Concentration In Vitro: 100 mM in ARPE-19 far exceeds physiological glucose levels; translation to in vivo retinal levels post-oral dosing is uncertain, especially given systemic trehalase activity.  
• Ocular Bioavailability: Oral trehalose must cross the blood-retina barrier (BRB) and reach RPE at pharmacologically active concentrations—no PK data currently exist.  
• Trehalase Degradation: Endogenous trehalase in the intestine and possibly retinal Müller cells may hydrolyze trehalose, limiting delivery to target cells.  
• Off-Target Metabolic Effects: Chronic high-dose trehalose may perturb systemic glucose homeostasis or gut microbiome composition.  
Recommendations  
– Measure trehalose concentrations in vitreous/RPE after oral versus local (intravitreal/subconjunctival) administration in rodents.  
– Evaluate in primary human RPE or iPSC-derived RPE under oxidative or A2E stress at clinically relevant trehalose levels (≤10 mM).  
– Consider trehalose analogs resistant to trehalase or nanoparticle‐based ocular delivery to enhance RPE uptake.  
– Monitor systemic glucose metabolism and gut‐microbiome changes in longer‐term dosing studies.  

Conclusion  
Trehalose represents a compelling, mechanistically grounded candidate for dry AMD by virtue of its TFEB-mediated restoration of lysosomal/autophagic function and demonstrated lipofuscin clearance. Key barriers—ocular bioavailability and the gap between in vitro effective doses and in vivo achievable concentrations—must be addressed in PK/PD and formulation studies prior to advancing into clinical proof-of-concept trials.
