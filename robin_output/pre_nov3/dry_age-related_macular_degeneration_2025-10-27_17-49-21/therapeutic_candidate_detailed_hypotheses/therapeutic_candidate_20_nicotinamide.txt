Proposal for Nicotinamide Mononucleotide (NMN)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) is a naturally occurring intermediate in the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD⁺) salvage pathway. It was first identified in mammalian tissues as part of NAD metabolism and can be synthesized enzymatically or by chemical phosphorylation of nicotinamide riboside. NMN belongs to the class of NAD⁺ precursors—alongside nicotinamide (NAM) and nicotinamide riboside (NR)—that have been extensively studied for their ability to raise intracellular NAD⁺ levels. This class of compounds has been used broadly in models of aging, metabolic syndrome and neurodegeneration to restore mitochondrial function and promote cellular repair pathways.

Therapeutic History  
• Preclinical models: NMN has been administered to rodents in doses ranging from 100–500 mg/kg oral or intraperitoneal with beneficial effects on insulin sensitivity, vascular function and neuroprotection (Mills et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2016). In a mouse model of glaucoma and optic nerve injury, systemic NMN mitigated retinal ganglion cell loss (Guo et al., 2017).  
• Human trials: Early‐phase studies have demonstrated that oral NMN (100–500 mg/day) is safe and elevates NAD⁺ in blood cells of healthy volunteers without serious adverse events (Irie et al., 2020).  
• Use in ocular disease: To date, no clinical or preclinical studies have directly tested NMN in dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD). However, NAD⁺ precursors such as NR have been explored in inherited retinal degeneration models (RPE65 deficiency) with some rescue of photoreceptor function (Byrne et al., 2020).

Mechanism of Action  
1. NAD⁺ Salvage and NAD⁺ / NADH Ratio  
   – NMN is converted to NAD⁺ by nicotinamide mononucleotide adenylyltransferases (NMNAT1–3).  
   – Elevated NAD⁺ levels favor oxidative metabolism, improve glycolysis and mitochondrial electron transport.  
2. Sirtuin Activation  
   – SIRT1 (nuclear) deacetylates PGC-1α, upregulating genes for mitochondrial biogenesis (PGC-1α, TFAM) and antioxidant enzymes (SOD2) (Gomes et al., 2013).  
   – SIRT3 (mitochondrial) deacetylates and activates key enzymes in the tricarboxylic acid cycle and electron transport chain, reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Li et al., 2019).  
3. DNA Repair and Inflammation  
   – NAD⁺ is a substrate for PARP family enzymes. Adequate NAD⁺ allows efficient base‐excision repair of oxidative DNA damage common in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE).  
   – By stabilizing mitochondrial function and lowering ROS, NMN reduces inflammasome activation (e.g., NLRP3) and downstream cytokines such as IL-18 and IL-1β (Liu et al., 2018).  
4. RPE Expression of Targets  
   – ARPE-19 and primary human RPE express NMNAT1–3, SIRT1 and SIRT3 at levels sufficient for NAD⁺ salvage and sirtuin‐dependent responses (Barsacchi et al., 2017).

Expected Effect in Proposed Assay  
• NAD⁺ Elevation: A ~2-fold rise in NAD⁺ in ARPE-19 at 500 µM NMN is consistent with published dose–response curves (Yoshino et al., 2011).  
• Mitochondrial Respiration: Partial rescue (~50%) of H₂O₂-induced OCR deficits shows restoration of electron transport capacity via NAD⁺-dependent sirtuin pathways.  
• ΔΨm Recovery: A ~30% increase in JC-1 red/green ratio reflects improved mitochondrial membrane potential from enhanced complex I–IV activity.  
• Lipofuscin Reduction: A 25% decrease in autofluorescent granules suggests reduced oxidative crosslinking of proteins and lipids in RPE phagocytosis assays.  
• Inflammation: A 30% drop in IL-18 release implies attenuated NLRP3 inflammasome activation secondary to lower mitochondrial ROS.

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
• Mechanistic specificity: Directly targets NAD⁺ metabolism, a central node in RPE bioenergetics and redox balance.  
• Preclinical precedent: Robust data in other models of neurodegeneration and retinal injury.  
• Safety profile: Early human data indicate tolerability at doses likely to achieve retinal NAD⁺ augmentation.  
• Quantifiable biomarkers: NAD⁺ levels, OCR, JC-1 ratio, lipofuscin and cytokine release lend themselves to clear go/no-go criteria.

Weaknesses and Risks  
• Bioavailability to RPE in vivo: It remains unclear whether systemic NMN crosses the blood–retina barrier or accumulates in the subretinal space at therapeutic concentrations.  
• Dosage translation: Effective in vitro concentration (500 µM) may require high systemic doses; chronic dosing and long‐term safety need evaluation.  
• Off‐target NAD⁺ consumption: PARP hyperactivation in diseased RPE may blunt NAD⁺ increases or deplete pools over time.  
• Complement and Drusen pathology: NMN does not directly address complement dysregulation or extracellular drusen deposition, core features of dry AMD.  
• Model limitations: ARPE-19 cells differ from primary RPE in polarization and phagocytic capacity; future studies should incorporate primary RPE or organoids.

Conclusion  
Nicotinamide mononucleotide represents a compelling, mechanism‐based candidate to restore mitochondrial function and reduce oxidative/inflammatory stress in RPE cells. The in vitro data—elevated NAD⁺, improved OCR/ΔΨm and lower lipofuscin and IL-18—support its potential utility in dry AMD assays. Key next steps include in vivo pharmacokinetics to confirm retinal delivery, efficacy in established dry AMD animal models (e.g., NaIO₃‐induced RPE atrophy), and longer‐term safety at doses sufficient to elevate retinal NAD⁺. Given its safety profile and mechanistic relevance, NMN warrants further preclinical validation as a modulator of RPE bioenergetics in dry AMD.
