Proposal for Rapamycin

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Rapamycin (sirolimus) is a macrocyclic lactone originally isolated in 1972 from Streptomyces hygroscopicus soil isolates on Easter Island (“Rapa Nui”). Its structure was elucidated by a multidisciplinary effort combining fermentation, solvent extraction, and NMR/mass‐spectrometry (Vezina et al., 1975). Rapamycin belongs to the “rapalog” class of small‐molecule inhibitors of the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1). Rapalogs—e.g., everolimus, temsirolimus—have been deployed clinically for immunosuppression (allograft rejection) and oncology (renal cell carcinoma, mantle‐cell lymphoma) through oral or parenteral routes.

Therapeutic History  
Biochemical and Clinical Uses  
•  Immunosuppression: Approved by FDA in 1999 to prevent kidney transplant rejection; systemic exposure dampens T‐cell proliferation via mTORC1 inhibition (Morris et al., 2005).  
•  Oncology: Everolimus and temsirolimus (rapalog derivatives) used in various cancers; rapamycin itself has seen off‐label trials in hematologic malignancies.  
•  Ophthalmology: Intravitreal sirolimus has been evaluated in small clinical studies for noninfectious uveitis (SAVE trial; Hogan et al., 2014) and for proliferative vitreoretinopathy (PVR) prevention. These studies have demonstrated local tolerability with minimal systemic exposure.  

Use in Dry AMD  
No clinical trials to date have registered rapamycin or rapalogs for dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD). However, preclinical data in murine dry‐AMD models (e.g., Ccl2⁻/⁻/Cx3cr1⁻/⁻ mice) have shown that intravitreal or subretinal rapamycin preserves photoreceptor function (scotopic ERG) and reduces RPE autofluorescent deposits (Levy et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2018), supporting translational relevance.

Mechanism of Action  
Primary Target: mTORC1—serine/threonine kinase complex that integrates nutrient and growth factor signals to suppress autophagy and promote protein/lipid synthesis.  
•  mTORC1 is composed of mTOR, Raptor, mLST8, PRAS40, DEPTOR (Saxton & Sabatini, 2017).  
•  Rapamycin binds FKBP12, and the FKBP12–rapamycin complex allosterically inhibits mTORC1 kinase activity, particularly toward ULK1 (a key autophagy initiator) and S6K1 (a driver of protein synthesis).  

Downstream Effects Relevant to RPE and Dry AMD  
•  Autophagy induction: In cultured human RPE cells loaded with the bisretinoid A2E, rapamycin (10–20 nM) elevates LC3‐II/LC3‐I ratio, decreases p62/SQSTM1 levels, and increases ULK1 phosphorylation at Ser555 (Chen et al., 2015).  
•  Mitophagy: Rapamycin augments PINK1 stabilization on depolarized mitochondria and Parkin recruitment, accelerating mitochondrial turnover (Koh et al., 2012).  
•  Lipofuscin clearance: By both macroautophagy and selective mitophagy, rapamycin reduces A2E‐derived autofluorescent granules in RPE by ~40% (Zhu et al., 2017).  
•  Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm): Rapamycin treatment in stressed RPE restores ΔΨm by ~20–30% (JC‐1 and TMRM assays), consistent with removal of dysfunctional mitochondria (Gong et al., 2018).  
•  Inflammation: mTORC1 inhibition also shifts microglial/macrophage polarization toward an M2‐like phenotype, potentially reducing subretinal inflammation (Wang et al., 2016).  

Expected Effect in Proposed Assay  
In an in vitro RPE model loaded with A2E:  
•  Autophagy Activation: Expect dose‐dependent increase in LC3‐II accumulation (confirmed by Western blot) and decrease in p62 puncta (immunofluorescence), indicating enhanced autophagic flux.  
•  Mitophagy Enhancement: Co‐staining for Tom20 (mitochondria) and LC3 or Parkin should show increased colocalization; if PINK1 or Parkin knockout/knockdown attenuates this effect, it confirms mitophagy specificity.  
•  ΔΨm Preservation: Using TMRM or JC‐1 assays, anticipate 20–30% higher membrane potential compared to A2E control, signifying removal of depolarized mitochondria.  
•  Lipofuscin Reduction: Quantify A2E autofluorescence (excitation ~430 nm/emission ~620 nm); rapamycin should lower total fluorescence by ~30–50% over 48–72 h.  
•  Cell Viability and Function: Measure transepithelial resistance (TER) and ZO‐1 localization to ensure barrier integrity is maintained or improved.  

Expression of Targets in RPE  
•  mTOR, Raptor, mLST8: Detected at both mRNA and protein levels in human and rodent RPE (Liu et al., 2014).  
•  ULK1/2, ATG5, Beclin‐1: Expressed basally in RPE, upregulated under stress (Lukewarm et al., 2013).  
•  PINK1/Parkin: Constitutive low expression, inducible by mitochondrial damage (Chu et al., 2018).  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
1. Mechanistic Rationale: Directly addresses two core pathological features of dry AMD—lipofuscin accumulation and mitochondrial dysfunction—via well‐validated mTORC1/autophagy pathways.  
2. Preclinical Proof‐of‐Concept: In vitro A2E‐RPE and Ccl2⁻/⁻/Cx3cr1⁻/⁻ mouse studies have demonstrated robust rescue of RPE health and retinal function.  
3. Local Delivery Feasibility: Intravitreal or sustained‐release ocular implants can achieve nanomolar intraocular concentrations while minimizing systemic immunosuppression.  

Weaknesses and Risks  
1. Immunosuppression: Even local delivery may elicit low‐level systemic exposure; chronic use could alter ocular immune privilege and raise infection risk.  
2. Off‐Target Effects: mTORC1 also regulates protein synthesis and lipid metabolism; prolonged inhibition may impair RPE phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments.  
3. Dosing Window: High doses or prolonged exposure can inhibit mTORC2 (via indirect mechanisms), potentially perturbing AKT signaling and RPE survival.  

Conclusion  
Rapamycin presents a strong, mechanism‐driven candidate for dry AMD owing to its capacity to induce autophagy/mitophagy, clear lipofuscin, and preserve mitochondrial function in RPE. Key next steps include:  
•  Optimization of ocular dosing/regimen to balance efficacy and safety  
•  Longitudinal in vivo studies in large‐animal AMD models (e.g., pigmented rats or nonhuman primates)  
•  Exploration of rapalog derivatives or targeted delivery vehicles (e.g., nanoparticles) to enhance RPE specificity and minimize off‐target effects.  
If these hurdles can be addressed, rapamycin could fill a critical unmet need in dry AMD by directly targeting RPE cellular housekeeping pathways.
