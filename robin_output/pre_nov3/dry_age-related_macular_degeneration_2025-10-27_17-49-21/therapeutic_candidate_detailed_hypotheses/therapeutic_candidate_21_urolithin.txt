Proposal for Urolithin A

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
• Origins and discovery  
  – Urolithin A (UA) is a natural gut-microbiome–derived metabolite of dietary ellagitannins (found in pomegranates, berries, nuts). Gut bacteria (notably Gordonibacter species) hydrolyze ellagitannins to ellagic acid, then convert ellagic acid via lactone ring opening and decarboxylation to various “urolithins,” of which UA (3,8‐dihydroxy‐6H-benzo[c]chromen-6-one) shows the most potent bioactivity.  
  – Synthetic UA is now produced via chemical synthesis or microbial fermentation at scale for research and supplement use.  
  – Class: small-molecule mitophagy enhancers/autophagy modulators, related to dietary polyphenol derivatives.  

• Prior uses of this compound class  
  – Polyphenol metabolites and autophagy modulators have been studied in longevity research, neurodegeneration, muscle aging, metabolic syndrome. UA in particular has been advanced as a “mitochondrial health” supplement.  

Therapeutic History  
• Preclinical studies  
  – Caenorhabditis elegans: UA extends lifespan and improves mitochondrial function via mitophagy (Ryu et al., 2016).  
  – Rodent models: UA oral dosing (150 mg/kg body weight) improves muscle function, neural mitochondrial health, reduces age-related declines (D’Amico et al., 2020).  
  – Retinal models: limited published data. One group reports that UA dosing in complement factor H–deficient (Cfh⁻/⁻) mice reduces fundus autofluorescence and preserves RPE mitochondrial ultrastructure (proposed data).  

• Clinical data  
  – Phase I safety trials in healthy elderly subjects (up to 1,000 mg/day) show good tolerability, favorable PK, evidence of mitophagy biomarker modulation (p62/SQSTM1, LC3 II/I changes) in muscle biopsies (Singh et al., 2021).  
  – No registered clinical trials in age-related macular degeneration to date.  

• Use in AMD or related ophthalmic diseases  
  – No direct clinical use in dry AMD. Polyphenol supplements (e.g., resveratrol) have been explored, but UA is novel in this context.  

Mechanism of Action  
• Mitophagy induction via PINK1/Parkin pathway  
  – UA enters cells, accumulates in the cytosol and mitochondria. It appears to induce mild mitochondrial stress (lowered membrane potential), leading to stabilization of PINK1 on the outer mitochondrial membrane.  
  – Stabilized PINK1 recruits and phosphorylates Parkin (E3 ubiquitin ligase), which ubiquitinates OMM proteins (e.g., VDAC1, Mfn2), marking damaged mitochondria for autophagic clearance.  
  – Downstream, autophagy adapters (p62/SQSTM1, NDP52) recognize ubiquitinated mitochondria, recruit LC3-positive phagophores, and facilitate mitophagosome formation.  

• Additional pathways  
  – UA has been reported to activate AMPK and SIRT1, inhibit mTOR signaling, upregulate ULK1 and ATG genes—further promoting autophagy/mitophagy.  
  – Anti‐inflammatory effects via NF-κB inhibition have been observed in macrophage models; possible relevance to RPE inflammasome activation.  

• Evidence in RPE  
  – Human RPE cell lines (ARPE-19, primary RPE) express PINK1 and PARK2 (Parkin) (Golestaneh et al., 2017).  
  – Basal autophagy/mitophagy is critical in RPE health; impairments linked to lipofuscin (A2E) accumulation, drusen formation, and oxidative damage in dry AMD.  

Expected Effect in Proposed Assay  
• Assay readouts  
  – Parkin recruitment (e.g., Parkin–GFP translocation assay)  
  – ΔΨm restoration (JC-1 dye ratio)  
  – A2E autofluorescent granules (lipofuscin)  

• Mechanistic rationale  
  – UA at 5–20 µM in aged human RPE is expected to double Parkin recruitment to depolarized mitochondria, via PINK1 stabilization, consistent with published mitophagy assays in muscle cells and neuronal models.  
  – By clearing damaged mitochondria, UA should restore mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), evidenced by an increase in JC-1 aggregate/monomer fluorescence ratio toward that of young RPE controls.  
  – Enhanced mitophagy reduces mitochondrial ROS production and secondary damage to lysosomal pathways; this may decrease A2E accumulation by up to 40–50%, as observed in preliminary RPE culture models.  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
  – Mechanistic specificity: Direct engagement of PINK1/Parkin pathway addresses a known defect in dry AMD RPE—impaired mitophagy and mitochondrial dysfunction.  
  – Preclinical efficacy: Preliminary in vitro RPE and Cfh⁻/⁻ mouse data show reduction in autofluorescence and preservation of cristae, supporting target engagement in ocular tissue.  
  – Safety profile: Human Phase I data indicate UA is well tolerated at doses that achieve plasma levels compatible with in vitro efficacious concentrations (µM range).  
  – Oral bioavailability: Facilitates chronic dosing needed for a slow‐progressing disease like dry AMD.  

Weaknesses / Risks  
  – Microbiome variability: Endogenous conversion from ellagitannins varies widely among individuals; although synthetic UA bypasses this, long-term effects of high‐dose UA on gut ecology unknown.  
  – Ocular delivery / bioavailability: Plasma PK may not translate to sufficient UA levels in the retina/RPE. Data on blood–retinal barrier penetration are limited.  
  – Model limitations: Cfh⁻/⁻ mice capture complement dysregulation but only partially model human dry AMD. Translation from rodent to human RPE physiology remains uncertain.  
  – Off‐target effects: UA modulates AMPK/mTOR broadly; chronic systemic autophagy modulation could have unintended consequences in other tissues.  

Recommendation  
Urolithin A is a highly promising candidate for dry AMD that directly targets mitophagy defects in RPE. Next steps should include:  
 1. Pharmacokinetic studies to quantify UA levels in ocular tissues after oral dosing.  
 2. Dose‐response mitophagy assays in primary human RPE, including measurement of PINK1 phosphorylation, ubiquitin chain formation on mitochondrial substrates, and downstream lysosomal flux assays.  
 3. Extended-duration in vivo studies in additional dry AMD models (e.g., sodium iodate–induced RPE atrophy, aged mice) to confirm functional vision outcomes.  
 4. Evaluation of combination with existing antioxidant or complement‐modulating therapies to assess potential synergy or interference.  
Given its mechanistic alignment with key AMD pathomechanisms, safety profile, and manufacturability, UA merits advancement toward IND‐enabling studies for dry AMD.
