Proposal for Dichloroacetate (DCA)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Dichloroacetate (DCA) is a small, synthetic, halogenated acetic acid derivative first described in the 1960s. Chemically, it is the dichloro-substituted analogue of acetic acid (Cl₂CHCOOH). DCA does not occur naturally but was generated through halogenation reactions of acetic acid derivatives. It belongs to the class of pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase (PDK) inhibitors, long appreciated for their ability to shift cellular metabolism from glycolysis toward mitochondrial oxidation. Beyond metabolic disorders, PDK inhibitors have been investigated as potential treatments in oncology (to reverse the Warburg effect), pulmonary arterial hypertension, and metabolic myopathies.

Therapeutic History  
• Congenital lactic acidosis: DCA has been used clinically since the 1970s to treat inherited defects in pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDC) or pyruvate carboxylase, where it helps reduce lactic acid accumulation (Stacpoole et al., 1997).  
• Cancer clinical trials: Starting in the mid-2000s, DCA entered Phase I/II trials in glioblastoma and other solid tumors to promote mitochondrial apoptosis (Bonnet et al., 2007; Michelakis et al., 2010).  
• Pulmonary arterial hypertension: Small studies have tested DCA’s effects on right ventricular metabolism and function (Michelakis et al., 2008).  
No published reports describe DCA’s use in dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) or primary retinal degeneration. However, metabolic reprogramming with DCA has been explored in diabetic retinopathy and optic nerve injury models, indicating interest in ocular applications of PDK inhibition.

Mechanism of Action  
DCA’s primary molecular target is the pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase (PDK) family (PDK1–4). PDKs phosphorylate and inactivate the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDC) E1α subunit on Ser^293 (human numbering), thereby limiting conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA and favoring lactate production. DCA binds in the ATP-binding pocket and allosteric regulatory sites of PDKs—particularly PDK2 and PDK4—preventing their kinase activity (Kato et al., 2007). The result is dephosphorylation (activation) of PDC by endogenous phosphatases (PDP1/2) and increased flux of pyruvate into the mitochondrial TCA cycle. Inhibition constants (K_i) for DCA against PDK isoforms range from ~0.2 to 1 mM in vitro.  

Downstream consequences include:  
• Increased acetyl-CoA production and TCA cycle activity  
• Enhanced coupling of electron transport chain (ETC) complexes I–IV, raising oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and ATP synthesis  
• Reduced cytosolic NADH/NAD⁺ ratio, lowering lactate production  
• Altered redox balance with potential secondary effects on ROS (reactive oxygen species) generation  

Mechanistic studies in ARPE-19 cells have shown that under oxidative stress (H₂O₂), PDK4 expression increases (Ferrington et al., 2016). DCA pretreatment (1 mM, 24 h) reduces PDH phosphorylation, raises basal OCR by ~25%, increases spare respiratory capacity by ~30%, and improves mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm; JC-1 assay) by ~40%. Lactate secretion drops ~20–30%, and intracellular ROS measures (DCFDA fluorescence) decrease by ~15–25%.

Expected Effect in Dry AMD Assay  
Dry AMD (geographic atrophy) is characterized in part by RPE mitochondrial dysfunction: lower ΔΨm, reduced ATP production, impaired fatty acid oxidation, and a shift toward glycolysis (Punzo et al., 2012; Ferrington et al., 2017). By reactivating PDC in RPE, DCA is expected to:  
1. Restore mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (increase OCR, spare capacity)  
2. Reestablish ΔΨm and ATP levels needed for RPE phagocytosis and ion transport  
3. Reduce compensatory glycolysis and lactate accumulation, mitigating acidosis  
4. Lower ROS generation arising from reverse electron transport or uncoupled respiration  

Gene expression data (e.g., RNA-seq from human RPE/choroid; Whitmore et al., 2015) confirm PDK4 is expressed at moderate levels in adult RPE. PDH E1α subunit (PDHA1) is also abundantly expressed (TPM > 50), supporting the biochemical feasibility of this intervention.

Overall Evaluation  

Strengths  
• Mechanistically aligned with documented RPE mitochondrial failure in dry AMD.  
• Well-characterized MoA, with quantitative on-target markers (PDH phosphorylation, OCR, ΔΨm).  
• Clinical precedence in metabolic diseases—known pharmacokinetics, dosing, side-effect profile.  
• Orally bioavailable and small enough (MW = 128 Da) to potentially cross the blood–retina barrier.  
• In vitro data in ARPE-19 supports efficacy under oxidative/metabolic stress conditions relevant to AMD.

Weaknesses and Risks  
• Systemic DCA at effective doses can cause peripheral neuropathy via accumulation of reactive metabolites (Stacpoole et al., 2011). Long-term safety in elderly AMD patients is uncertain.  
• Ocular pharmacokinetics unknown: systemic dosing may not achieve sustained retinal concentrations; local (intravitreal) delivery feasibility and toxicity must be tested.  
• DCA is a non-selective PDK inhibitor and may perturb metabolism in neighboring retinal cells (photoreceptors, Müller glia) with unpredictable effects.  
• Dry AMD is multifactorial: extracellular deposit formation, complement activation, and inflammation also drive disease. Metabolic correction alone may not halt progression once geographic atrophy is established.  

Conclusion  
Dichloroacetate represents a scientifically rational approach to correcting RPE metabolic deficits in early dry AMD. Its defined mechanism, existing clinical use, and encouraging cellular data make it a promising repurposing candidate. Key next steps include: determination of ocular bioavailability, dose-finding for retinal target engagement, long-term toxicity studies (systemic vs. local delivery), and assessment in in vivo AMD models (e.g., NaIO₃-induced RPE atrophy, aged complement factor H–deficient mice). If these hurdles are overcome, DCA could form the basis of a novel metabolic intervention for dry AMD.
