Assay Overview  
1. Disease aspect modeled  
   – Dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is characterized in part by accumulation of neutral lipid–rich deposits (drusen) beneath the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). Excess intracellular lipid droplets in RPE cells contribute to cellular dysfunction, oxidative stress, complement activation, and local inflammation.  
   – This assay models the RPE lipid overload seen in early dry AMD and tests a compound’s ability to reduce or clear lipid droplets.  

2. Readouts and methodology  
   – Lipid loading: RPE cells (e.g. ARPE-19 or iPSC-derived RPE) are incubated with a defined concentration of long-chain fatty acids (commonly oleic acid : albumin complexes) for 12–24 h to induce neutral lipid droplet formation.  
   – Compound treatment: Test compounds are added during, immediately after, or following the lipid‐loading phase.  
   – Staining and quantification: After treatment (typically 24–72 h), cells are fixed and stained with BODIPY 493/503 (or BODIPY 558/568 C12) to label neutral lipids. Nuclei are counterstained (e.g. DAPI). High‐content fluorescence microscopy or automated image analysis is used to quantify:  
     • Number of droplets per cell  
     • Total droplet area per cell  
     • Mean fluorescence intensity of BODIPY per field  
   – Data normalization: Results are normalized to vehicle controls (fully lipid‐loaded, no treatment) and to non–lipid‐loaded cells.  

3. Biological material  
   – RPE cell lines: ARPE-19 is commonly used for initial screens due to ease of culture.  
   – Human iPSC-derived RPE: offer better polarity, pigmentation, and barrier function, more closely resembling in vivo RPE.  
   – Primary human fetal or adult RPE: less widely used for high‐throughput but higher physiological relevance.  

Biomedical Evidence  
1. Lipid accumulation and drusen formation  
   – Drusen, the hallmark extracellular deposits of dry AMD, are ~40–60% neutral lipid by volume (Curcio et al., Prog Retin Eye Res 2010). Their lipid core includes cholesterol esters, triglycerides, and apolipoproteins.  
   – RPE lipid homeostasis is impaired in aging: decreased β-oxidation, autophagy decline, and reduced cholesterol efflux (Zhao et al., Prog Retin Eye Res 2015).  

2. Pathogenic consequences  
   – Intracellular neutral lipid droplets correlate with RPE oxidative stress and para-inflammation (Mitter et al., Autophagy 2014).  
   – Lipid overload in RPE activates the complement cascade, recruits microglia/macrophages, and triggers the NLRP3 inflammasome—key events in dry AMD progression (Tseng et al., J Neuroinflammation 2013; Kauppinen et al., Prog Retin Eye Res 2016).  
   – Genetic links: Polymorphisms in lipid‐handling genes (ABCA1, LIPC, CETP) are associated with AMD risk (Fritsche et al., Nat Genet 2013).  

3. Therapeutic rationale  
   – Agents that promote lipid droplet turnover—by enhancing autophagy, stimulating PPAR/LXR pathways, or increasing cholesterol efflux—have been hypothesized to slow or prevent drusen formation and RPE dysfunction.  

Previous Use  
1. Assay development and mechanistic studies  
   – Mitter et al. (Autophagy 2014): Demonstrated that macroautophagy is required for clearance of oleic acid–induced lipid droplets in ARPE-19 cells. They used BODIPY staining and showed that autophagy inducers (e.g. rapamycin) reduce droplet burden, suggesting a protective mechanism against fatty acid toxicity.  
   – Wang et al. (Sci Rep 2016): Employed a similar lipid‐loading/BODIPY protocol to show that activation of PPARα promotes peroxisome‐mediated β‐oxidation and reduces neutral lipid content in iPSC-derived RPE.  

2. Drug discovery/screening applications  
   – Though no large‐scale compound libraries have been published specifically for this RPE lipid droplet assay, related screens in hepatic or adipocyte models have successfully identified small molecules that reduce intracellular triglycerides (e.g. autophagy enhancers, LXR agonists). The RPE model adapts these principles to the ocular context.  
   – Key discoveries: Validation that autophagy inducers and PPAR/LXR activators ameliorate lipid accumulation in RPE, providing proof‐of‐concept that pharmacological modulation of lipid turnover can rescue RPE cell health.  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
 – Disease relevance: Directly models a core pathological feature of dry AMD—intracellular neutral lipid accumulation in RPE.  
 – Quantitative and scalable: BODIPY staining with high‐content imaging allows dose–response curves and moderate‐throughput screening.  
 – Mechanistic insight: Can be combined with markers of autophagy (LC3, p62), oxidative stress (ROS assays), or inflammation (IL-1β, complement deposition).  
 – Flexible biology: Usable in immortalized or patient‐derived RPE, enabling studies of genetic risk backgrounds.  

Weaknesses & Caveats  
 – Artificial lipid source: Oleic acid overload may not fully recapitulate the complex lipid species found in drusen (cholesterol esters, phospholipids, bisretinoids).  
 – Monoculture limitation: Lacks drusen microenvironment components (Bruch’s membrane alterations, choriocapillaris, immune cells).  
 – RPE maturity: ARPE-19 often display incomplete polarization and under‐express key transporters; hiPSC‐RPE are better but more variable and lower throughput.  
 – Single‐endpoint focus: Lipid droplet reduction does not by itself prove functional rescue of RPE barrier integrity, phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments, or prevention of complement activation.  

Summary  
The lipid droplet reduction assay in RPE is a well‐grounded in vitro model for one of the earliest, central features of dry AMD pathogenesis. It provides a quantitative, mechanistically informative platform to identify compounds that enhance lipid turnover. However, hits from this assay will require secondary validation in more complex systems—co-cultures, organoids, or in vivo models—to assess complementary aspects such as complement regulation, inflammatory signaling, and tissue‐level physiology.
