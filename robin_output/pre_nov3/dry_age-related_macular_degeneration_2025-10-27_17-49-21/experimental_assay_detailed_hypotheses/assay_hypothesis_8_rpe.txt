Assay Overview  
•  Disease aspect modeled: This in vitro assay recapitulates subretinal/choroidal inflammation driven by RPE–microglia cross-talk in dry AMD. It specifically models inflammasome-mediated cytokine release in response to drusen components (e.g. cholesterol crystals, Aβ), a key upstream event in geographic atrophy.  
•  Cells and materials: Human or rodent primary RPE cells (or ARPE-19 with caveats) grown on Transwell or in direct co-culture with primary microglia (e.g. murine retinal microglia) or microglial cell lines (e.g. BV2). Drusen mimetics (cholesterol crystals, Aβ, CEP-protein adducts) are applied to the apical RPE layer.  
•  Readouts: Secreted IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α levels in the co-culture supernatant, quantified by ELISA or multiplex bead array. Optionally, caspase-1 activity assays, qPCR for NLRP3, ASC speck formation by immunofluorescence, and cell‐specific viability assays (LDH release, live/dead staining).  

Biomedical Evidence  
•  Centrality of inflammasome activation in dry AMD:  
   – Tarallo et al. (Cell 2012) demonstrated that Alu RNA accumulation in RPE activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, triggering IL-18/IL-1β release and RPE degeneration.  
   – Kerur et al. (Nat Med 2013) showed that drusen components (cholesterol crystals) activate NLRP3 in RPE and recruit macrophages/microglia, driving subretinal inflammation in murine models.  
   – Wang et al. (Invest Ophth Vis Sci 2016) and Doyle et al. (PLOS One 2012) reported elevated IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α in aqueous humor and post-mortem AMD eyes, correlated with atrophy progression.  
•  RPE–microglia interactions:  
   – Subretinal microglia accumulation is a hallmark of dry AMD (Gupta et al. Prog Retin Eye Res 2003). These microglia adopt a pro-inflammatory phenotype upon contact with stressed RPE or drusen material (Xu et al. J Neuroinflammation 2016).  
   – Crosstalk studies (Xi et al. J Neuroinflammation 2020) show that RPE-derived chemokines (CCL2) recruit microglia, which in turn secrete TNF-α and exacerbate RPE dysfunction.  

Previous Use  
•  Drug discovery and target validation:  
   – Small‐molecule inhibitors of NLRP3 (e.g. MCC950) have been tested in RPE monocultures and in RPE–microglia co-cultures for their ability to reduce IL-1β/IL-18 release (Lamkanfi & Dixit, Nat Rev Drug Discov 2009; Coll et al. Nat Med 2015).  
   – Anti-inflammatory biologics (anti-IL-1β antibodies) were profiled in co-culture systems to assess RPE protection (Vringer & Tait, J Clin Invest 2019).  
•  Mechanistic discoveries:  
   – Use of co-culture revealed that microglia amplify RPE inflammasome activation via ROS and mitochondrial DNA release (Guo et al. J Neurochem 2018).  
   – The dual requirement for “signal 1” (drusen/TLR activation) and “signal 2” (crystal uptake/lysosomal damage) in co-culture mirrors in vivo priming and activation of inflammasome in AMD.  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
•  Disease relevance: Directly models a core pathogenic axis—RPE inflammasome activation and microglial amplification—in dry AMD.  
•  Readout specificity: Quantitative ELISA/multiplex for IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α provides clear markers of efficacy.  
•  Modularity: Can substitute human iPSC-derived RPE or primary human microglia, and vary drusen components to match patient-specific phenotypes.  
•  Mechanistic depth: Enables downstream assays (caspase-1 activity, ASC specks) to confirm on-target (NLRP3) effects.  

Weaknesses  
•  Complexity and variability: Primary cells show donor variability; ARPE-19 and BV2 lines may not fully recapitulate human in vivo physiology.  
•  Throughput: Co-culture and drusen component preparation are labor-intensive; ELISA plates limit large compound library screens.  
•  Missing elements: Lacks Bruch’s membrane, choroidal endothelium and complement cascade components, all of which modulate AMD progression.  
•  Endpoint limitations: Cytokine release is a surrogate for long-term RPE survival and photoreceptor support—additional co-culture with photoreceptors or transepithelial resistance measurements may be required.  

Conclusion  
The RPE–microglia co-culture inflammation assay offers a biologically grounded, mechanistic platform to screen and profile anti-inflammatory and anti-inflammasome therapeutics for dry AMD. Its key strength lies in modeling the NLRP3-driven cytokine loop documented in both preclinical models and human tissue. However, to fully capture the multifactorial nature of geographic atrophy, this assay should be complemented by other readouts (barrier function, complement activation) or more complex organotypic/explant models in later discovery phases.
