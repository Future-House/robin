Query: “Role of alternative complement pathway components (CFH, C3a, C5b-9) in mediating RPE cell injury and subretinal inflammation: development of cell‐based assays measuring complement deposition, cell viability, and proinflammatory cytokine release”

Below is a structured review of the rationale, precedent and practical considerations for a cell‐based assay in which human RPE monolayers are exposed to alternative‐pathway complement components (CFH, C3a, C5b‐9), and where readouts include (1) complement deposition, (2) RPE viability/cytotoxicity, and (3) pro-inflammatory cytokine release.  Taken together, the evidence supports the utility of such an assay for screening or profiling drugs aimed at the complement‐driven arm of dry AMD pathogenesis, but also points to key design choices and limitations.

1.  Rationale: complement in dry AMD pathogenesis  
   •  Genetic associations  
      –  Polymorphisms in complement factor H (CFH), C3, C2/CFB and other regulators strongly associate with AMD risk (Hageman et al., 2005; Klein et al., 2005; Fritsche et al., 2016).  
      –  CFH Y402H variant correlates with increased alternative‐pathway activation in Bruch’s membrane and drusen (Mullins et al., 2011).  
   •  Histopathology  
      –  Drusen and RPE/choriocapillaris junctions in AMD eyes stain for C3 activation fragments and the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) (Johnson et al., 2000; Crabb et al., 2002).  
      –  Sublytic MAC deposition on RPE can trigger proinflammatory signalling without causing outright lysis (Lanz et al., 2013).  
   •  Mechanistic studies  
      –  In vitro, human RPE (primary or ARPE-19) spontaneously express complement proteins (C3, FB), regulators (CFH, CD46/CD55) and receptors, and are capable of local alternative‐pathway activation (Rutar et al., 2012; Anderson et al., 2010).  
      –  Exogenous C3a/C5a enhance RPE secretion of IL-6, IL-8, MCP-1 and VEGF (Strainic et al., 2008; Streit & Kolb, 1998).  
      –  Sublytic C5b-9 insertion on RPE elevates NF-κB signalling and drives cytokine release (Yu et al., 2012).  

2.  Precedents for cell‐based complement assays  
   •  Complement deposition assays  
      –  Immunofluorescence or ELISA measurement of deposited C3b/iC3b or neo‐epitope of C5b-9 on RPE monolayers after incubation with normal versus complement-depleted sera (Kaur et al., 2009; Beekhuizen et al., 2010).  
      –  Quantitative imaging using confocal microscopy and automated image analysis (quantify spot intensity/area).  
   •  Viability and cytotoxicity  
      –  LDH release, MTT/XTT, CellTiter-Glo® to capture complement‐mediated lysis or sublytic dysfunction (Skeie & Mahajan, 2014).  
      –  Annexin V/PI flow or high‐content imaging for apoptosis versus necrosis after MAC insertion.  
   •  Cytokine and chemokine readouts  
      –  Multiplex bead-based immunoassays (Luminex) or ELISA for IL-6, IL-8, MCP-1, TNFα, VEGF secreted by RPE under complement attack (Rutar et al., 2012; Kauppinen et al., 2016).  

3.  How this maps to dry AMD drug discovery  
   •  Target validation and mechanism of action  
      –  If your therapeutic aims to stabilize CFH function, block C3 convertase, neutralize C3a/C5a or prevent MAC assembly, this assay directly measures those modes of action at an RPE‐relevant interface.  
   •  Screening and lead optimization  
      –  Medium‐throughput format (96- or 384-well) is feasible using plate‐based deposition and viability kits.  
      –  Allows rank ordering of compounds by potency in preventing C3b/C5b-9 deposition, preserving RPE viability and blunting cytokine release.  
   •  Biomarker linkage  
      –  Cytokine signature (e.g. IL-6/IL-8 ratio) may serve as a proximal PD biomarker translatable to aqueous humor or serum in clinical studies.  

4.  Key assay design considerations  
   4.1  Source of complement  
      –  Pooled normal human serum (NHS) provides all pathway components; heat-inactivated or factor-depleted serum controls specificity.  
      –  Recombinant C3a/C5a can be added to isolate anaphylatoxin effects from MAC.  
   4.2  RPE model  
      –  Primary human RPE (better physiological fidelity but donor variability) versus ARPE-19 (robust, but lower complement expression).  
      –  Differentiated iPSC-RPE could capture patient genotype (e.g. CFH Y402H).  
   4.3  Endpoint timing  
      –  Early (1–4 h) for complement deposition, medium (4–24 h) for cytokines, late (24–48 h) for cell death.  
   4.4  Controls and counter-screens  
      –  Add C5‐blocking antibody or CD59 to define MAC‐specific effects.  
      –  Oxidative stress (H2O2, blue-light) co-treatment to mimic multi-factorial injury.  

5.  Limitations and complementary approaches  
   •  Reductionist nature  
      –  Lacks RPE–choroid 3D architecture, Bruch’s membrane, microglia/macrophage interactions.  
   •  Complement overactivation versus homeostatic roles  
      –  Sub-lytic MAC can be pro-survival in some contexts; careful interpretation needed (Yu et al., 2012).  
   •  Complement-independent pathways  
      –  Oxidative stress, lipid accumulation and autophagy are also drivers of RPE injury in AMD (Kannan et al., 2017).  
   •  Complement–microglia cross-talk  
      –  Co-culture assays with microglia or macrophages can reveal additional pro-inflammatory amplification loops (Liu et al., 2013).  

6.  Conclusions  
   –  A cell‐based assay that measures alternative‐pathway complement deposition, RPE viability and cytokine release is well grounded in AMD genetics, histopathology and in vitro mechanistic studies.  
   –  It provides a scalable platform to triage and optimize complement‐modulating therapies in early‐stage discovery.  
   –  To more fully recapitulate dry AMD pathophysiology, one should consider orthogonal models (co-cultures, ECM‐coated inserts, oxidative hits), but as a first‐pass screen for complement‐focused agents this assay is both relevant and actionable.
