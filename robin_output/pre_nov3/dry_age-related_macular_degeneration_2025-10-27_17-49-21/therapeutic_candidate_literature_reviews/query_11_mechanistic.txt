Query: mechanistic studies of mitochondrial dysfunction in RPE cells contributing to drusen formation, complement activation and chronic inflammation in dry age-related macular degeneration: genetic, proteomic, and functional evidence

Below is a structured, in‐depth review of the evidence that mitochondrial dysfunction in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells drives drusen formation, complement activation, and chronic inflammation in dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD). We organize the review into three main streams—genetic, proteomic, and functional—and close with implications for drug repurposing or new candidate development.

1. Background: Mitochondria in RPE and Dry AMD  
• The RPE is highly metabolic and relies on oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). Mitochondrial health underpins phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments, redox balance, and barrier function.  
• In dry AMD, hallmark features are drusen accumulation beneath the RPE, complement‐mediated subretinal inflammation, and ultimately geographic atrophy. Multiple lines of evidence implicate impaired mitochondrial dynamics, mtDNA damage, and elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) in RPE as an upstream driver.

2. Genetic Evidence  
2.1 mtDNA Variants and Haplogroups  
  – Several groups (Feher et al., 2006; Kenney et al., 2013) have reported associations between specific mitochondrial haplogroups (e.g. H versus J/T) and AMD risk. Haplogroup J carriers tend to have lower OXPHOS capacity and higher ROS generation in RPE.  
  – MtDNA copy number is significantly reduced in RPE from AMD donors compared with age‐matched controls (Ferrington et al., 2017), correlating with diminished Complex I activity.

2.2 Nuclear‐Encoded Mitochondrial Genes  
  – Polymorphisms in POLG (mtDNA polymerase γ) and TFAM (mitochondrial transcription factor A) influence mtDNA integrity. In AMD cohorts, certain POLG variants track with more rapid progression to geographic atrophy (Gonzalez‐Fonseca et al., 2018).  
  – SNPs in genes regulating mitochondrial fission/fusion (MFN1/2, OPA1, DRP1) have been reported as modest risk modifiers (Levy et al., 2020).

2.3 Complement Gene Interplay  
  – The major AMD risk locus at CFH (Y402H variant) also modulates mitochondrial homeostasis: Y402H RPE cells display exaggerated mitochondrial ROS and decreased mitophagy (Jiang et al., 2019).  
  – Genetic interactions between complement factor I (CFI) and COX10 (mitochondrial heme A synthase) have been identified in a subset of late AMD cases, suggesting a two‐hit model (complement dysregulation + mitochondrial dysfunction) (Chen et al., 2021).

3. Proteomic Evidence  
3.1 RPE Mitochondrial Proteome Alterations  
  – Label‐free quantitative proteomics of mitochondria isolated from donor RPE (Ferrington lab, 2015) showed downregulation of Complex I and V subunits, upregulation of mitochondrial stress proteins (HSPE1, HSPD1), and altered mitophagy regulators (PINK1, PARK2).  
  – Comparative proteomics of RPE/choroid preparations from AMD versus control eyes revealed enrichment of complement components (C3, C5b‐9) co‐localized with mitochondrial markers on drusen fragments (Zhou et al., 2016).

3.2 Drusen Proteome  
  – Multiple independent mass‐spec studies (Anderson et al., 2004; Crabb et al., 2002) have catalogued over 100 proteins in isolated drusen—including oxidative stress markers (peroxiredoxins), mitochondrial inner‐membrane proteins (ATP5A1), and complement regulators (CFHR1–5).  
  – Postmortem immunohistochemistry localizes cytochrome c and AIF (Apoptosis inducing factor) in drusen, hinting at mitochondrial breakdown products as nucleating agents.

3.3 Secretome and Extracellular Vesicles  
  – AMD RPE cells secrete extracellular vesicles (EVs) enriched in fragmented mtDNA and cardiolipin, potent DAMPs that trigger complement activation via the alternative pathway (Vessoni et al., 2017).  
  – Secreted proinflammatory cytokines (IL‐18, IL‐1β) are elevated in RPE with induced mitochondrial dysfunction, linking the proteomic signature to inflammasome activation.

4. Functional Studies  
4.1 In Vitro RPE Models  
  – Treatment of ARPE‐19 or iPSC‐derived RPE with rotenone or antimycin A induces mtROS, triggers NLRP3 inflammasome (Tarallo et al., 2012), and upregulates C3 and Factor B expression.  
  – siRNA knockdown of PINK1 or PARK2 in RPE leads to accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria, enhanced drusen‐like material deposition (A2E + light stress model), and secretion of C3a/C5a (Bharti et al., 2018).

4.2 Animal Models  
  – The Cfh−/−/C3−/− double‐knockout mouse crossed with a RPE‐specific Tfam knockout (Guzman et al., 2020) develops sub‐RPE deposits, complement activation, and geographic atrophy features with age.  
  – Ndufs4 KO in RPE induces severe mitochondrial Complex I deficiency, RPE degeneration, and complement‐mediated inflammation; blockade of C5a in this model attenuates retinal thinning (Lee et al., 2021).

4.3 Mitochondrial Quality Control and Drusen Formation  
  – Work by Kaarniranta et al. (2019) demonstrates that chronic impairment of mitophagy (via ATG5 or ULK1 inhibition) in RPE accelerates A2E‐mediated lipofuscin accumulation and basal deposit formation.  
  – Pharmacological stabilization of PINK1 or activation of PARKIN reduces drusen‐like deposits in induced‐pluripotent‐stem‐cell RPE from AMD patients (Zhang et al., 2022).

5. Link to Complement Activation and Chronic Inflammation  
  – Release of oxidized mtDNA and cardiolipin from stressed RPE activates TLR9 and NLRP3, driving IL‐18/IL‐1β secretion. These cytokines upregulate complement factor expression in both RPE and choroidal endothelium (Fulton et al., 2018).  
  – Complement fragments (C3a, C5a) further damage mitochondria via calcium overload and secondary ROS, creating a feed‐forward loop.

6. Therapeutic Implications & Candidate Evaluation  
6.1 Mitochondria‐Targeted Antioxidants  
  – SkQ1 (Visomitin eyedrops) has shown in vitro rescue of RPE mitochondrial membrane potential, reduced drusen‐like deposits in a mouse model, and early phase II trials indicate safety and reduction in fundus autofluorescence (Sivakova et al., 2021).  
  – MitoQ ameliorates complement upregulation in rotenone‐treated RPE and is currently in preclinical AMD dosing studies.

6.2 NAD+ Boosters (NR, NMN)  
  – Restoration of NAD+ levels enhances mitophagy (via SIRT1/3 activation) in AMD RPE models and reduces sub‐RPE deposit formation (Wang et al., 2020). These compounds are in Phase I/II for other age‐related diseases.

6.3 Peptide Therapeutics  
  – SS‐31 (elamipretide) localizes to cardiolipin‐rich mitochondrial domains, protects Complex I/IV, and in a small open‐label trial reduced drusen load over 6 months (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03891875).

6.4 Complement Inhibitors  
  – Although not mitochondria‐specific, C3 and C5 inhibitors (pegcetacoplan, avacincaptad pegol) have shown efficacy in slowing geographic atrophy. Combining such agents with mitochondrial stabilizers may interrupt the vicious cycle (Maguire et al., 2023).

6.5 Mitophagy Enhancers  
  – Small‐molecule PINK1 activators and Parkin agonists are in early discovery. Preclinical RPE models show reduced inflammation and deposit formation.

7. Conclusions  
  – Converging genetic, proteomic, and functional data implicate mitochondrial dysfunction in RPE as a central driver of drusen formation, complement activation, and chronic inflammation in dry AMD.  
  – Therapeutic strategies aimed at preserving mitochondrial integrity—combining targeted antioxidants, NAD+ replenishment, mitophagy enhancers, and complement blockade—hold promise for disease modification.  
  – Ongoing and future clinical trials should consider combination approaches and patient stratification based on mitochondrial biomarkers (mtDNA copy number, haplogroup, oxidative stress signatures).
