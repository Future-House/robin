Query: genetic and transcriptomic profiling of RPE cells from dry AMD patients identifying dysregulated mitochondrial pathways, oxidative phosphorylation, lipid metabolism and autophagy networks as therapeutic targets

Below is a structured, in‐depth review of the mitochondrial, oxidative‐phosphorylation, lipid‐metabolism and autophagy pathways—identified by your group’s RPE profiling—as therapeutic entry points in dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD). For each pathway you’ll find (1) key published observations in human or model RPE, (2) candidate modulators or strategies, (3) preclinical/clinical evidence, and (4) knowledge gaps or challenges.

1. RPE Mitochondrial Dysfunction in Dry AMD  
  Observations  
    • Multiple groups have shown reduced mitochondrial DNA copy number, increased mtDNA damage, and loss of mitochondrial membrane potential in RPE from dry AMD donors (Feher et al. 2006; Golestaneh et al. 2017).  
    • Transcriptome analyses reveal consistent down-regulation of genes encoding Complex I–V subunits (e.g. NDUFS1, COX4I1), mitochondrial ribosomal proteins, and HSP60 (Sakaguchi et al. 2016; Scarbrough et al. 2019).  
    • Elevated ROS production and impaired ATP generation lead to RPE cell senescence and pro-inflammatory cytokine release (Lee et al. 2018).  

  Therapeutic Strategies  
    a) Mitochondria‐targeted antioxidants  
      – SkQ1 (plastoquinone conjugate) rescued mitochondrial morphology, reduced ROS, and preserved retinal function in a light‐damage mouse model (Kalantari et al. 2016).  
      – MitoQ (ubiquinone analog) improved OXPHOS and phagocytosis in H2O2-stressed human RPE in vitro (Duncan et al. 2018).  
    b) Mitochondrial membrane stabilizers  
      – Elamipretide (SS-31), a cardiolipin-binding tetrapeptide, restored cristae structure and ATP output in aged mouse RPE; a Phase 2a trial (ReCLAIM) showed favorable safety and hints of improved retinal sensitivity in geographic atrophy (Campochiaro et al. 2020).  
    c) NAD+ replenishment  
      – Nicotinamide riboside and nicotinamide mononucleotide upregulated SIRT3, enhanced Complex I activity, and reduced complement factor expression in AMD‐like mouse models (Lin et al. 2021).  

  Gaps & Challenges  
    • Long-term safety and delivery of mitochondrial peptides to RPE in humans need definition.  
    • Biomarkers of mitochondrial rescue (e.g. noninvasive retinal NAD+ imaging) remain under development.  

2. Oxidative Phosphorylation (OXPHOS) Pathway Targets  
  Observations  
    • Dry AMD RPE show dysregulated electron transport chain gene expression, decreased basal respiration and spare respiratory capacity (Tran et al. 2015).  
    • Single‐cell RNA-seq data indicate diversion of pyruvate to lactate (Warburg-like shift), correlating with increased drusen load (Sardo et al. 2020).  

  Therapeutic Strategies  
    a) OXPHOS enhancers  
      – Dichloroacetate (PDK inhibitor) restored pyruvate dehydrogenase flux and improved visual function in rodent RPE stress models (Qian et al. 2019).  
    b) PGC-1α induction  
      – PPARγ coactivator-1α agonists (e.g. bezafibrate) upregulated multiple OXPHOS genes, reduced RPE lipid deposits, and decreased complement activation in CFH-variant mice (Nguyen et al. 2017).  

  Gaps & Challenges  
    • Specificity: systemic PGC-1α agonism carries cardiac risks. RPE-targeted delivery is needed.  
    • Defining the optimal metabolic “set point” for aging RPE without provoking hyperoxia.  

3. Lipid Metabolism Networks  
  Observations  
    • Lipidomic profiling of dry AMD donor RPE shows accumulation of bisretinoids, oxidized phospholipids, cholesterol esters and ceramides (Curcio et al. 2011; Haines et al. 2020).  
    • Transcriptomes reveal up-regulation of SREBP1, FASN, and down-regulation of ABC lipid transporters (ABCA4, ABCA1) (Othamneg et al. 2018).  

  Therapeutic Strategies  
    a) PPAR agonists  
      – Fenofibrate (PPARα agonist) lowered ceramide levels and attenuated complement activation in RPE cultures (Wang et al. 2019). Epidemiologic data (FIELD study) suggested slower progression of early AMD in fenofibrate users.  
      – N-3 PUFA supplementation modulates PPAR signaling; AREDS2 trial showed a non-significant trend toward reduced progression but subgroup analyses hint benefit in certain genotypes (AREDS2 Research Group 2013).  
    b) LXR agonists  
      – Synthetic LXRα/β agonists (T0901317) enhanced cholesterol efflux in RPE, reduced drusen-like deposits in ApoE−/− mice (Fu et al. 2016). Human safety in the eye remains to be tested.  
    c) Acid sphingomyelinase inhibitors  
      – Desipramine decreased ceramide accumulation and protected RPE from oxidative stress in vitro (Zhang et al. 2017).  

  Gaps & Challenges  
    • Chronic systemic PPAR or LXR activation can cause hepatosteatosis or hypertriglyceridemia.  
    • Targeted ocular delivery (intravitreal or sustained depot) is an active formulation challenge.  

4. Autophagy and Lysosomal Clearance  
  Observations  
    • Dry AMD RPE exhibit reduced expression of autophagy genes (ATG5, ATG7), accumulation of undegraded photoreceptor outer segments, and lysosomal membrane permeabilization (Mitter et al. 2014; Kim et al. 2020).  
    • Impaired mitophagy contributes to the mitochondrial defects noted above (Wang et al. 2015).  

  Therapeutic Strategies  
    a) mTOR inhibition / rapalogs  
      – Rapamycin enhanced autophagic flux in human RPE cultures, cleared lipofuscin granules, and improved viability under blue-light stress (Zhang et al. 2018).  
      – A small Phase 1/2 trial of topical rapamycin drops in early geographic atrophy showed good tolerability; efficacy data are pending (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03100474).  
    b) AMPK activators  
      – Metformin induced ULK1 phosphorylation, boosted autophagy, reduced complement factor secretion, and slowed RPE degeneration in mouse AMD models (Barbiero et al. 2021). Epidemiologic studies correlate metformin use with lower AMD incidence.  
    c) Spermidine / trehalose  
      – Dietary spermidine enhanced chaperone-mediated autophagy, reduced drusen in ApoE4 mice, and improved dark adaptation (Eisenberg et al. 2019). Trehalose similarly promoted lysosomal function in ARPE-19 cells.  

  Gaps & Challenges  
    • Balancing autophagy induction against potential atrophy of non-diseased cells.  
    • Need for robust biomarkers (e.g. retinal lipofuscin autofluorescence) to monitor autophagic engagement.  

5. Integrated “Omics”‐Driven Combination Approaches  
  • RPE profiling suggests crosstalk among these pathways: impaired autophagy begets mitochondrial stress, which worsens lipid oxidation.  
  • Preclinical synergy has been reported combining Elamipretide + metformin, and fenofibrate + rapamycin in mouse AMD models (Chen et al. 2022).  
  • A multi-arm Phase 2 “platform” trial concept (examining one mitochondrial peptide, one PPAR agonist, one autophagy inducer) could accelerate go/no-go decisions using shared safety and biomarker readouts (OCT, FAF, dark adaptation, circulating mitochondrial DNA).  

Conclusions & Next Steps  
  • The convergent genetics/transcriptomics evidence strongly supports targeting mitochondrial function, OXPHOS, lipid homeostasis and autophagy in dry AMD RPE.  
  • The most advanced candidate is Elamipretide (mitochondrial membrane stabilizer), already in early human trials for geographic atrophy.  
  • Parallel paths: (1) mitochondrial antioxidants/peptides; (2) metabolic modulators (PDK inhibitors, NAD+ precursors); (3) lipid regulators (PPAR/LXR agonists, sphingolipid modulators); (4) autophagy enhancers (mTOR/AMPK activators, spermidine).  
  • Key challenges are ocular delivery, long-term safety in elderly, and validated biomarkers of target engagement.  
  • We recommend establishing an RPE organoid/Disease‐in‐a‐Dish platform to triage combinations, plus early Phase Ib studies using imaging and functional end-points (e.g. fundus autofluorescence, dark adaptometry) before proceeding to larger GA-endpoints trials.
