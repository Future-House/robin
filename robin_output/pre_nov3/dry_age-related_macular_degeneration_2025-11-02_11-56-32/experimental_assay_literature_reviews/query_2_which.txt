Query: Which cell culture models best recapitulate RPE degeneration and extracellular deposit formation in dry AMD—such as human iPSC-derived RPE, primary donor RPE, and ARPE-19 cells—and what are their relative advantages, limitations, and translatability for mechanistic drug screening?

Human iPSC-derived RPE (hiPSC-RPE) and primary human RPE best recapitulate RPE degeneration and extracellular (drusen-like) deposit formation; ARPE-19 is useful for tractable assays but has important fidelity limits. hiPSC-RPE from AMD patients and risk genotypes reproduce disease-relevant phenotypes, including increased expression/secretion of complement proteins (e.g., C3), lipid accumulation, and formation of basal, collagen IV–associated deposits enriched for drusen proteins (APOE, TIMP3, EFEMP1) after “aging” culture (~90 days); complement-competent serum induces extracellular APOE and lipid deposition, and nicotinamide reverses drusen-associated signatures, supporting mechanistic and pharmacologic translatability (dalvi2019pluripotentstemcells pages 11-13, dujardin2024developmentofa pages 268-268, bharti2022cellculturemodels pages 16-17, dujardin2024developmentofa pages 274-276). Strengths include patient-specific genetics and capacity to model early molecular events; caveats are long culture times, donor-to-donor variability requiring genotyping and stress paradigms to unmask phenotypes, and potential genomic/epigenetic instability that can affect throughput and reproducibility (bharti2022cellculturemodels pages 12-14, malek2020modelsofpathologies pages 7-10, malek2020modelsofpathologies pages 13-15).

Primary donor RPE (fetal/adult) display high physiological fidelity, polarization, and barrier function; fetal RPE constitutively secrete basal APOE-positive deposits that are amplified by complement, and adult donor RPE retain disease-relevant mitochondrial, autophagic, and secretory phenotypes. Complement exposure drives deposition of APOE, vitronectin, clusterin, serum amyloid P, and C5b-9, enabling direct study of complement-modulated depositogenesis (bharti2022cellculturemodels pages 8-9, bharti2022cellculturemodels pages 16-17). Limitations are scarcity, batch variability, postmortem shifts, and dedifferentiation with passage, which constrain throughput (malek2020modelsofpathologies pages 7-10, malek2020modelsofpathologies pages 4-7).

ARPE-19 can form banded fibrillar material with long-term culture and shows increased deposit burden with albumin/retinal homogenate challenge, and it is highly amenable to HTS and genetic manipulation. However, continuous lines often lose polarity, tight junctions, pigmentation, and metabolic functions with passage or suboptimal culture and may adapt to oxidative stress, reducing disease relevance; they are best positioned for early screens with confirmation in higher-fidelity models (bharti2022cellculturemodels pages 16-17, malek2020modelsofpathologies pages 10-13, malek2020modelsofpathologies pages 13-15, bharti2022cellculturemodels pages 19-20, malek2020modelsofpathologies pages 15-18, bharti2022cellculturemodels pages 32-33).

hiPSC-RPE and primary human RPE most faithfully model RPE degeneration and drusen-like deposit formation, with hiPSC-RPE offering patient-specific genetics and demonstrated pharmacologic responsiveness. ARPE-19 supports scalable, mechanistic HTS but requires cautious interpretation and secondary validation. A staged pipeline—ARPE-19 triage, followed by hiPSC-/primary RPE validation (±complement/co-culture)—maximizes translatability for dry AMD drug discovery (bharti2022cellculturemodels pages 32-33, bharti2022cellculturemodels pages 19-20, bharti2022cellculturemodels pages 16-17).
