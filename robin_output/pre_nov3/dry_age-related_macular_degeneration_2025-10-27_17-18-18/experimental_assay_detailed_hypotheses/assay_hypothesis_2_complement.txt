Assay Overview  
• Goal and disease facet modeled  
  – Models sublytic complement activation on human retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), a key driver of RPE dysfunction and drusen formation in dry AMD.  
• Biological materials  
  – ARPE-19 cells grown to confluence in a 2D monolayer.  
  – 10–20% pooled normal human serum (NHS) as source of active complement.  
• Assay workflow and readouts  
  1. Pre-treat ARPE-19 monolayers with test compound or vehicle.  
  2. Add NHS to trigger C3b opsonization and membrane attack complex (MAC/C5b-9) assembly on RPE.  
  3. Incubate for 1–4 h at 37 °C.  
  4. Readouts:  
     – Fluid-phase anaphylatoxins C3a and C5a in supernatant by sandwich ELISA.  
     – Cell-associated MAC (C5b-9) via immunofluorescence or high-content imaging.  
     – Optional: LDH release or transepithelial resistance (TER) for membrane integrity.  

Biomedical Evidence  
• Genetic and clinical linkage of complement to dry AMD  
  – Polymorphisms in CFH, CFI, C3, C9, and other regulators confer 2–7× risk of dry AMD (Hageman et al. 2005; Fritsche et al. 2016).  
  – Elevated levels of systemic C3a/C5a and local C5b-9 have been documented in patient sera, drusen, and RPE–Bruch’s membrane preparations (Johnson et al. 2001; Anderson et al. 2010).  
• Pathogenic role of sublytic MAC on RPE  
  – Sublytic MAC induces chronic RPE stress: mitochondrial dysfunction, VEGF up-regulation, inflammasome activation (Tarallo et al. 2012; Wang et al. 2016).  
  – In primate and rodent models, genetic or antibody-mediated complement dysregulation accelerates RPE atrophy and photoreceptor loss (Nozaki et al. 2006; Lyzogubov et al. 2016).  
• Clinical biomarkers  
  – Increased C5a correlates with geographic atrophy progression (O’hara et al. 2017).  
  – Complement split products in ocular fluids track disease severity (Anderson et al. 2010).  

Previous Use  
• Assay precedent in drug discovery  
  – Anderson et al. (2010) first described ARPE-19 + NHS to quantify RPE‐surface MAC and C3a release, validating complement activation as a druggable axis.  
  – Zipfel & Skerka (2014) and subsequent groups used similar 2D RPE models to screen compstatin analogs (C3 inhibitors) and anti-C5 antibodies.  
• Key discoveries  
  – Demonstration that exogenous CFH or CFH‐mimetic peptides protect RPE from sublytic MAC (Weismann et al. 2011).  
  – Identification of the alternative pathway amplification loop as the dominant driver of RPE complement deposition (Kelly et al. 2018).  
  – Insights into cross-talk between complement activation and NLRP3 inflammasome in RPE (Tarallo et al. 2012).  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
  • Disease relevance: directly models a genetically and pathologically validated driver of dry AMD—RPE complement dysregulation.  
  • Human cell context: uses human RPE line and human complement source, improving translational fidelity versus rodent systems.  
  • Dual readouts: fluid-phase (C3a/C5a) and surface‐deposited MAC enable mechanistic profiling.  
  • Medium throughput: compatible with 96- or 384-well formats, amenable to small‐molecule and biologic screening.  

Weaknesses  
  • Cell line limitations: ARPE-19 lacks full polarization, native Bruch’s membrane interactions, and aged‐RPE phenotype.  
  • Serum variability: donor‐to‐donor differences in complement activity require rigorous standardization or use of pooled/prequalified lots.  
  • Narrow focus: models only the complement axis; dry AMD is multifactorial (oxidative stress, lipid accumulation, mitochondrial decline).  
  • Incomplete pathology: does not recapitulate drusen biogenesis, outer segment phagocytosis defects, or RPE–photoreceptor crosstalk.  

Recommendation  
As a primary screening assay for complement‐targeted agents in dry AMD, this complement deposition inhibition assay is well supported by genetic, biochemical, and clinical literature. To increase physiological relevance and capture broader aspects of AMD pathogenesis, it should be complemented with:  
  – Polarized iPSC-derived RPE on synthetic Bruch’s membrane mimics.  
  – Oxidative stress and lipofuscin accumulation models.  
  – Co‐culture systems including choroidal endothelial cells or photoreceptor–RPE organoids.
