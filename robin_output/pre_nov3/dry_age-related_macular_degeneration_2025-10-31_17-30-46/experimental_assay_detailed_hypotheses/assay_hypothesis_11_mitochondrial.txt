Assay Overview  
• Assay goal and disease aspect modelled  
  – Dry age-related macular degeneration (dry AMD) is driven in large part by progressive dysfunction and death of retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells, in which mitochondrial impairment and excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) are central.  
  – This assay simultaneously measures mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) and mitochondrial superoxide production (ROS) in live RPE cells under stress.  
• Readouts and methodology  
  1. TMRE (tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester) fluorescence intensity per cell as a proxy for ΔΨm.  
  2. MitoSOX Red fluorescence intensity per cell as a probe for mitochondrial superoxide.  
  3. High-content imaging (automated confocal or wide-field) in 96-well plates, enabling quantitative, per-cell multiplexed readouts.  
  4. Automated image analysis pipelines extract intensity, morphology, and subcellular localization metrics.  
• Biological material  
  – Human RPE cell models: iPSC-derived RPE (patient or control lines), primary adult donor RPE, or ARPE-19 cells.  
  – Optionally, RPE cells are pre-stressed with known dry AMD–relevant insults (e.g., A2E/lipofuscin precursors, complement components C3a/C5a, low-level H2O2, cigarette smoke extract) to mimic chronic oxidative load.  

Biomedical Evidence  
• Central role of mitochondrial dysfunction & ROS in dry AMD  
  – Post-mortem studies: RPE from AMD donors shows decreased mitochondrial number, disrupted cristae, and increased mtDNA damage (Ferrington et al. Invest Ophthalmol Vis Sci. 2017).  
  – Clinical correlation: Patients with dry AMD display elevated retinal oxidative biomarkers; AREDS antioxidant formulation slows progression, underscoring the oxidative component (Age-Related Eye Disease Study Research Group. Arch Ophthalmol. 2001).  
  – Mechanistic insights: Mitochondrial ROS in RPE triggers NLRP3 inflammasome activation, inflammatory cytokine release, complement dysregulation, and drusen accumulation (Tseng-Incenti et al. J Clin Invest. 2016).  
  – Rodent/Culture models: RPE exposed to A2E and blue light produce excessive mitochondrial ROS, leading to cell death similar to RPE atrophy in dry AMD (Suter et al. Mol Vis. 2000).  

• Why ΔΨm / ROS are high-value endpoints  
  – ΔΨm collapse is an early indicator of mitochondrial depolarization preceding apoptosis.  
  – Mitochondrial superoxide is a proximal effector of mtDNA damage and redox signaling relevant to inflammation and cell death.  
  – Multiplexing these two readouts provides insight into both bioenergetic failure and oxidative injury, two pillars of RPE pathology in dry AMD.  

Previous Use in Drug Discovery  
• Vann et al. (Assay Drug Dev Technol. 2015)  
  – Developed TMRE + MitoSOX high-content assay in neuronal cells to screen mitochondrial protectants. Identified small molecules preserving ΔΨm and reducing ROS under neurotoxic stress.  
• Ferrington et al. (Free Radic Biol Med. 2016)  
  – Applied TMRE imaging in primary human RPE treated with oxidative insults; showed mitochondrial protective effects of SS-31 (elamipretide) in preserving ΔΨm.  
• High-content RPE screening examples  
  – MitoQ and MitoTEMPOL were profiled in ARPE-19 for ROS suppression and viability under H2O2 challenge, correlating with improved phagocytosis capacity (Tanito et al. IOVS. 2012).  
  – NAD+ precursors (NR, NMN) tested in iPSC-RPE using similar ΔΨm/ROS assays showed dose-dependent protection (Cortes et al. Stem Cell Reports. 2018).  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
  – Pathophysiologically relevant: directly quantifies mitochondrial health in RPE, a known dry AMD driver.  
  – High-content, multiplexed: simultaneous ΔΨm and ROS readouts reduce assay-to-assay variability and provide richer mechanistic insight.  
  – Scalable to 96- or 384-well format for moderate to high throughput.  
  – Compatible with human-derived cells (iPSC-RPE, primary RPE), improving translational relevance.  

Weaknesses  
  – Dye limitations: TMRE is sensitive to dye concentration, quenching, and plasma-membrane potential; MitoSOX can react with non-mitochondrial oxidants, potentially confounding specificity.  
  – RPE cell models: ARPE-19 may not fully recapitulate primary RPE metabolism or pigmentation; iPSC-derived lines vary by donor and differentiation protocol.  
  – Simplified context: does not capture complement, drusen formation, or immune cell interactions—key facets of dry AMD beyond mitochondrial stress.  
  – Single-timepoint snapshot: chronic, cumulative mitochondrial damage in vivo may not be fully modelled in acute assay windows.  

Conclusion  
The simultaneous TMRE/MitoSOX high-content assay offers a robust, mechanistically grounded platform to screen for mitochondrial protectants in RPE models of dry AMD. While dye specificity and model choice must be carefully controlled, the assay directly interrogates core pathways of RPE degeneration. Integrating this screen with orthogonal assays (e.g., phagocytosis, inflammasome activation, complement deposition) would strengthen candidate triaging and accelerate discovery of disease-modifying therapeutics.
