Proposal for Nilotinib (Tasigna®, Novartis), a c-Abl tyrosine kinase inhibitor.

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Nilotinib (Tasigna®, Novartis) is a synthetic small molecule inhibitor originally developed as a second‐generation BCR–ABL tyrosine kinase inhibitor for the treatment of chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML). Developed through iterative medicinal chemistry optimization processes that sought to overcome resistance to first‐generation inhibitors such as imatinib, nilotinib is structurally designed to selectively bind the ATP-binding pocket of the c-Abl kinase, thereby inhibiting its catalytic activity (Karim et al., 2020). As a representative member of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), nilotinib falls into the class of targeted anticancer agents that have been widely used in oncology, with extensive preclinical and clinical experience in modulating aberrant kinase signaling pathways. The synthetic origins of nilotinib involve derivatization of earlier BCR–ABL inhibitors to improve potency and specificity, and its development has been a model for the design of targeted agents in diseases driven by kinase dysregulation (Karim et al., 2020). While its initial application has been in CML, its potent inhibition of c-Abl has attracted attention for repurposing in other conditions where this kinase plays a pathological role, including neurodegeneration and, as hypothesized here, dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) (Karim et al., 2020; Fowler et al., 2019).

Therapeutic History:  
Nilotinib has a well-documented clinical history as an FDA-approved therapeutic for CML, where its efficacy and safety profile have been established across numerous clinical trials and long-term follow-up studies. Over the past decade, repurposing efforts have extended into neurodegenerative diseases based on its ability to cross the blood–brain barrier (albeit at modest concentrations) and modulate pathogenic protein aggregation via autophagy enhancement. Preclinical studies in models of α-synucleinopathy, Parkinson’s disease, and Alzheimer’s disease have demonstrated that nilotinib can reduce toxic protein burden, improve autophagic clearance, and rescue neuronal survival by inhibiting c-Abl mediated pathways (Karim et al., 2020). Despite these promising findings in neurological contexts, a systematic search of clinical trial databases reveals that nilotinib has not yet been directly investigated in dry AMD or related retinal degenerative conditions (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.). This lack of prior clinical evaluation in the retinal domain represents both a gap in the evidence base and an opportunity for further translational research, building on the extensive safety and pharmacokinetic data already available from its oncological and neurodegenerative applications (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.; Santos, Ambrósio, & Léger, 2024).

Mechanism of Action:  
The central mechanism of nilotinib is its inhibition of the aberrantly activated c-Abl tyrosine kinase. Under normal conditions, c-Abl regulates various cellular processes, including cell-cycle control, DNA repair, and stress responses. However, in pathological settings such as CML and certain neurodegenerative diseases, c-Abl becomes hyperactivated, leading to detrimental downstream effects including the activation of the tumor suppressor p53 via phosphorylation (Karim et al., 2020). In neurodegenerative models, c-Abl activation has been shown to interfere with autophagy, a critical intracellular degradation process, by upregulating p53 and inhibiting key autophagy initiators such as ULK1 and Beclin1 (Karim et al., 2020). Mechanistic studies in α-synucleinopathy models have demonstrated that nilotinib’s inhibition of c-Abl reduces the phosphorylation of p53 and decreases downstream mTORC1 signaling, thereby releasing autophagic blockades (Karim et al., 2020). Specifically, nilotinib treatment has been shown to enhance autophagic flux through increased conversion of LC3-I to LC3-II and reduced accumulation of the autophagy adaptor p62, indicating improved autophagosome formation and lysosomal clearance (Karim et al., 2020). Moreover, by inhibiting c-Abl, nilotinib alleviates the phosphorylation of ULK1 and enables its activation in an AMPK-dependent manner, which is critical for the initiation of autophagy; there is also emerging evidence that this inhibition may drive the formation or activation of the Beclin1 complex, further promoting autophagosome nucleation (Karim et al., 2020). Thus, nilotinib exerts a dual effect: it directly impedes the kinase activity of c-Abl while indirectly reactivating autophagic pathways that are otherwise suppressed by c-Abl’s pathological signaling via p53 and mTOR (Karim et al., 2020).

Expected Effect:  
Based on the hypothesized role of c-Abl in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) dysfunction, it is anticipated that nilotinib will have several beneficial effects in the context of dry AMD. In RPE cells, chronic oxidative stress and drusen-associated stressors are known to activate c-Abl, leading to the phosphorylation and inhibition of key autophagy regulators such as ULK1 and Beclin1. This interruption of autophagic flux is believed to contribute to lipofuscin accumulation, particularly of A2E, a toxic by-product implicated in the pathogenesis of dry AMD (Kim, Hwang, & Kwon, 2020; Karim et al., 2020). By inhibiting c-Abl, nilotinib should theoretically reverse this block, restore ULK1/Beclin1 activity, and re-establish effective autophagic clearance. In in vitro RPE cell assays that employ stresses such as A2E challenge and oxidative insults, nilotinib is expected to decrease the accumulation of lipofuscin and A2E, increase markers of autophagic flux such as LC3-II, and reduce p62 levels, thereby indicating enhanced lysosomal degradation (Kim, Hwang, & Kwon, 2020; Santos, Ambrósio, & Léger, 2024). With improved autophagic processing, the cellular homeostasis within RPE cells should be restored, thereby preventing the degenerative changes that contribute to drusen formation and the overall progression of dry AMD. Furthermore, animal studies in neurodegenerative models have shown that nilotinib can lead to delayed onset of clinical symptoms and improved cell survival, lending support to the potential translational value of these mechanisms in the retinal environment (Karim et al., 2020). It is important to note that while supportive mechanistic evidence exists from neurodegeneration research, direct experimental validation of nilotinib’s impact on RPE autophagy and lipofuscin clearance in dry AMD-specific models remains to be performed (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.; Santos, Ambrósio, & Léger, 2024).

Overall Evaluation:  
Nilotinib represents a compelling candidate for repurposing in dry age-related macular degeneration due to its well-characterized pharmacological profile, established safety through its use in CML, and promising mechanistic insights from neurodegeneration studies. One of the primary strengths of this candidate is its clear molecular mechanism: by inhibiting c-Abl, nilotinib has the potential to reverse the autophagy blockade mediated by pathological p53 activation and mTOR signaling, a mechanism that is plausibly relevant to the pathology observed in RPE cells under oxidative and drusen stress (Karim et al., 2020). The existing literature strongly supports the notion that c-Abl plays a pivotal role in autophagy regulation, with multiple studies demonstrating that its inhibition leads to enhanced autophagic clearance of toxic protein aggregates in neuronal models (Karim et al., 2020; Kim, Hwang, & Kwon, 2020). This mechanistic similarity is particularly encouraging given that autophagic dysfunction and accumulation of cellular debris such as lipofuscin (and its A2E component) are central to the progression of dry AMD (Kim, Hwang, & Kwon, 2020; Karim et al., 2020).

Another strength is the translational potential based on nilotinib’s pharmacokinetic profile. Despite challenges often associated with drug delivery to the central nervous system and ocular tissues, nilotinib has demonstrated the ability to cross the blood–brain barrier at pharmacologically relevant doses, suggesting that similar (albeit low-dose) penetration of the blood–retina barrier might be achievable. This is supported by the documented systemic administration protocols in preclinical neurodegeneration models where nilotinib exhibited biological activity in brain tissues (Karim et al., 2020), and by extension, such dosing strategies may be adapted for ocular tissues. The fact that nilotinib is already FDA-approved means that its safety profile, dosing parameters, and potential side effects are well understood, which significantly reduces the risk and accelerates the timeline for repurposing in a new indication (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.; Santos, Ambrósio, & Léger, 2024).

However, there are several weaknesses and challenges that must be acknowledged. First, despite the promising mechanistic data in neurodegenerative models, direct evidence for nilotinib’s efficacy in RPE cells and in models of dry AMD is currently lacking. A systematic search of clinical trial databases has revealed no registered trials for nilotinib in the context of AMD, which suggests that while preclinical data in other systems is robust, the translational leap to retinal applications remains uncharted (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.). Moreover, the molecular environment in the retina, particularly within the RPE, may differ in significant ways from neuronal tissues; factors such as differential expression patterns of autophagy regulators, variations in stress response signaling, and the unique barrier properties of the retina may influence nilotinib’s ultimate efficacy (Santos, Ambrósio, & Léger, 2024; Kim, Hwang, & Kwon, 2020).

Another potential concern is the specificity of nilotinib’s activity in the ocular environment. While its primary mechanism is through c-Abl inhibition, nilotinib has been documented to interact with other kinases (such as DDR1/DDR2) at low nanomolar concentrations (Fowler et al., 2019), which could lead to off-target effects that may be undesirable in the delicate architecture of the retina. These off-target interactions could potentially disrupt normal cellular processes within the retina, potentially exacerbating rather than ameliorating retinal degeneration if not carefully controlled (Fowler et al., 2019; Hussain et al., 2019). Additionally, while nilotinib’s safety profile has been demonstrated in systemic oncology settings, the safety and tolerability of chronic nilotinib administration directly affecting ocular tissues have yet to be thoroughly evaluated. Intraocular injections or other targeted delivery methods might be required to achieve therapeutic concentrations in the retina, and these routes carry their own risks of complications, including infection and retinal detachment (Santos, Ambrósio, & Léger, 2024).

In summary, nilotinib offers a conceptually appealing avenue for the treatment of dry AMD by targeting a clear molecular driver—c-Abl mediated autophagy dysfunction—that is implicated in the accumulation of toxic aggregates in RPE cells. The extensive preclinical data from neurodegenerative models provides a strong foundation that supports the hypothesis that nilotinib can restore autophagic flux by reactivating ULK1/Beclin1 function and reducing p53-mediated inhibition (Karim et al., 2020). The fact that nilotinib is already approved for human use and has well-characterized pharmacokinetics and safety data further enhances its attractiveness for repurposing. Nonetheless, significant hurdles remain, most notably the absence of direct evidence in retinal models, potential differences in drug permeability and target engagement in RPE cells, and the risk of off-target effects in ocular tissues. These challenges underscore the need for rigorous preclinical validation in RPE culture systems (e.g., using A2E challenge assays) and well-designed dry AMD animal models before advancing to clinical trials (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.; Santos, Ambrósio, & Léger, 2024).

Overall, given its mechanistic rationale, previous efficacy in related neurodegenerative contexts, and favorable safety profile in oncology, nilotinib stands out as a promising candidate for further exploration as a treatment for dry AMD. Its repurposing offers a high translational value with the potential to address a major unmet medical need, provided that future studies can confirm its ability to cross the blood–retina barrier, restore autophagic processing in RPE cells, and ultimately prevent RPE degeneration without incurring significant ocular toxicity (Fowler et al., 2019; Karim et al., 2020). Continued interdisciplinary research involving detailed biochemical studies, targeted delivery assessments, and in vivo pharmacodynamic evaluations will be critical to fully validate nilotinib’s therapeutic potential in the context of dry AMD.
