Proposal for 3,4-Dimethoxychalcone (DMC; Cayman Chemical catalog #CAY32002), a mTOR-independent TFEB activator.

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
3,4-Dimethoxychalcone (DMC; Cayman Chemical catalog #CAY32002) is a small synthetic chalcone derivative belonging to the flavonoid family that has emerged in preclinical studies as a potent modulator of autophagy and lysosomal biogenesis. Derived through conventional organic synthetic routes involving aldol condensation reactions between appropriately substituted aromatic aldehydes and acetophenones, DMC exhibits a chalcone scaffold decorated with two methoxy groups that enhance its lipophilicity and potential bioactivity. Its chemical simplicity confers excellent drug‐like properties including favorable solubility, ease of synthesis, and established routes for chemical modification, thereby making it an attractive candidate for repurposing in indications such as dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) where manipulation of autophagic flux is desired (Gupta et al., 2023; Carmona-Gutierrez et al., 2019). This compound has been investigated primarily for its ability to stimulate autophagy via transcription factor EB (TFEB) activation, a central regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy that is crucial for cellular clearance mechanisms in postmitotic cells including those in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) (Carmona-Gutierrez et al., 2025; Falcão et al., 2024).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, chalcones have been explored extensively in the context of longevity, anti‐cancer therapies, and metabolic regulation due to their versatile bioactivity profiles. DMC, in particular, has been reported to induce autophagy and promote lysosomal clearance in various cell types, with evidence emerging from studies in both mammalian cell culture systems and invertebrate models such as Caenorhabditis elegans. In one well‐documented study, DMC was shown to promote autophagic flux, leading to improved clearance of protein aggregates and damaged organelles without engaging the classical inhibition of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway—a mechanism that distinguishes it from rapalogs and may reduce associated immunosuppressive liabilities (Gupta et al., 2023; Carmona-Gutierrez et al., 2025). Although most of the pre‐clinical investigations using DMC have focused on its beneficial effects in the context of cardiac injury and age‐related neurodegeneration, these studies provide a strong rationale to consider its repurposing for diseases with an autophagy–lysosomal dysfunction component such as dry AMD (Lu et al., 2021; Falcão et al., 2024). Furthermore, the literature on alternative TFEB activators—such as trehalose and rapamycin—in RPE cells and AMD models underscore the therapeutic promise of targeting TFEB, even though direct reports of DMC use in AMD models remain limited; instead, the extrapolation of its mechanism from other systems supports its evaluation in RPE-specific assays (Falcão et al., 2024; Falcão et al., 2025).

Mechanism of Action:  
The molecular underpinnings of DMC’s activity center on its capacity to induce TFEB dephosphorylation and promote its nuclear translocation in a manner that appears to bypass the canonical mTORC1 inhibitory pathway. Under homeostatic conditions, TFEB is phosphorylated by mTORC1, which sequesters it in the cytoplasm and limits its transcriptional regulation of genes involved in lysosomal and autophagy pathways. In stress situations, however, TFEB can undergo dephosphorylation—commonly mediated by the phosphatase calcineurin—allowing it to translocate into the nucleus, bind to coordinated lysosomal expression and regulation (CLEAR) elements, and upregulate a broad array of lysosomal hydrolases, membrane proteins, and autophagy-related genes (Pan et al., 2019; Falcão et al., 2024). DMC is hypothesized to engage this calcineurin–TFEB axis directly, thereby inducing TFEB dephosphorylation without necessitating upstream mTOR inhibition. This mechanism is particularly advantageous given that it avoids the systemic immunosuppressive effects observed with rapamycin-like compounds that target mTOR directly (Gupta et al., 2023; Carmona-Gutierrez et al., 2025). Importantly, in primary neuronal and epithelial models, treatment with DMC has been reported to increase lysosome number, enhance cathepsin activity, and stimulate autophagic flux, all indicative of robust TFEB activation (Carmona-Gutierrez et al., 2025). On a biochemical level, the compound appears to trigger a signaling cascade that culminates in the dephosphorylation of key regulatory residues on TFEB, thereby relieving its cytoplasmic retention. Once in the nucleus, TFEB binds to CLEAR network promoters, simultaneously inducing genes responsible for lysosomal biogenesis (e.g., LAMP1, Cathepsin D) as well as components of the autophagic machinery, which collectively enhance the cell’s capacity to clear damaged organelles and protein aggregates (Falcão et al., 2024; Falcão et al., 2025). Despite isolated reports suggesting that DMC might also exert mTOR inhibitory effects (as observed in certain cardiovascular studies), the prevailing data supporting an mTOR-independent activation of TFEB confers mechanistic specificity that is critical for its proposed use in dry AMD, where minimizing off-target immunosuppressive effects is essential (Lu et al., 2021; Gupta et al., 2023).

Expected Effect:  
In the context of dry AMD, the RPE is burdened with the accumulation of photo-oxidized bisretinoids such as A2E and lipofuscin granules, which disrupt normal cellular function and eventually lead to cell death. The expected pharmacological effect of DMC in relevant assays centers on its capacity to induce TFEB nuclear translocation in RPE cells, consequently upregulating genes that drive lysosomal biogenesis and autophagic clearance. By enhancing the lysosomal degradation machinery, DMC would ideally promote the removal of toxic aggregates associated with oxidative stress and cellular aging, thus preserving RPE cell viability (Falcão et al., 2024; Falcão et al., 2025). In practical terms, when applied to human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived RPE cells loaded with A2E, DMC is anticipated to reduce intracellular levels of this toxic byproduct along with the associated lipofuscin burden, thereby mitigating cellular stress and preventing cell death. Moreover, the activation of the TFEB pathway is expected to bolster additional cytoprotective responses, including the upregulation of cathepsin activity and other lysosomal hydrolases that contribute to a more efficient degradation of phagocytosed photoreceptor outer segment debris, a function that is crucial for RPE homeostasis (Pan et al., 2019; Falcão et al., 2025). The enhanced autophagic flux facilitated by DMC is also likely to intersect with antioxidative pathways, potentially engaging the NRF2/KEAP1 axis, which further reinforces cellular defenses against oxidative damage—a major contributing factor in AMD pathogenesis (Falcão et al., 2025). Thus, in preclinical assays and future in vivo dry AMD models, DMC should manifest as a compound that not only clears deleterious aggregates but also restores overall lysosomal function and cellular homeostasis without the deleterious side effects often associated with mTOR inhibitors (Falcão et al., 2024; Pan et al., 2019).

Overall Evaluation:  
The evaluation of 3,4-Dimethoxychalcone (DMC) as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD presents several compelling strengths alongside certain challenges that must be critically addressed through further research. Among the primary strengths is DMC’s ability to modulate the TFEB pathway in a manner that is claimed to be independent of mTOR inhibition. This mTOR-independent mechanism offers a significant advantage over traditional mTOR inhibitors such as rapamycin, as it theoretically minimizes the risk of systemic immunosuppression and other mTOR-related side effects while still harnessing the potent autophagy-enhancing and lysosome-boosting effects mediated by TFEB (Gupta et al., 2023; Carmona-Gutierrez et al., 2025). Additionally, the favorable physicochemical properties of DMC—including its small molecular size, established synthetic routes, and amenable solubility profile—facilitate its potential development as a therapeutic agent for ocular applications where drug delivery and tissue penetration into the retina are critical considerations (Carmona-Gutierrez et al., 2019). The compound’s demonstrated capacity to increase lysosomal number, stimulate cathepsin activity, and elevate autophagic flux in other cell types further supports its viability as a candidate for clearing chronic intracellular accumulations characteristic of dry AMD (Falcão et al., 2024).

From a biochemical standpoint, the targeted activation of the calcineurin–TFEB axis by DMC aligns well with current therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring cellular homeostasis in degenerative diseases. By promoting nuclear translocation of TFEB without broadly inhibiting mTOR, DMC could specifically upregulate genes involved not only in lysosomal biogenesis but also in general autophagic processing, thereby addressing both the accumulation of toxic aggregates (such as A2E and lipofuscin granules) and the compromised cellular clearance pathways in the RPE (Pan et al., 2019; Falcão et al., 2025). Moreover, the potential engagement of downstream protective pathways—possibly including cross-talk with the NRF2-mediated antioxidative response—accentuates DMC’s promise as a dual-action compound that simultaneously mitigates oxidative stress and improves proteostasis in retinal cells (Falcão et al., 2025).

However, notable weaknesses and uncertainties remain that merit cautious consideration. First, although DMC has been shown in various experimental models to enhance autophagic and lysosomal functions, direct evidence supporting its efficacy specifically in RPE cells or in animal models of dry AMD is currently limited. Most reports detailing its mechanism originate from studies in cardiac and neuronal contexts, leaving a gap in replication and validation within ocular tissues. This translational gap necessitates rigorous preclinical testing using human iPSC-derived RPE cells and established dry AMD animal models to confirm that the TFEB activation observed in other systems translates effectively to the RPE environment (Ghosh et al., 2024; Ghosh et al., 2023).

Another challenge lies in the mechanistic ambiguity surrounding whether DMC acts entirely in an mTOR-independent fashion. While several studies extol its ability to activate TFEB without direct mTOR suppression—thus avoiding the side effects of mTOR inhibitors—other reports have suggested that related compounds may exhibit some degree of mTOR inhibition, which could potentially complicate the pharmacological profile of DMC (Lu et al., 2021; Gupta et al., 2023). This discrepancy underscores the need for detailed mechanistic studies using RPE-specific models to delineate the precise signaling cascades engaged by DMC, including assessment of calcineurin activity, TFEB dephosphorylation status, and the downstream transcriptional activation of CLEAR network genes (Gupta et al., 2023; Carmona-Gutierrez et al., 2025).

Furthermore, while the proposed benefits of enhanced autophagic flux and lysosomal biogenesis are clear, the potential for off-target effects, long-term toxicity, and issues related to drug delivery in the ocular environment must be thoroughly evaluated. Ocular pharmacokinetics can differ markedly from systemic profiles, and the challenges associated with ensuring adequate retinal bioavailability—particularly in the context of the blood–retina barrier—should not be underestimated. In addition, the possible influence of DMC on other cellular pathways, such as those governing mitochondrial function and metabolic regulation, may introduce additional layers of complexity when considering chronic treatment regimens for a slowly progressing condition like dry AMD (Ghosh et al., 2024; Gupta et al., 2023).

In terms of translational potential, DMC’s repurposing for dry AMD would benefit from head-to-head comparisons with other TFEB activators that have been studied in RPE cells, such as trehalose and rapamycin. While these agents have already demonstrated beneficial effects on lipofuscin clearance and oxidative stress reduction in experimental settings, the distinct advantage of DMC may reside in its ability to engage the TFEB pathway without the collateral effects on mTOR, thereby offering a cleaner safety profile. Nevertheless, systematic studies comparing dose–response relationships, pharmacodynamics, and potential synergistic effects with other autophagy modulators will be essential prior to advancing DMC into clinical development (Falcão et al., 2024; Falcão et al., 2025).

An additional consideration is the heterogeneity present among dry AMD patients. Genetic factors, such as the presence of the CFH Y402H variant, and environmental stressors can influence the progression and severity of RPE degeneration. It will be critical to establish whether DMC’s TFEB-mediated effects are robust across different RPE phenotypes and whether any differential responses may necessitate patient stratification in future clinical trials. The potential for DMC to rescue autophagy-related gene expression and lysosomal function, as suggested by similar findings with TFEB activators in other models, provides a strong impetus for such studies, but careful experimental design will be paramount (Ghosh et al., 2024; Ghosh et al., 2023).

Overall, the repurposing of 3,4-Dimethoxychalcone as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD is supported by a solid rationale predicated on its capacity to activate TFEB and enhance autophagic and lysosomal processes in a manner that is potentially independent of mTOR inhibition. Its chemical properties, ease of synthesis, and preliminary evidence from related experimental models collectively argue in favor of its evaluation in RPE-specific assays and animal models of dry AMD. At the same time, key challenges—including the need for clear demonstration of its mTOR-independent mechanism in ocular tissues, comprehensive toxicity profiling, and optimization of drug delivery strategies—must be addressed in subsequent preclinical studies. Given the robust body of literature supporting the critical role of TFEB in maintaining RPE health and mitigating lipofuscin accumulation, DMC stands out as a promising candidate that could offer a novel approach to the treatment of dry AMD, provided that future research confirms its efficacy and safety in the retinal context (Ghosh et al., 2023; Ghosh et al., 2024; Gupta et al., 2023).

In conclusion, the current comprehensive literature review suggests that 3,4-Dimethoxychalcone exhibits significant potential as a repurposed drug candidate for dry AMD. Its unique mechanism of inducing TFEB dephosphorylation and promoting lysosomal biogenesis—thereby enhancing autophagic clearance of photo-oxidized bisretinoids and lipofuscin granules—addresses a fundamental pathogenic process in RPE degeneration. While the preclinical evidence on DMC from non-ocular models is compelling, rigorous validation in RPE-specific systems is essential to definitively establish its role in modulating autophagy and improving retinal cell survival. The strengths of DMC in terms of mechanistic specificity, favorable pharmacological properties, and avoidance of mTOR-related side effects make it a strong candidate for further development, while the challenges associated with direct translation to ocular models underscore the need for carefully designed preclinical studies. As such, the proposed direction for our drug development effort should focus on detailed in vitro assays using human iPSC-derived RPE cells, followed by in vivo evaluation in dry AMD animal models, to fully delineate the therapeutic potential and safety profile of DMC for clinical application in dry AMD (Gupta et al., 2023; Carmona-Gutierrez et al., 2025; Falcão et al., 2024; Pan et al., 2019).
