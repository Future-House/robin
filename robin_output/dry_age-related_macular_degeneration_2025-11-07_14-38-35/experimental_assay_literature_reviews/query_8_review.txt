Query: review comparative advantages and limitations of primary human RPE, immortalized RPE cell lines, and iPSC-derived RPE cells for modeling dry age-related macular degeneration, including differentiation protocols, functional maturity, and reproducibility of key disease phenotypes in vitro

Below is a side-by-side comparison of three in vitro human RPE sources commonly used to model dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD). For each we summarize (1) origin and differentiation approach, (2) functional maturity, (3) reproducibility of key AMD phenotypes, and (4) major advantages and limitations.

1. Primary Human RPE  
• Origin / “Differentiation”  
  – Isolated postmortem or from cadaveric donor eyes (age-matched, AMD vs non-AMD).  
  – Enzymatic digestion (dispase, trypsin) followed by selective adhesion. No pluripotent‐to‐RPE differentiation required.  
• Functional maturity  
  – Near-native morphology: cobblestone monolayer, high pigmentation, apical microvilli.  
  – Transepithelial resistance (TEER) routinely >500 Ω·cm².  
  – Robust phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments (POS), polarized VEGF/A secretion, tight junctions (ZO-1), melanin synthesis, normal complement factor expression.  
• Reproducibility of AMD phenotypes  
  – Can capture donor-specific features: drusen deposits in long-term culture, complement dysregulation, lipofuscin accumulation.  
  – High inter-donor variability in baseline function, donor age and disease stage strongly influence phenotype.  
• Advantages  
  – Gold standard for mature RPE physiology.  
  – Best recapitulation of native RPE gene expression and morphology.  
• Limitations  
  – Limited supply, high cost, ethical/regulatory hurdles.  
  – Short life span in vitro (typically <5 passages), pronounced donor-to-donor variability.  
  – Low throughput; challenging for large drug screens.

2. Immortalized RPE Cell Lines (e.g., ARPE-19, hTERT-RPE1, D407)  
• Origin / Differentiation  
  – ARPE-19: spontaneously immortalized from adult donor; D407 from human RPE tumor; hTERT-RPE1 via telomerase transduction.  
  – Standard culture: DMEM:F12 + 10% FBS on plastic. To induce partial differentiation: low serum (1%), Matrigel or ECM coating, nicotinamide (10 mM), Activin A (100 ng/mL), extended culture (4–6 weeks).  
• Functional maturity  
  – Moderate polarization: TEER ~50–150 Ω·cm² (can be pushed to ~200 Ω·cm² with prolonged differentiation).  
  – Lower phagocytic index (20–50% of primary), incomplete tight junction formation, reduced melanin, attenuated polarized secretion.  
• Reproducibility of AMD phenotypes  
  – Readily model generic stress pathways (oxidative stress, inflammation, autophagy), complement activation upon cytokine treatment.  
  – Poor recapitulation of drusen‐like deposits or true lipofuscin; limited genotype-dependent phenotypes.  
• Advantages  
  – Unlimited expansion, low cost, high reproducibility between labs.  
  – Easy genetic manipulation (CRISPR, over-expression, siRNA).  
• Limitations  
  – Dedifferentiated phenotype limits disease relevance.  
  – Genomic abnormalities (aneuploidy) may confound signaling.  
  – Not ideal for modeling RPE-specific barrier or advanced AMD hallmarks.

3. iPSC-Derived RPE  
• Origin / Differentiation  
  – Patient- or control-derived iPSCs differentiated via either:  
     1) Spontaneous pigmented patch isolation (30–60 days), or  
     2) Directed protocols (Wnt inhibition + Activin A; defined media such as X VIVO™10 or mTeSR1 with nicotinamide, IGF-1) over 60–90 days.  
  – Purification by manual selection or magnetic sorting (e.g. CD121a).  
• Functional maturity  
  – Achieve cobblestone morphology, TEER ~200–300 Ω·cm².  
  – Phagocytosis at ~60–80% of primary RPE levels.  
  – Express RPE markers (RPE65, BEST1, CRALBP), secrete polarized VEGF/A.  
• Reproducibility of AMD phenotypes  
  – Patient‐specific lines recapitulate risk-allele phenotypes: increased complement activation (CFH risk), drusen‐like subRPE deposits in extended culture, heightened oxidative stress susceptibility.  
  – Batch-to-batch and line-to-line variability; epigenetic memory can affect differentiation efficiency.  
• Advantages  
  – Unlimited, genotype-specific (“disease in a dish”) modeling.  
  – Amenable to CRISPR correction/introduction of AMD risk variants.  
  – Better physiologic relevance than immortalized lines.  
• Limitations  
  – Long, labor-intensive differentiation (2–3 months).  
  – Higher costs, specialized expertise required.  
  – Variability in maturation state and functional readouts across iPSC lines.

Summary of Key Trade-Offs  
• Scalability vs. physiological fidelity: Immortalized lines excel in throughput but fall short on mature RPE functions; primary RPE are physiologically closest but low throughput; iPSC-RPE strike an intermediate, offering disease-specificity at moderate throughput.  
• Disease modeling: Only primary and iPSC-RPE faithfully model drusen-like deposits, complement dysregulation, lipofuscin; immortalized lines are best for generic stress/inflammation assays.  
• Cost/time: Primary RPE require minimal differentiation time but have sourcing constraints; iPSC-RPE demand months and skilled staff; immortalized lines are rapid, low‐cost.  
• Reproducibility: Highest with immortalized lines; primary RPE show high donor variance; iPSC-RPE reproducibility depends on protocol standardization and line characterization.

Choosing the right model depends on the specific question:  
– For target validation of RPE-specific pathways (phagocytosis, barrier integrity, drusen formation), use primary or well-characterized iPSC-RPE.  
– For high-throughput screening of small molecules against oxidative‐stress or inflammatory readouts, ARPE-19 or hTERT-RPE1 are most practical.  
– For genotype–phenotype correlation and personalized AMD studies, invest in iPSC-RPE with isogenic controls.
