Proposal for MitoQ (Sigma MitoQ; Cayman #18010)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
MitoQ (mitoquinone) is a synthetic, mitochondria-targeted antioxidant generated by covalently linking a ubiquinone headgroup to a decyl triphenylphosphonium (TPP⁺) cation, yielding a delocalized lipophilic cation that partitions across biological membranes and accumulates several-hundredfold inside the mitochondrial matrix driven by the large negative inner mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) (Chico et al., 2016; Smith & Murphy, 2010; Fields et al., 2023). Within mitochondria, MitoQ adsorbs predominantly to the matrix-facing leaflet of the inner mitochondrial membrane where the ubiquinone moiety is reduced by the respiratory chain to the corresponding hydroquinone (mitoquinol), which then acts as a chain-breaking antioxidant that intercepts lipid peroxyl radicals and limits cardiolipin and phospholipid peroxidation in situ (Smith & Murphy, 2010; Vlachantoni et al., 2011). The TPP⁺-driven mitochondrial targeting strategy (also used by related agents such as SkQ1, decylTPP conjugates, and TPP-Niacin) was devised to overcome the poor organelle-level bioavailability and off-target scavenging that limited earlier, non-targeted antioxidants in clinical translation (Fields et al., 2023).

The compound and its class were developed and extensively characterized by Murphy and colleagues, including in vitro uptake/antioxidant activity, radiolabeled biodistribution, and oral tablet formulations suitable for animal toxicology and human dosing (Smith & Murphy, 2010). After oral dosing, MitoQ exhibits low but measurable bioavailability (~10%), undergoes first-pass metabolism (intestinal wall and liver), and is excreted as unchanged drug and conjugated metabolites of the reduced quinol form (glucuronides/sulfates), with phase I human pharmacokinetics showing a Cmax ≈33 ng/mL at ~1 hour after an 80 mg dose (Zinovkin & Zamyatnin, 2019; Smith & Murphy, 2010). Long-term oral administration in rodents was tolerated at 500 μM in drinking water without overt toxicity; quantitative LC/MS/MS confirms tissue distribution to heart, liver, brain, and other organs over weeks to months (Smith & Murphy, 2010). In a retinal context, oral MitoQ accumulates in mouse retina to steady-state levels on the order of ~1.3 nmol/g wet tissue (with no retinal toxicity in wild-type mice at 500 μM dosing), establishing biological access to ocular tissues via systemic exposure (Vlachantoni et al., 2011).

MitoQ belongs to the broader class of mitochondria-targeted quinones (short-chain quinones conjugated to lipophilic cations), which includes plastoquinone analogs such as SkQ1 that share the same TPP⁺ carrier but differ in the redox headgroup and, potentially, in reactivity and recycling kinetics within mitochondria (Chico et al., 2016; Baksheeva et al., 2018). This class has been deployed across neurological, cardiovascular, inflammatory, and hepatic models, with mixed clinical translation; importantly for ophthalmology, SkQ1 has advanced to ocular topical formulations (Visomitin) with evidence of retinal protection in animal models of light-induced damage and age-related retinopathy, illustrating that TPP⁺-conjugated antioxidants can reach and act within retinal tissues when appropriately delivered (Baksheeva et al., 2018; Grigoryan et al., 2012; Lukashev et al., 2014).

Therapeutic History  
Preclinical and clinical experience with MitoQ spans multiple organ systems and disease models. In animal studies, orally administered MitoQ has shown protective effects in models of ischemia-reperfusion, endotoxin/sepsis, and neurodegeneration, consistent with its mitochondrial antioxidant mechanism and tissue distribution profile (Lukashev et al., 2014; Fields et al., 2023). For example, MitoQ decreased inflammatory cytokines and liver enzyme elevations in sepsis models, reduced neurological deficits in an Alzheimer’s transgenic mouse model, and modestly prolonged survival with improved pathology in the SOD1-G93A ALS mouse model when given chronically in drinking water (Lukashev et al., 2014). Additional preclinical reports include beneficial effects in diabetic kidney disease via Nrf2/PINK1-associated mitophagy and in sensorineural hearing loss driven by mitochondrial ROS, supporting broad bioactivity against mitochondrial oxidative injury across tissues (Shyam et al., 2021).

Human experience includes long-term oral dosing in Parkinson’s disease (PD) patients (40–80 mg/day for 12 months) without slowing disease progression but providing important safety data for year-long administration, as well as shorter trials demonstrating improved vascular function and reduced oxidative stress biomarkers or decreased hepatic transaminases in chronic hepatitis C without antiviral benefit (Smith & Murphy, 2010; Zinovkin & Zamyatnin, 2019; Mason et al., 2022). Across randomized studies of mitochondria-targeted antioxidants, MitoQ was generally well tolerated (with occasional non-serious gastrointestinal adverse events), but the evidence base remains short-term and of low-to-very-low certainty for many endpoints (Mason et al., 2022). Crucially for AMD, no registered human clinical trials testing MitoQ in age-related macular degeneration were identified on ClinicalTrials.gov, indicating an absence of clinical translational activity in this indication to date (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.).

Ocular and retinal history is mixed. On the positive side, MitoQ is orally bioavailable to the retina in mice at nanomole-per-gram levels without overt retinal toxicity in wild-type animals (outer nuclear layer thickness unchanged; no TUNEL-positive photoreceptor cells), supporting general ocular tolerability and access (Vlachantoni et al., 2011). In vitro, MitoQ (as a mitochondria-targeted antioxidant) has been reported to protect RPE cells from blue light–induced oxidative stress, providing cell-level plausibility in a phototoxic paradigm relevant to RPE oxidative injury (Jarrett & Boulton, 2012). However, in an independent oxidative RPE insult model (NaIO₃ in hTERT RPE-1), MitoQ failed to rescue cells from mitochondrial depolarization and apoptosis, whereas a CD36 ligand (MPE-001) that enhanced autophagy did prevent cell death, directly documenting a negative result for MitoQ in this RPE oxidative-stress paradigm (Dorion et al., 2021).

In vivo retinal degeneration models have been largely negative for MitoQ efficacy. Multiple inherited photoreceptor degeneration (IPD) mouse models—Pde6brd1/rd1, Pde6batrd1/atrd1, Rho⁻/⁻, and Prph2rds/rds—showed no mitigation of photoreceptor loss or apoptosis with oral MitoQ; outer nuclear layer degeneration progressed on schedule, and TUNEL-positive cell counts were unaffected by treatment (Vlachantoni et al., 2011). Mechanistic biomarker analyses in these models also failed to show improvements in Complex I activity or reduced glutathione (GSH), consistent with a lack of impact on core mitochondrial oxidative endpoints in this setting (Vlachantoni et al., 2011). These negative outcomes likely reflect the mismatch between MitoQ’s primary antioxidant chemistry and the dominant ROS species present in these models (see Mechanism of Action), as well as disease-specific drivers beyond ROS that dominate in fast-progressing genetic photoreceptor loss (Vlachantoni et al., 2011).

By contrast, SkQ1—another TPP⁺-linked mitochondrial antioxidant with a plastoquinone headgroup—has repeatedly shown retinal protection in animal models, including light-induced retinal damage in rabbits (preservation of photoreceptors, suppression of H₂O₂ accumulation, inhibition of arrestin disulfide dimerization, improvement of antioxidant capacity; topical administration achieved nanomolar retinal levels) and prevention of RPE atrophy and retinopathy in senescence-accelerated OXYS rats (systemic dosing) (Baksheeva et al., 2018; Tong et al., 2022). SkQ1 also protected RPE and choroid structure in long-term organotypic rat posterior eyecup cultures at nanomolar concentrations, reducing apoptosis and preventing RPE detachment from Bruch’s membrane (Grigoryan et al., 2012). Beyond preclinical studies, SkQ1 has been formulated as an ophthalmic solution (Visomitin) and advanced into human trials for dry eye and ocular indications, demonstrating the feasibility of TPP⁺-linked antioxidants in topical ocular delivery; although not directly probative for AMD, this trajectory indicates class-level translational potential with appropriate formulation (Lukashev et al., 2014).

This body of work positions MitoQ as mechanistically plausible for mitigating mitochondrial oxidative damage in RPE and retina, with retinal access established in mice, but with mixed-to-negative efficacy in photoreceptor degeneration models and at least one negative RPE oxidative injury study; critically, there is no published evidence of MitoQ improving RPE barrier function endpoints (TEER, ZO-1/occludin) or preventing RPE atrophy in AMD-like models in vivo (Vlachantoni et al., 2011; Dorion et al., 2021).

Mechanism of Action  
MitoQ exploits the Δψm-dependent electrophoretic uptake of the TPP⁺ carrier to concentrate within mitochondria, where the ubiquinone moiety is reduced by the electron transport chain (predominantly at Complex II) to the hydroquinone mitoquinol, which is the active antioxidant species (Smith & Murphy, 2010; Gueven et al., 2017). The quinol intercalates into the inner membrane lipid bilayer, where it donates hydrogen atoms to lipid peroxyl radicals, terminating chain propagation in lipid peroxidation and thereby preserving mitochondrial membrane integrity and function; it also reduces reactive nitrogen species such as peroxynitrite-derived intermediates in membrane environments (Smith & Murphy, 2010). After oxidation back to ubiquinone, MitoQ is recycled by the respiratory chain to maintain antioxidant capacity, making it a “rechargeable” mitochondrial antioxidant under conditions where electron flow remains sufficient (Zinovkin & Zamyatnin, 2019).

Two mechanistic constraints are salient for RPE/retinal applications:

- ROS selectivity: MitoQ shows negligible direct reactivity toward hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and has limited ability to decrease mitochondrial superoxide generation; its predominant efficacy is in lipid radical interception within membranes, not in neutralizing aqueous ROS such as H₂O₂ (Smith & Murphy, 2010; Vlachantoni et al., 2011). Under some conditions, MitoQ or the TPP⁺ moiety can exhibit pro-oxidant behavior, increasing ROS in endothelial cells and isolated mitochondria, underscoring context-dependent redox outcomes (Gueven et al., 2017).

- Dependence on Δψm and ETC activity: Uptake and recycling of MitoQ rely on sustained mitochondrial membrane potential and electron transport activity; in severely depolarized RPE mitochondria (for example, after NaIO₃ injury or intense oxidant exposure), mitochondrial accumulation and bioactivation to the quinol may be compromised (Gueven et al., 2017).

In retinal phototoxicity and AMD-relevant oxidative stress, mitochondrial ROS from photoreceptors and RPE are central drivers, with H₂O₂ acting as a major mediator of phototoxicity; oxidative protein modifications such as arrestin disulfide dimerization and lipid peroxidation products (e.g., malondialdehyde) are robust biomarkers in illuminated retina (Baksheeva et al., 2018). In rabbit light-induced retinal damage, a mitochondria-targeted TPP⁺ antioxidant (SkQ1) suppressed H₂O₂ accumulation, prevented protein oxidation, reduced lipid peroxidation, and improved antioxidant enzyme dynamics (AOA, GPx), consistent with neutralizing mitochondrial sources of ROS and stabilizing redox homeostasis in retinal cells (Baksheeva et al., 2018). The success of SkQ1 in H₂O₂-centric retinal injury contexts may reflect differences in redox kinetics or membrane interactions of the plastoquinone headgroup compared to ubiquinone in MitoQ, or differences in delivery (topical eye drops achieving local retinal exposure) (Baksheeva et al., 2018).

For RPE barrier integrity specifically, tight junction proteins (ZO-1, occludin) and TEER are degraded by oxidative stressors including H₂O₂, blue light in A2E-loaded RPE, and NaIO₃; mitochondrial dysfunction (Δψm loss, increased mitochondrial ROS, impaired ATP generation) is causally linked to RPE injury in these paradigms (Tong et al., 2022). Mitochondria-targeted antioxidants can restore mitochondrial membrane potential and limit mitochondrial ROS in RPE cells, as shown with TPP-Niacin in H₂O₂-challenged ARPE-19, which preserved Δψm, reduced ROS, and upregulated antioxidant and mitochondrial biogenesis programs, albeit without reported barrier readouts (Kim et al., 2021; Tong et al., 2022). Related TPP-conjugates (e.g., TPP-D-NAC) demonstrated selective quenching of mitochondrial ROS in RPE and established an experimental pipeline for assessing RPE bioenergetics, mitochondrial ROS, and structural endpoints that would be directly applicable to evaluating MitoQ (Datta et al., 2023).

Taken together, MitoQ’s molecular pharmacology—robust lipid peroxidation interception when mitochondria are polarized, limited H₂O₂ reactivity, dependence on ETC for recycling—supports a priori efficacy in oxidative paradigms dominated by lipid radical propagation and intact Δψm, while suggesting reduced performance in H₂O₂-dominant or severely depolarizing insults (Smith & Murphy, 2010; Vlachantoni et al., 2011; Gueven et al., 2017).

Expected Effect  
The proposed hypothesis is that MitoQ will accumulate in RPE mitochondria to scavenge mitochondrial superoxide–derived lipid peroxyl radicals, preventing oxidative degradation of tight junction proteins (ZO-1/occludin) and restoring TEER in A2E- or H₂O₂-challenged monolayers. Mechanistically, if Δψm is maintained sufficiently to support TPP⁺-driven uptake and ETC recycling, mitoquinol should suppress membrane lipid peroxidation and secondary oxidative cascades, indirectly preserving junctional complexes that are susceptible to ROS-driven damage (Smith & Murphy, 2010; Gueven et al., 2017). This expectation is supported in part by prior in vitro RPE photostress data where MitoQ afforded protection against blue light–induced oxidative stress, a model in which A2E photosensitization generates lipid peroxidation and singlet oxygen that could be intercepted effectively by a membrane-localized quinol antioxidant (Jarrett & Boulton, 2012; Tong et al., 2022). In such A2E/blue-light paradigms, measuring reductions in lipid peroxidation endpoints (e.g., MDA/4-HNE) and preservation of ZO-1/occludin belts would be consistent with MitoQ’s established lipid radical scavenging capacity (Smith & Murphy, 2010; Tong et al., 2022).

By contrast, in H₂O₂-dominant paradigms (e.g., acute H₂O₂ challenge), the compound’s negligible direct reactivity toward H₂O₂ and reliance on Δψm for accumulation predict weaker effects, especially if H₂O₂ exposure collapses Δψm and impairs recycling of MitoQ to mitoquinol (Smith & Murphy, 2010; Gueven et al., 2017). Indeed, in an NaIO₃ oxidative injury model in hTERT RPE-1 cells—a paradigm with mitochondrial depolarization and apoptosis—MitoQ did not rescue cell viability, underscoring this mechanistic limitation in certain RPE oxidative injuries (Dorion et al., 2021). Therefore, for the H₂O₂ arm of the proposed assay, any TEER restoration or preservation of ZO-1/occludin is less certain a priori and should be interpreted alongside direct mitochondrial readouts (Δψm by TMRM, mitochondrial ROS by MitoSOX, ATP, spare respiratory capacity) and lipid peroxidation metrics to delineate the operative ROS pathways (Datta et al., 2023; Tong et al., 2022).

Assay design considerations that align with the compound’s mechanistic strengths include:

- Using A2E-loaded ARPE-19 or primary human RPE under blue-light exposure to generate a membrane-lipid–centric oxidative insult and monitoring junctional integrity (ZO-1/occludin immunostaining), TEER, MDA/4-HNE, and mitochondrial function (Δψm, OCR by Seahorse), with MitoQ pretreatment titrated across a sub-cytotoxic range (proposed in vitro non-toxicity up to 10 μM in some systems) (Jarrett & Boulton, 2012; Gueven et al., 2017; Datta et al., 2023).

- Including positive mitochondrial antioxidant controls from the TPP⁺ class with stronger retinal/RPE evidence (e.g., SkQ1) to benchmark effects on oxidative markers and junctional integrity, recognizing that SkQ1 has demonstrated in vivo retinal protection and topical ocular bioavailability (Baksheeva et al., 2018).

- Incorporating NaIO₃ as a severe oxidative RPE stressor to test the boundaries of MitoQ efficacy relative to autophagy-restoring agents (e.g., CD36 ligand MPE-001), given the prior negative result in NaIO₃-challenged hTERT RPE-1 cells (Dorion et al., 2021).

- Emphasizing lipid peroxidation readouts in addition to TEER/ZO-1 because MitoQ’s most robust and direct antioxidant activity is at the level of lipid radical chain termination (Smith & Murphy, 2010).

In vivo, the most directly relevant AMD-like model for RPE mitochondrial oxidative stress is the conditional RPE Sod2 knockout mouse, which recapitulates progressive RPE dysfunction with photoreceptor loss and choroidal changes; this model provides a suitable platform to test whether chronic MitoQ can stabilize mitochondrial redox status, preserve RPE structure, and protect photoreceptors, while carefully monitoring retinal function and structure (Mao et al., 2014). Given the earlier negative outcomes in fast photoreceptor-degeneration mutants (rd1, etc.), any in vivo testing should prioritize RPE-centric oxidative stress models rather than photoreceptor-centric genetic degeneration (Vlachantoni et al., 2011; Mao et al., 2014).

Parallel formulation and delivery research should address MitoQ’s low aqueous solubility and micelle formation to enable ocular delivery if systemic exposure proves insufficient, learning from SkQ1 topical development but recognizing the physicochemical differences between ubiquinone and plastoquinone analogs (Zinovkin & Zamyatnin, 2019; Lukashev et al., 2014).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths

- Strong mechanistic rationale for mitochondrial targeting in RPE/retina: Mitochondria are a major ROS source in RPE and photoreceptors, and mitochondrial oxidative stress is causally linked to RPE degeneration, photoreceptor loss, and AMD pathobiology; targeting antioxidants to mitochondria is a coherent strategy to limit oxidative injury at its source (Tong et al., 2022; Mao et al., 2014). The TPP⁺ carrier ensures several-hundredfold mitochondrial accumulation of MitoQ under polarized conditions, concentrating antioxidant capacity in the organelle most relevant to RPE oxidative damage (Chico et al., 2016; Fields et al., 2023).

- Documented retinal exposure and systemic tolerability: Oral MitoQ accumulates in mouse retina without retinal toxicity at doses producing steady-state nanomole-per-gram retinal levels; chronic oral administration in rodents and year-long oral dosing in humans are generally well tolerated, supporting translational feasibility from a safety perspective (Vlachantoni et al., 2011; Smith & Murphy, 2010; Mason et al., 2022).

- Class precedent for ocular efficacy: SkQ1, a closely related TPP⁺-linked plastoquinone, demonstrates robust retinal protection in light-induced injury and age-related retinopathy models and achieves retinal bioavailability via topical eye drops, underscoring that the TPP⁺-mitochondrial antioxidant modality can succeed in ocular tissues when appropriately matched to the injury context and delivery route (Baksheeva et al., 2018; Tong et al., 2022; Lukashev et al., 2014).

- RPE in vitro plausibility under photostress: Literature indicates that MitoQ protects RPE from blue light–induced oxidative stress, consistent with efficacy in A2E/blue-light paradigms where lipid peroxidation is a key damage pathway that mitoquinol can intercept in membranes (Jarrett & Boulton, 2012; Tong et al., 2022; Smith & Murphy, 2010).

Weaknesses and Risks

- Mixed/negative retinal efficacy data in vivo: MitoQ failed to protect photoreceptors in multiple inherited degeneration models, with no improvement in apoptosis, ONL thickness, Complex I activity, or retinal GSH, indicating that in at least some retinal pathologies MitoQ does not deliver functional benefit (Vlachantoni et al., 2011).

- Direct negative RPE oxidative result: In hTERT RPE-1 NaIO₃ injury, MitoQ did not rescue mitochondrial depolarization or cell death, whereas autophagy restoration via a CD36 ligand was effective, highlighting a boundary condition where MitoQ’s dependence on Δψm and limited H₂O₂ reactivity may undermine efficacy (Dorion et al., 2021).

- ROS selectivity constraints: MitoQ is a potent chain-breaking antioxidant for membrane lipid peroxidation but reacts negligibly with H₂O₂ and does not suppress superoxide generation; H₂O₂ is a major mediator of light-induced phototoxicity in retina, and RPE oxidative injury often features elevated H₂O₂, suggesting that MitoQ may be less effective in H₂O₂-centric insults unless upstream mitochondrial ROS generation and lipid peroxidation dominate (Smith & Murphy, 2010; Vlachantoni et al., 2011; Baksheeva et al., 2018).

- Delivery/formulation uncertainties for ocular use: Although oral dosing achieves retinal levels in mice, the absolute concentrations, human ocular PK, and durability required for AMD are undefined; MitoQ’s low aqueous solubility and propensity to form micelles complicate formulation, and no ocular/topical PK data are available for MitoQ (as opposed to SkQ1) (Zinovkin & Zamyatnin, 2019; Vlachantoni et al., 2011; Smith & Murphy, 2010). There are no registered clinical trials of MitoQ for AMD (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.).

- Potential context-dependent pro-oxidant effects: Reports of increased ROS in endothelial cells and isolated mitochondria under certain conditions suggest a non-zero risk of paradoxical redox effects, mandating careful dose-ranging and redox biomarker monitoring in RPE systems (Gueven et al., 2017).

Evidence Gaps for the Proposed Hypothesis (TEER/ZO-1 Rescue)

- No published data were identified that directly demonstrate MitoQ preserves ZO-1/occludin localization or restores TEER in ARPE-19 or primary human RPE under A2E/blue-light, H₂O₂, or NaIO₃ challenge; extant RPE data are limited to blue light–induced oxidative protection without barrier metrics and a negative NaIO₃ rescue (Jarrett & Boulton, 2012; Dorion et al., 2021). In vitro work with related TPP-conjugates (TPP-Niacin) shows rescue of mitochondrial function and ROS but did not report barrier outcomes, reinforcing that barrier-protective efficacy remains unproven for this class in RPE (Kim et al., 2021; Tong et al., 2022).

Go/No-Go Assessment and Development Path

- Go decision for rigorous in vitro PoC restricted to A2E/blue-light paradigms: Based on the mechanistic alignment of MitoQ with lipid peroxidation–dominated photostress, we recommend focused evaluation in A2E-loaded RPE under controlled blue-light exposure with comprehensive readouts including TEER, ZO-1/occludin belt integrity, mitochondrial function (Δψm, OCR), mitochondrial ROS (MitoSOX), and lipid peroxidation (MDA/4-HNE), benchmarking against SkQ1 where feasible (Jarrett & Boulton, 2012; Smith & Murphy, 2010; Tong et al., 2022; Baksheeva et al., 2018).

- Conditional stop for H₂O₂- and NaIO₃-dominant paradigms unless robust signals emerge: Given MitoQ’s poor H₂O₂ reactivity and the negative NaIO₃ result, we advise deprioritizing these models for primary PoC, or using them as stress-test arms to define boundaries of efficacy rather than as primary success criteria (Smith & Murphy, 2010; Dorion et al., 2021).

- If in vitro barrier rescue is confirmed, proceed to an RPE-centric in vivo model: The conditional RPE Sod2 knockout mouse offers a relevant disease model to test chronic MitoQ for preservation of RPE structure and photoreceptor survival, using functional (ERG), structural (OCT, histology), and redox biomarkers; formulation strategy should initially use oral dosing (supported by retinal uptake and safety in mice) while acknowledging the need for future ocular delivery optimization if systemic exposure is inadequate (Mao et al., 2014; Vlachantoni et al., 2011; Smith & Murphy, 2010).

- Parallel formulation and delivery research: Address MitoQ’s low aqueous solubility and micelle formation to enable ocular delivery if systemic exposure proves insufficient, learning from SkQ1 topical development but recognizing the physicochemical differences between ubiquinone and plastoquinone analogs (Zinovkin & Zamyatnin, 2019; Lukashev et al., 2014).

Conclusion  
MitoQ is a mechanistically sound mitochondria-targeted antioxidant with documented retinal access and a favorable systemic safety profile, but the existing retinal/RPE efficacy evidence is mixed: supportive in RPE photostress (blue light) paradigms by class logic and a single review citation, negative in NaIO₃-challenged RPE, and negative across multiple inherited photoreceptor degeneration models in vivo (Jarrett & Boulton, 2012; Dorion et al., 2021; Vlachantoni et al., 2011). Its chemistry—potent interception of membrane lipid radicals, limited H₂O₂ reactivity, and dependence on Δψm—suggests that model selection will dictate outcomes; AMD-relevant assays that emphasize lipid peroxidation (A2E/blue light) and preserve some mitochondrial polarization are the most plausible settings to observe barrier protection (Smith & Murphy, 2010; Tong et al., 2022).

Given the absence of direct evidence for TEER or tight-junction preservation by MitoQ in RPE and the lack of AMD clinical trials, the program should proceed only with a constrained, hypothesis-driven preclinical plan: demonstrate robust barrier and mitochondrial rescue in A2E/blue-light RPE models with rigorous controls and mechanistic biomarkers; if successful, validate in an RPE-centric in vivo system (RPE Sod2 KO) with careful safety and PK characterization; if signals remain weak or limited to narrow conditions, resources should pivot to alternative mitochondrial strategies with stronger retinal/RPE evidence (e.g., SkQ1 or peptides like Elamipretide) (Mao et al., 2014; Baksheeva et al., 2018; Tong et al., 2022). Overall, MitoQ’s potential for dry AMD is context-dependent and currently unproven for barrier restoration; its advancement should be contingent on clear in vitro PoC in photostress-driven RPE models and confirmation in an RPE-centric in vivo system (Dorion et al., 2021; Vlachantoni et al., 2011; ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.).
