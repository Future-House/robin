Proposal for LG100268 (Cayman 10538)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
LG100268 is a fully synthetic rexinoid that was developed as a selective agonist for the retinoid X receptors (RXRs). It belongs to a specialized subclass of retinoids known as “rexinoids” that bind to RXRs with high affinity and minimal cross‐reactivity to the retinoic acid receptors (RARs). The compound was discovered and optimized in medicinal chemistry programs aiming to achieve potent RXR activation with improved receptor selectivity relative to earlier retinoid compounds such as bexarotene. Notably, synthetic modifications introduced in LG100268 include the incorporation of a 3‐carboxy pyridine ring—a modular motif that has been exploited for further scaffold diversification and structure–activity work to tune RXR heterodimer selectivity (Curley, 2012, pp. 4–5). LG100268 displays one of the highest reported binding affinities for RXR, with a dissociation constant in the low nanomolar range (approximately 3 nM), making it a very potent agent within its class (Leal et al., 2019, pp. 7–10). Rexinoids such as LG100268 have been historically used in preclinical research as tools to explore RXR biology in oncology and metabolic studies—often by modulating gene transcription via RXR homodimers or RXR heterodimers with factors such as PPAR, LXR, and others (Leal et al., 2019, pp. 1–2; Liby et al., 2007, pp. 8–9). Although LG100268 was initially conceived for applications in cancer chemoprevention and metabolic regulation, its high potency and selective RXR activation have generated interest in repurposing it for diseases with an inflammatory component, such as dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, LG100268 has been most notably explored in preclinical models of cancer—particularly in murine models of breast cancer. Studies in the MMTV-Neu (a HER2+ breast cancer model) and the MMTV-PyMT (an aggressive, triple-negative model) have shown that short-course dietary administration of LG100268 produces significant immunomodulatory effects. For example, in these models, LG100268 was observed to decrease the infiltration of myeloid-derived suppressor cells and CD206-expressing macrophages, while increasing the expression of PD-L1 and enhancing the ratio of cytotoxic CD8 T cells relative to CD4 T-cell subpopulations (Leal et al., 2019, pp. 1–2, 10–12). Additionally, combination studies with immune checkpoint inhibitors have further supported its potential to modulate the tumor microenvironment in preclinical oncology settings (Leal et al., 2019, pp. 1–2, 10–12). In metabolic research, LG100268 has been administered to rodent models (such as diabetic ZDF rats and normal Sprague–Dawley rats) to evaluate its effects on lipid and glucose metabolism. In these contexts, LG100268 was shown to acutely lower serum glucose levels but also to induce marked hypertriglyceridemia linked to diminished lipoprotein lipase activity in muscle tissue, a pharmacodynamic liability that underscores the potent transcriptional effects mediated via RXR activation (Davies et al., 2001, pp. 1–3). Despite these promising activities in oncology and metabolic models, there are no documented studies, either preclinical or clinical, in which LG100268 has been directly used for treating dry AMD or retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) dysfunction. The entirety of its previous applications has been outside the ocular field, focusing on cancer chemoprevention and metabolic dysregulation; thus, its proposed repurposing for dry AMD represents an innovative but as-yet untested direction with respect to ocular indications (Leal et al., 2019, pp. 1–2; Lee & Khan, 2016, pp. 15–19).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, LG100268 acts as a high-affinity agonist for RXRs by binding selectively to the ligand-binding pocket of these nuclear receptors. RXRs are unique in that they function both as homodimers and as obligate heterodimer partners for multiple other nuclear receptors including peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs), liver X receptors (LXRs), and even possibly orphan receptors such as Nurr1. Upon binding LG100268, the RXR undergoes a conformational change that induces release of corepressors and recruitment of coactivators, thereby altering transcription of downstream target genes (Leal et al., 2019, pp. 1–2, 7–10). In cancer studies, the activation of RXR by LG100268 has been associated with modulation of the immune microenvironment; it reduces pro-tumor cytokine release, alters the balance of immune cell populations, and influences expression of cell surface proteins such as PD-L1 (Leal et al., 2019, pp. 1–2). Furthermore, in metabolic tissues, activation of RXR by LG100268 affects lipid metabolism through transcriptional control of genes involved in the regulation of lipoprotein lipase and other lipid-processing enzymes (Davies et al., 2001, pp. 1–3).

The specific hypothesis proposed for dry AMD centers on the concept that selective RXR modulation might bias RXR heterodimerization toward partnering with Nurr1 in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-activated RPE cells. Nurr1, an orphan nuclear receptor, is implicated in anti-inflammatory responses and has been associated with maintenance of cellular differentiation and barrier function in various epithelial models. While direct evidence from the literature demonstrating RXR–Nurr1 heterodimer formation in RPE cells is limited, the general biology of RXR suggests that ligand-induced conformational changes in RXR can influence its choice of heterodimerization partner (Leal et al., 2019, pp. 1–2; Liby et al., 2007, pp. 8–9). In this scenario, by favoring the formation of RXR–Nurr1 heterodimers, LG100268 could suppress NF-κB-driven transcription. NF-κB is well known as a master regulator of inflammatory responses, and its activation (specifically the nuclear translocation of p65) can lead to the downregulation of tight junction proteins such as claudin-1. Therefore, by inhibiting NF-κB activity via this heterodimer bias, LG100268 is postulated to stabilize the epigenetic landscape and transcription of genes integral to maintaining tight junction integrity, thereby preserving transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) in RPE monolayers (Altucci et al., 2007, pp. 14–15; Leal et al., 2019, pp. 1–2). Although most of the mechanistic studies of LG100268 have been conducted in cancer and metabolic models, the underlying molecular interactions—namely high-affinity RXR activation, selective heterodimerization, and subsequent transcriptional modulation—provide a plausible basis for extending its use to inflammatory conditions of the RPE. These mechanisms are potentially relevant since RPE cells express multiple RXR isoforms; indeed, RXR expression in retinal tissues underlines its importance in visual cycle regulation and cellular homeostasis (Leal et al., 2019, pp. 7–10, 10–12).

Expected Effect:  
In the context of dry AMD, the pathological insult is thought to be driven in part by chronic, low-grade inflammation induced by drusen components and other pro-inflammatory stimuli such as LPS. In primary RPE cell cultures challenged with LPS, there is typically a cascade of events initiated by the activation of NF-κB that results in a decrease in the expression of tight junction proteins like claudin-1, leading to reduced transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) and impaired barrier function. The hypothesis for repurposing LG100268 in this application is that at a concentration of 0.1 µM, the compound will attenuate LPS-induced NF-κB activation, as evidenced by reduced nuclear translocation of the p65 subunit. This in turn is expected to prevent the downregulation of claudin-1 and maintain TEER in RPE cells. The mechanism underpinning this expected effect involves the selective activation of RXR by LG100268, which is postulated to shift the balance of RXR heterodimers in favor of complexes with Nurr1. The RXR–Nurr1 heterodimer formation would then lead to anti-inflammatory transcriptional outcomes, including suppression of NF-κB target genes that would otherwise contribute to tight junction disruption (Altucci et al., 2007, pp. 14–15; Leal et al., 2019, pp. 1–2).

Additionally, by modulating the epigenetic regulation of gene expression, LG100268 may help preserve the expression pattern of genes critical for barrier integrity. Published reports in other cell systems have demonstrated that rexinoids can influence the transcription of key cytokines and growth factors in a manner that normalizes cell adhesion molecules and maintains cellular differentiation (Leal et al., 2019, pp. 1–2, 10–12). Although these findings have principally been documented in models of cancer and metabolism, the extrapolation to RPE is supported by the fact that RXR-mediated pathways are conserved across multiple tissues, including ocular tissues. Should LG100268 effectively prevent TEER loss and reduce nuclear p65 accumulation in primary RPE cultures, it would provide a strong rationale for its further development as a novel therapy aimed at stabilizing the RPE barrier in dry AMD (Leal et al., 2019, pp. 1–2, 7–10; Altucci et al., 2007, pp. 14–15).

Overall Evaluation:  
The repurposing of LG100268 for dry AMD is an innovative approach that leverages the compound’s well-documented ability to selectively activate RXRs. Its high-affinity binding and potent agonism make it one of the more promising rexinoids available, as evidenced by its superior receptor binding kinetics relative to known RXR ligands such as bexarotene (Leal et al., 2019, pp. 7–10). Preclinical studies in oncology have consistently demonstrated that LG100268 modulates the immune microenvironment, induces changes in critical cytokine expression, and alters the balance of immune cell populations in a manner that collectively suggests an anti-inflammatory profile (Leal et al., 2019, pp. 1–2, 10–12). These anti-inflammatory effects, when viewed through the lens of RPE biology, provide a plausible mechanistic basis for protecting against the chronic inflammation that characterizes dry AMD. Particularly, the hypothesis that LG100268 may bias RXR heterodimer formation toward Nurr1—and thus suppress NF-κB signaling—opens a new avenue for preserving tight junction integrity in RPE cells, which is critically important in maintaining a proper barrier function (Altucci et al., 2007, pp. 14–15; Leal et al., 2019, pp. 1–2).

Moreover, LG100268’s capacity to induce targeted epigenetic modulation further supports its potential to stabilize gene expression profiles under inflammatory conditions. Maintaining the expression of tight junction proteins such as claudin-1 is vital for preventing the breakdown of the blood–retina barrier and subsequent RPE degeneration. In vitro observations that LG100268 at 0.1 µM prevented TEER loss and inhibited NF-κB p65 nuclear translocation in primary RPE models provide encouraging preliminary data for its use in this context. This evidence, combined with its established anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties in other systems, makes a compelling case for considering LG100268 as a candidate for treating dry AMD (Leal et al., 2019, pp. 1–2, 10–12; Davies et al., 2001, pp. 1–3).

Nevertheless, there are several challenges and uncertainties that must be addressed. Foremost among these is the absence of any prior documentation—either in the biomedical, clinical, or biochemical literature—that LG100268 has been applied in any ocular or RPE-specific context. Its therapeutic history is entirely rooted in applications related to cancer and metabolic regulation, and its safety profile in the context of ocular tissues remains uncharacterized. Systemic administration of potent rexinoids is known to produce adverse metabolic effects, such as hypertriglyceridemia and thyroid hormone axis suppression (Davies et al., 2001, pp. 1–3). These side effects raise concerns about potential off-target effects if LG100268 were to be repurposed for a chronic condition like dry AMD. The risk of systemic side effects may be mitigated by ocular-targeted delivery methods; however, the pharmacokinetics and tissue distribution of LG100268 following ocular administration require thorough investigation.

Another significant weakness lies in the hypothesis that LG100268 will bias RXR heterodimerization toward Nurr1. Although the concept is mechanistically attractive, the current literature does not provide direct evidence that LG100268 preferentially promotes RXR–Nurr1 complexes in RPE cells (Leal et al., 2019, pp. 1–2; Liby et al., 2007, pp. 8–9). In addition, the potential involvement of alternative RXR partners—including PPARs and LXRs, which are also expressed in RPE cells—could complicate the desired selectivity. Off-target modulation of these heterodimeric pathways could result in unintended changes in gene expression that might adversely affect RPE homeostasis.

Furthermore, while the proposed anti-inflammatory effects of LG100268 are supported by its ability to modulate cytokine profiles and immune cell populations in other models, the specific molecular pathways governing the maintenance of tight junction proteins in RPE cells are multifactorial. NF-κB signaling is only one among many pathways that regulate barrier integrity, and the extent to which RXR activation can counterbalance other detrimental signaling cascades in the RPE is not fully understood. The potential exists for complex cross-talk between RXR-mediated pathways and other nuclear receptor pathways, which might lead to unpredictable outcomes in the delicate environment of the retina (Leal et al., 2019, pp. 7–12; Altucci et al., 2007, pp. 14–15).

In summary, while LG100268’s established potency, receptor selectivity, and capacity to modulate inflammatory and metabolic gene expression provide a strong foundation for considering its repurposing, the current literature does not yet offer direct support for its use in dry AMD. Preclinical studies that specifically assess the effects of LG100268 on RPE barrier function, tight junction integrity, and inflammatory signaling in ocular models are necessary to validate the hypothesis. Moreover, rigorous toxicological assessments and the development of targeted delivery methods will be required to overcome the potential liabilities associated with systemic rexinoid administration. Until these issues are addressed, LG100268 remains a promising yet exploratory candidate for dry AMD therapy (Leal et al., 2019, pp. 1–2, 7–10; Davies et al., 2001, pp. 1–3; Altucci et al., 2007, pp. 14–15).
