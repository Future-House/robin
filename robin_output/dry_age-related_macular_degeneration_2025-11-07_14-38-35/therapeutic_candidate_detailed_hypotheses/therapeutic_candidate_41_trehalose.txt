Proposal for Trehalose (Sigma T9531)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Trehalose (α,α-trehalose) is a naturally occurring, nonreducing disaccharide composed of two d-glucose molecules linked by an α,α-1,1-glycosidic bond; it is broadly found in bacteria, fungi, insects, invertebrates, and plants but is not synthesized by vertebrates, including humans (trehalase in the small intestine hydrolyzes ingested trehalose to glucose) (Laihia & Kaarniranta, 2020, pp. 1–3). Trehalose’s physicochemical properties include high aqueous solubility, chemical stability, and resistance to glucosidases; it has been mass-produced since the 1990s via enzymatic methods and has been approved for food use by EU regulators and the US FDA, with human exposure generally safe apart from low incidences of malabsorption (Laihia & Kaarniranta, 2020, pp. 1–3). In drug-development parlance, trehalose is a small-molecule, carbohydrate autophagy modulator and proteostasis regulator; it exhibits both biophysical “chemical chaperone” activity that stabilizes macromolecules and biologic activity that induces the autophagy–lysosome program via TFEB/TFE3 activation and related nutrient-sensing pathways (Abokyi, 2021, pp. 27–32; Jeong et al., 2021, pp. 1–3, 12–13; Khalifeh et al., 2020, pp. 3–5). As a class, autophagy-enhancing small molecules (including trehalose) have been utilized across neurodegeneration and metabolic disease models to promote clearance of toxic protein aggregates or dysfunctional organelles and to restore lysosomal function, with trehalose showing efficacy signals in preclinical Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, Huntington’s disease, and lysosomal storage–related models (Abokyi, 2021, pp. 27–32; Khalifeh et al., 2020, pp. 3–5; Lotfi et al., 2018, pp. 12–15). Within ophthalmology, trehalose has established use as a topical osmoprotectant/bioprotective excipient in eye-drop formulations for dry-eye disease (commonly 3% w/v), and it is under active investigation for posterior-segment neuroprotection, including age-related retinal degeneration models relevant to AMD (Laihia & Kaarniranta, 2020, pp. 1–3, 18–20; Cox et al., 2025, pp. 1–2, 10–13).

Therapeutic History  
Clinical/translational use: Trehalose is approved as a safe food ingredient and has been widely used in humans, including topical ophthalmic preparations for dry eye disease and post-LASIK care, with multiple clinical trials and a marketed formulation (e.g., Thealoz Duo, 3% trehalose plus hyaluronic acid) supporting ocular-surface safety and symptomatic benefit; the patented formulation increased tear film thickness after a single drop in clinical testing (Laihia & Kaarniranta, 2020, pp. 1–3, 18–20). Safety data support tolerability of topical trehalose in humans and of high-concentration in vitro exposure across multiple cell types; ocular-surface animal models show safety and barrier-protective effects, and in vitro human RPE studies report no loss of viability up to 200 mM for 24 h, though dosing windows matter for efficacy (Laihia & Kaarniranta, 2020, pp. 1–3, 5–7). Oral trehalose is hydrolyzed by intestinal trehalase and exhibits poor systemic bioavailability, limiting efficacy for some systemic indications; parenteral trehalose achieves low-millimolar plasma levels transiently, which can activate TFEB/TFE3 in macrophages in vivo, but oral exposure is generally insufficient to reach such systemic concentrations in mice in atherosclerosis models (Evans et al., 2018, pp. 2–3). Nonetheless, oral trehalose has shown retinal effects in mice, improving autophagy pathways and preserving cone ERG responses during aging, suggesting either BRB permeability or indirect choroidal effects; the authors noted that establishing BRB permeability is important for translation (Abokyi, 2021, pp. 181–188).

Ocular/retinal preclinical evidence: Multiple independent groups demonstrate mechanistic and functional efficacy of trehalose in RPE/retinal models relevant to dry AMD pathophysiology. In human RPE cell systems (ARPE-19, haRPE, and iPSC-RPE), trehalose increases autophagic flux (LC3-II accumulation with CQ, free GFP readouts), upregulates lysosomal biogenesis markers (LAMP-1/2, cathepsin D), elevates TFEB transcripts and protein, expands the lysosomal compartment (Lysotracker), engages both macroautophagy and chaperone-mediated autophagy, and confers autophagy-dependent protection against oxidative stressors such as hydroquinone (HQ), a cigarette smoke–derived oxidant implicated in AMD (Abokyi, 2021, pp. 151–160; Fisher et al., 2022, pp. 6–8). In these RPE systems, trehalose also shifts transcription toward anti-oxidative/anti-angiogenic profiles (NRF2 up, VEGF-A down; Hsp27 down), without evidence of trehalose-induced ROS/cell death at effective doses; however, higher doses can lose cytoprotection—despite ROS suppression—likely due to excessive autophagy, underscoring a narrow therapeutic window (Abokyi, 2021, pp. 154–155, 160–165). In primary murine RPE, trehalose restored autophagic flux under oxidative stress and improved mitochondrial respiratory function in vitro (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 1–2). In vivo, topical trehalose eye drops increase autophagy–lysosome activity in mouse RPE (LC3B turnover, p62 degradation, LC3B–LAMP1 colocalization) and protect against light-induced outer retinal degeneration (preserving ONL thickness, RPE morphology, and junctional F-actin) without overt changes in unchallenged retina (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 1–2, 10–13). Oral trehalose increased retinal autophagy and improved cone photopic ERG with age, and in a lysosomal enzyme-deficiency model (MPS IIIB) trehalose reduced retinal degeneration, restored retinal structure/function, and extended lifespan, with effects dependent on canonical autophagy and TFEB activation; trehalose increased TFEB nuclear translocation and CLEAR gene expression in brain and rescued retinal phenotypes (Abokyi, 2021, pp. 181–188; Lotfi et al., 2018, pp. 12–15).

AMD-model efficacy: In an APOE knockout model that recapitulates early AMD features (decreased retinal function, Bruch’s membrane thickening), chronic trehalose treatment ameliorated functional loss and reduced Bruch’s membrane thickening while normalizing autophagy markers (restoring LC3-II:LC3-I to WT, increasing LC3/LAMP1 labeling and autophagosome–lysosome fusion in RPE), with pathway analyses pointing to ATM–AMPK activation and, for trehalose, increased p38/MAPK14 signaling (Vessey et al., 2022, pp. 1–2, 11–13). In AMD-relevant human risk models, trehalose rescued lysosomal function in CFH Y402H iPSC-RPE—restoring LAMP1 and cathepsin D/L and reducing intracellular lipid accumulation (BODIPY) and APOE trend—consistent with TFEB activation independent of mTOR; in vivo, trehalose rescued CLEAR network expression and prevented basal laminar deposits in Cryba1 cKO retina and improved early RPE changes in Akt2 KI mice, positioning trehalose as an mTOR-independent TFEB activator that counteracts AKT2-driven lysosomal/autophagy defects (Ghosh et al., 2023, pp. 11–15, 34–40; Ghosh et al., 2024, p. 6).

Clinical status for AMD: A ClinicalTrials.gov query indicates at least two registered trials for “Trehalose AND macular degeneration,” but the excerpt provides no identifiers or results, so clinical evidence in AMD is presently insufficient for efficacy conclusions (ClinicalTrials.gov, n.d.). Collectively, these histories support trehalose as an autophagy/lysosome-modulating candidate with direct RPE/retina efficacy signals in AMD-relevant models, established human ocular-surface safety, and translational rationale for non-neovascular AMD (Cox et al., 2025, pp. 10–13; Ghosh et al., 2024, p. 6; Vessey et al., 2022, p. 1).
