Proposal for SMER28 (Sigma SML1248)

I. Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
SMER28 is a small‐molecule enhancer of autophagy originally identified through high‐throughput chemical screens designed to activate intracellular clearance pathways. It is chemically characterized as a bromo‐substituted quinazoline and belongs to a series of “SMER” compounds initially disclosed by leading research institutions to augment autophagy independently of mTOR signaling (Esteves, Oliveira, & Cardoso, 2013; Sarkar & Rubinsztein, 2008). The synthesis of SMER28 has been streamlined into a three‐step process using accessible reagents such as 2-amino-5-bromobenzoic acid and 1,1′-carbonyldiimidazole (CDI), facilitating its large-scale preparation without requiring extensive purification of intermediates (Koukourakis et al., 2018). Originally, SMER28 was discovered during efforts to identify compounds that could promote autophagy and protein clearance, a focus that later expanded its potential use to diseases characterized by proteostatic stress. Its structure–activity relationships and ease of synthesis render SMER28 an attractive candidate for drug repurposing strategies beyond its initial applications, particularly in diseases where defective autophagy contributes to pathogenesis. This candidate’s established role in enhancing cellular clearance mechanisms positions it well for evaluation in dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD), a condition marked by impaired removal of misfolded proteins and lipids in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) (Doulatov et al., 2017; Esteves, Oliveira, & Cardoso, 2013).

II. Therapeutic History  
SMER28 has an extensive preclinical history that underscores its potential for broad therapeutic applications. In initial studies focused on Diamond-Blackfan Anemia (DBA), SMER28 was shown to promote erythropoiesis and enhance hemoglobin expression in both in vitro systems and zebrafish models of DBA, where ribosomal protein mutations lead to anemia-like phenotypes (Doulatov et al., 2017). These findings indicated that SMER28 could effectively modulate cellular differentiation and survival pathways in stressed cell populations. Subsequent investigations expanded its evaluation to radioprotection, where SMER28 demonstrated protective effects in mouse bone marrow and liver tissues subjected to irradiation. These studies revealed that SMER28’s activation of autophagy could preserve normal tissue architecture and improve recovery after cellular injury (Koukourakis et al., 2018). Furthermore, in models of neurodegenerative diseases, SMER28 has been implicated in enhancing both autophagic and proteasomal clearance mechanisms to reduce the burden of misfolded and aggregation-prone proteins such as mutant huntingtin and polyQ-expanded proteins (Wrobel, Hill, & Rubinsztein, 2023). Although none of these prior studies were conducted directly in ocular tissues, their outcomes suggest that the underlying mechanism of SMER28—enhancement of the intracellular degradation machinery—is broadly applicable. Given that the RPE in dry AMD is characterized by the accumulation of toxic protein aggregates and lipid deposits due to defective autophagy (Fernández-Albarral et al., 2021), repurposing SMER28 for dry AMD addresses a significant disease mechanism while potentially leveraging its established safety and efficacy in other systems.

III. Mechanism of Action  
SMER28’s mechanism of action is defined by its ability to induce autophagy in an mTOR-independent manner—a key advantage over conventional autophagy modulators that act via mTOR inhibition. At the molecular level, SMER28 binds directly to the Valosin Containing Protein (VCP/p97), an essential chaperone involved in protein quality control and degradation pathways (Wrobel et al., 2022). This binding stabilizes VCP and stimulates its D1 ATPase activity, thereby enhancing both the formation and maturation of autophagosomes. An increase in VCP activity is known to promote the formation of phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate (PI(3)P), a lipid mediator critical for the nucleation of autophagic vesicles (Wrobel et al., 2022). Downstream of PI(3)P production, the assembly of the lipidation machinery—including LC3, a ubiquitin-like protein requisite for autophagosome elongation—is enhanced, as indicated by increased LC3-II formation in cells treated with SMER28 (Wrobel et al., 2022).

Simultaneously, SMER28’s action is dependent on Beclin 1, whose involvement in autophagy initiation further supports the autophagic flux observed upon treatment (Wrobel et al., 2022). Importantly, these effects are achieved without a detectable alteration in the downstream effectors of mTOR signaling; measurements of AKT activation and other mTOR-related pathways remain unaffected, affirming the compound’s mTOR-independent operation (Wrobel et al., 2022; Koukourakis et al., 2018). In parallel, the compound also appears to enhance the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS), an additional degradation pathway, in a manner that is similarly VCP-dependent (Wrobel, Hill, & Rubinsztein, 2023). The convergence of autophagy and UPS pathways is particularly relevant in cellular systems where proteostasis is compromised.

While there is robust evidence supporting the activation of autophagic flux via VCP and Beclin 1, the specific hypothesis for dry AMD posits that SMER28 may also engage ULK1-dependent autophagy mechanisms. ULK1 (UNC-51-like kinase 1) is a serine/threonine kinase upstream in the autophagy cascade that integrates stress signals to initiate autophagy, and genetic ablation of ULK1 or ATG5 has been shown to replicate RPE barrier defects (Fernández-Albarral et al., 2021; Davis, 2015). However, the current literature does not provide direct evidence linking SMER28 with ULK1 activation; rather, the bulk of mechanistic data implicates the compound in VCP-mediated processes (Whitmarsh-Everiss & Laraia, 2021; Runwal, 2019; Esteves, Oliveira, & Cardoso, 2013). Despite this gap, the overall mode of action—enhancing autophagic and proteasomal clearance pathways while bypassing mTOR—remains firmly established. Thus, SMER28 targets a network of molecular interactions, pivoting on the modulation of VCP’s activity, increased PI(3)P production, and subsequent LC3 lipidation, all of which consolidate its role as an effective autophagy inducer (Wrobel et al., 2022).

IV. Expected Effect  
The central hypothesis driving the evaluation of SMER28 in dry AMD is that its administration will lead to activation of autophagy in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells, thereby promoting the clearance of accumulated lipids and misfolded proteins at cell–cell contacts. In dry AMD, the RPE exhibits dysfunctional autophagy, which contributes to the accumulation of toxic compounds such as A2E—a lipofuscin precursor known to induce oxidative stress and compromise cellular architecture (Fernández-Albarral et al., 2021; Frost, Mitchell, & Boesze-Battaglia, 2014). By facilitating the removal of these deleterious aggregates, SMER28 is expected to alleviate proteostatic and lipotoxic stress within the RPE.

Although most studies elucidating SMER28’s effects have been performed in non-ocular systems, the mechanistic insights gained can be extrapolated to the RPE environment. Specifically, SMER28’s ability to increase LC3 lipidation and enhance autophagic flux—as evidenced by changes in LC3-II levels and Beclin 1 dependency—offers a promising strategy for reducing intracellular lipid droplets and misfolded protein aggregates (Wrobel et al., 2022). This improved clearance should, in turn, restore proper intracellular communication and protein homeostasis near cell–cell junctions.

A critical consequence of this process is the anticipated reassembly of tight junction proteins, particularly ZO-1 and occludin. These proteins are essential for maintaining barrier integrity in the RPE, and their disassembly under stress conditions leads to a decrease in trans-epithelial electrical resistance (TEER), a surrogate marker for barrier function (Fernández-Albarral et al., 2021; Davis, 2015). By reducing the cellular burden of lipid and protein aggregates, SMER28 may enable the reformation of ZO-1 and occludin belts at cell–cell contacts, thereby improving TEER. Although direct measurements of ZO-1 and occludin reassembly following SMER28 treatment have not been detailed in the existing literature, analogous improvements in tight junction morphology have been observed in other proteostatic and neurodegenerative models where SMER28 enhanced cellular protein clearance (Wrobel, Hill, & Rubinsztein, 2023).

Furthermore, SMER28’s mTOR-independent nature is expected to confer a selective therapeutic advantage. Unlike mTOR inhibitors, which often exert widespread metabolic effects that could be harmful in chronic conditions, SMER28’s mechanism minimizes the risk of systemic side effects while still promoting the clearance of pathogenic intracellular substrates (Wrobel et al., 2022). In the proposed assay, RPE cells challenged with A2E or other proteostatic stressors would ideally show increased markers of autophagy (e.g., LC3-II accumulation, reduced p62 levels) alongside functional restoration of barrier properties as measured by TEER assays. The improvement in barrier integrity is expected to reflect not only the biochemical clearance of toxic aggregates but also structural reorganization at intercellular junctions—a dual benefit that addresses key pathogenic features of dry AMD (Fernández-Albarral et al., 2021).

Additionally, the genetic correlations drawn from studies where knockdown of crucial autophagy regulators such as ULK1 or ATG5 leads to RPE barrier defects further support the notion that pharmacological enhancement of autophagy may reverse such phenotypes. Although direct evidence linking SMER28 with ULK1 activation in RPE cells is sparse, the overall strategy of boosting autophagic flux to restore cellular homeostasis remains compelling (Davis, 2015; Fernández-Albarral et al., 2021). In summary, SMER28 is expected to work by reducing intracellular accumulations of harmful proteins and lipids, promoting the reassembly of ZO-1 and occludin at cell–cell contacts, and improving TEER under conditions that mimic the stress found in dry AMD.

V. Overall Evaluation  
SMER28 presents several compelling strengths as a potential therapeutic candidate for dry AMD, yet it also faces challenges that must be addressed through further targeted research.

Strengths:  
One of the foremost strengths of SMER28 is its robust ability to induce autophagy through mechanisms independent of mTOR inhibition. This property is vital because mTOR inhibitors, while effective at promoting autophagic activity, can induce broad, deleterious metabolic effects not well tolerated in chronic conditions such as AMD (Wrobel et al., 2022). By bypassing mTOR, SMER28 offers a more selective approach that focuses on enhancing intracellular clearance pathways while maintaining the overall metabolic balance. The molecular interactions it engages—predominantly through binding and activating VCP/p97, stimulating the production of PI(3)P, and driving LC3 lipidation—are well documented in several studies (Wrobel et al., 2022). This dual modulation of both autophagic and proteasomal pathways not only promotes protein aggregate clearance but also potentially addresses the accumulation of toxic lipids, which is central to AMD pathogenesis.

The extensive preclinical data supporting SMER28’s efficacy in models of Diamond-Blackfan Anemia, radioprotection, and neurodegenerative diseases offers reassurance regarding its safety profile and mechanism of action (Doulatov et al., 2017; Koukourakis et al., 2018; Esteves, Oliveira, & Cardoso, 2013; Sarkar & Rubinsztein, 2008). The compound’s success in these diverse models underscores its versatility and hints at its capacity to produce similar benefits in the RPE, a tissue highly sensitive to proteostatic imbalances. Furthermore, its inclusion in the SMER series—where analogous molecules have been effective in clearing aggregation-prone proteins in neurodegeneration—adds credence to the hypothesis that SMER28 could alleviate the intracellular debris that contributes to dry AMD (Esteves, Oliveira, & Cardoso, 2013; Sarkar & Rubinsztein, 2008).

Weaknesses:  
Despite the promising attributes outlined above, significant gaps in the current literature present challenges for the immediate repurposing of SMER28 for dry AMD. The most notable gap lies in the lack of direct evidence concerning its effects on RPE cells, specifically regarding the reassembly of tight junction proteins like ZO-1 and occludin. The hypothesis that SMER28 will restore TEER by promoting the reformation of these junctional complexes is logically sound given the role of autophagy in RPE homeostasis (Fernández-Albarral et al., 2021; Frost, Mitchell, & Boesze-Battaglia, 2014), yet direct mechanistic and functional data in ocular models remain sparse.

Moreover, while the literature robustly supports a mechanism involving VCP/p97 and subsequent autophagic flux, there is currently no conclusive evidence establishing that SMER28 activates ULK1-dependent autophagy within RPE cells. Studies have indicated that SMER28 predominantly functions via VCP-mediated pathways and enhances LC3 lipidation without evidence of direct ULK1 or ATG5 activation (Whitmarsh-Everiss & Laraia, 2021; Runwal, 2019; Esteves, Oliveira, & Cardoso, 2013). Given that genetic knockdown of ULK1 and ATG5 replicates detrimental RPE barrier defects, the absence of clear data on SMER28’s influence on these upstream regulators introduces uncertainty regarding the full scope of its autophagic modulation in the context of dry AMD.

Another potential weakness is the translatability of findings from non-ocular systems to the RPE environment. The RPE is a highly specialized cell type with unique metabolic requirements and structural features. Its reliance on constant autophagic turnover to manage oxidative stress and lipid accumulation means that interventions must be finely tuned. Although SMER28’s impact on proteostasis in other tissues is well established, its precise effects on RPE cell physiology—especially under circumstances of A2E-induced stress—must be rigorously validated through dedicated in vitro and in vivo studies.

In addition, most of the supportive studies for SMER28 have been conducted in contexts of systemic or neurodegenerative challenges rather than in ocular models. This necessitates caution when extrapolating positive outcomes from models of Diamond-Blackfan Anemia or radioprotection to the pathophysiology of dry AMD (Doulatov et al., 2017; Koukourakis et al., 2018). The hypothesis that SMER28 will facilitate the reassembly of ZO-1/occludin belts and restore TEER, while promising, remains speculative until confirmed by functional assays in RPE cell cultures and suitable animal models of AMD.

Overall, SMER28 combines a favorable mechanism of action with a promising preclinical safety and efficacy record that renders it an attractive candidate for repurposing in dry AMD. Its selective activation of autophagic clearance pathways without engaging mTOR minimizes potential off-target effects, making it particularly suitable for chronic administration in age-related diseases. However, critical unanswered questions regarding its direct impact on RPE barrier function, particularly the reassembly of tight junction proteins and TEER restoration, must be addressed in future studies (Fernández-Albarral et al., 2021; Frost, Mitchell, & Boesze-Battaglia, 2014; Davis, 2015). Moreover, the putative involvement of ULK1-dependent autophagy in SMER28’s mechanism remains to be definitively characterized in ocular models (Whitmarsh-Everiss & Laraia, 2021; Runwal, 2019).

In conclusion, while SMER28 has yet to be directly evaluated in ocular systems, its strong mechanistic foundation—as evidenced by its ability to enhance autophagy and proteasomal clearance via VCP stimulation, LC3 lipidation, and Beclin 1 dependency—supports its potential utility in mitigating the autophagic deficits observed in dry AMD (Wrobel et al., 2022). The anticipated clearance of lipid droplets and misfolded proteins, combined with the potential for facilitating the reassembly of ZO-1 and occludin at cell–cell junctions, collectively addresses two critical aspects of dry AMD pathogenesis. Consequently, SMER28 emerges as a promising candidate with significant potential to restore RPE function and barrier integrity, provided that future empirical studies confirm these effects in the appropriate ocular models (Fernández-Albarral et al., 2021; Davis, 2015; Koukourakis et al., 2018).

As an expert drug development team leader evaluating novel therapeutic candidates, I recommend progressing SMER28 to the next stage of rigorous, mechanism-focused preclinical studies using RPE cell cultures and relevant animal models of dry AMD. Emphasis should be placed on detailed analyses of autophagic flux, ULK1 activation status, and the integrity of tight junction reassembly under conditions of A2E-induced or proteostatic stress. This targeted approach will help clarify SMER28’s full mechanistic profile in RPE cells and validate its potential to restore TEER and overall barrier function, thereby establishing its viability as a repurposed therapeutic for dry AMD (Wrobel et al., 2022; Davis, 2015).

Overall, SMER28 stands out due to its mTOR-independent autophagy induction, dual engagement of autophagic and proteasomal pathways, and a well-established preclinical background in diverse stress models, all of which offer promising avenues for counteracting the cellular dysfunctions underlying dry AMD. Nonetheless, further studies are essential to bridge the current knowledge gap regarding its specific effects on ULK1-dependent autophagy and tight junction reassembly in RPE cells, which are fundamental for achieving the desired therapeutic outcomes in dry AMD (Whitmarsh-Everiss & Laraia, 2021; Runwal, 2019; Davis, 2015).

In summary, SMER28’s ability to robustly stimulate autophagic flux and promote cellular clearance mechanisms, together with its favorable safety profile demonstrated in non-ocular models, provides a strong rationale for its repurposing in dry AMD. While the theoretical framework suggests that SMER28 may ameliorate the proteostatic and lipotoxic stress impairing RPE function—thereby restoring the reassembly of ZO-1/occludin belts and improving TEER—the success of this therapeutic strategy will ultimately depend on empirical validation in ocular-specific studies. Pending such investigations, SMER28 remains a compelling candidate that merits further exploration as a novel approach to combat the significant unmet medical need posed by dry AMD (Doulatov et al., 2017; Wrobel et al., 2022; Frost, Mitchell, & Boesze-Battaglia, 2014).

Given the convergence of mechanistic insights, preclinical efficacy in multiple models, and the compelling need for improved therapeutic strategies in dry AMD, SMER28 merits prioritization for further research. Its progression into RPE-based studies could potentially pave the way for a new class of autophagy-modulating, mTOR-independent therapeutics aimed at restoring RPE function and halting the progression of dry AMD, thereby offering hope for improved outcomes in this prevalent cause of vision loss (Fernández-Albarral et al., 2021; Davis, 2015; Koukourakis et al., 2018).
