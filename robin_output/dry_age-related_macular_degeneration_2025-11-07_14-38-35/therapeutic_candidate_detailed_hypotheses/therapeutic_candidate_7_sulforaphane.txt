Proposal for Sulforaphane (Sigma S4441)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
Sulforaphane (SFN) is a naturally occurring isothiocyanate generated from the glucosinolate glucoraphanin by the β-thioglucosidase myrosinase, which is abundant in cruciferous vegetables (notably broccoli sprouts). Its electrophilic –N=C=S moiety underlies covalent reactivity with nucleophilic cysteines on sensor proteins, most prominently Keap1, and thereby triggers a broad antioxidant and cytoprotective transcriptional response controlled by Nrf2 (NFE2L2) via antioxidant response elements (AREs) in gene promoters (phase-2 enzymes, redox systems, and detoxification pathways) (Zhang et al., 2024; Chang et al., 2020). In retinal and epithelial contexts, SFN has been repeatedly characterized as an “indirect antioxidant” that elevates glutathione (GSH) and induces ARE-dependent enzymes such as NAD(P)H:quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1), heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1/HMOX1), glutathione S-transferases (GSTs), and thioredoxin (Trx1), among others (Zhou et al., 2006; Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019; Tanito et al., 2005; Liu & Yan, 2018). In human RPE-derived cell models, SFN increased GSH and induced NQO1 and GST, consistent with canonical ARE program activation (Zhou et al., 2006). In vivo ocular models show SFN attenuates retinal light damage and improves functional outcomes, further anchoring its relevance to oxidative injury in the outer retina and RPE (Tanito et al., 2005; Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019).

Within its class, SFN is a prototypical dietary electrophile that activates Nrf2 signaling; this class has been broadly studied for chemoprevention and cytoprotection across organs. In ocular tissues, SFN has been shown to protect RPE and photoreceptors from diverse oxidative and phototoxic insults, often in an Nrf2-dependent manner (Yang et al., 2021; Falcão et al., 2025). Beyond Nrf2, SFN engages SIRT1/PGC-1α cross-talk, PARP1/SIRT1/NAD+ stress-response nodes, and autophagy/lysosome-dependent transcriptional programs (TFEB/TFE3), all of which intersect with oxidative, inflammatory, and proteostasis stress pathways in RPE biology (Wang & Tang, 2022; Falcão et al., 2025).

Practical development considerations include bioavailability and delivery. Human studies and reviews indicate that the bioactive isothiocyanate is formed from glucoraphanin in a process heavily dependent on myrosinase (plant or microbiota), with documented systemic exposure and biomarker modulation in clinical settings; however, SFN’s oral bioavailability is limited by solubility, first-pass metabolism, and short plasma half-life, posing translational challenges for ocular exposure through blood–ocular barriers (Zhang et al., 2024). Preclinical delivery strategies such as nanoparticle and albumin carrier systems have been proposed to overcome ocular delivery constraints (Zhang et al., 2024).

Therapeutic History  
SFN and related Nrf2 activators have a long history as cytoprotective agents in preclinical models spanning oncology prevention, vascular and metabolic protection, and neuroprotection. In humans, SFN or broccoli sprout preparations have been administered in controlled trials with documented pharmacodynamic biomarker changes and well-characterized disposition and metabolism; crossover and dose-response studies highlight interconversion with related isothiocyanates (erucin) and underscore the importance of active myrosinase for consistent systemic exposure (Zhang et al., 2024; Qin, 2007). A pilot study detected SFN metabolites in human breast tissue after a single oral broccoli sprout dose, demonstrating tissue penetration to an epithelial compartment and supporting oral bioavailability to peripheral tissues (Qin, 2007). Although multiple clinical contexts are covered in these translational reviews and trials, there are no reported clinical trials of SFN specifically targeting dry AMD or RPE degeneration in the provided material (Zhang et al., 2024).

In ocular systems, the compound’s therapeutic history is robust at the preclinical level. In cultured human RPE, SFN protects against photooxidative injury (A2E/blue light) and chemical oxidants, increases GSH, and induces phase-2 enzymes including NQO1 and GST (Zhou et al., 2006). Microarray and validation work in human RPE-19 cells demonstrate SFN-induced nuclear translocation of Nrf2 and upregulation of thioredoxin-1, with enhanced oxidative stress resistance following H₂O₂ (Ye et al., 2013). In vivo, SFN attenuates retinal light damage in mice through ARE-mediated upregulation of thioredoxin (Trx1), and recovers cone function in a model of RPE oxidative stress in an Nrf2-dependent manner (Tanito et al., 2005; Falcão et al., 2025). SFN also protects rodent retinas from ischemia–reperfusion injury by increasing Nrf2 nuclear accumulation and HO-1 expression; pharmacologic inhibition of HO-1 abolishes protection, causally implicating HO-1 (Pan et al., 2014). In inherited retinal degeneration models (rd10 and tubby), systemic SFN improves retinal function and reduces degeneration, with evidence that ERK signaling and the Trx/TrxR/Nrf2 axis mediate effects in some settings (Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019).

Comparable ocular benefits have been shown in non-RPE tissues. For example, human corneal endothelial FECD cells exhibit SFN-induced Nrf2 nuclear translocation and upregulation of canonical ARE genes (NQO1, HO-1), with reduced ROS and apoptosis (Ziaei et al., 2013). In corneal surface epithelium, SFN protects against oxidative stress via Nrf2/HO-1 activation, supporting generalizability across ocular epithelial cell types (Liu & Yan, 2018). These data reinforce the core mechanism and pharmacology relevant to RPE while underlining the absence of AMD-specific clinical translation to date (Zhang et al., 2024).

Notably, although there is a recent report in an inflamed ARPE-19 model that vitamin D and SFN (alone and combined) reduced ROS, reduced IL-6/IL-8, lowered VEGF, and restored epithelial integrity markers including ZO-1 and claudin-4 expression, the provided material does not include functional barrier endpoints (TEER or FITC-dextran permeability), and claudin-1 is not shown (Bergandi et al., 2024). Thus, while “marker restoration” has been reported for ZO-1 and claudin-4 under TGF-β stress, quantitative barrier function restoration has not yet been demonstrated within the provided sources (Bergandi et al., 2024).

Mechanism of Action  
Primary mechanism—Keap1 cysteine targeting and Nrf2 activation: SFN’s electrophilic isothiocyanate group reacts with redox-sensitive cysteines on Keap1 (notably Cys151), disrupting the Cul3–Keap1 E3 ligase complex and stabilizing Nrf2, which accumulates and translocates to the nucleus. There, Nrf2 heterodimerizes with small Maf proteins and binds ARE sequences to upregulate a broad enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant and detoxification program (e.g., NQO1, HO-1, GSTs, GCLM, Trx1, SRXN1), increasing cellular reductive capacity (GSH pools, thiol buffering) and restoring redox homeostasis under oxidative stress (Chang et al., 2020; Ziaei et al., 2013; Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019). In RPE models, SFN does not consistently increase Nrf2 transcription but promotes nuclear translocation and DNA-binding activity, consistent with post-translational stabilization and activation; ARE target induction follows (Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019; Chang et al., 2020). In ARPE-19, SFN preconditioning increases NQO1 mRNA/activity, boosts cellular GSH, and increases GST activity; microarray and validation data add induction of SRXN1, GCLM, and Trx1 with concurrent Txnip downregulation, aligning with amplified reductive and detoxification capacity (Zhou et al., 2006; Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019). Under t-BHP stress, SFN increases nuclear Nrf2 DNA-binding, elevates NQO1, GR, and GPx1 transcripts, and drives HO-1, NQO1, and GR protein induction (with partial discordance between GPx1 mRNA and protein), while reducing proinflammatory ICAM-1 and MCP-1 (Chang et al., 2020).

Nrf2-dependent ocular efficacy: In vivo, SFN protection from retinal ischemia–reperfusion requires HO-1, as HO-1 inhibition reverses neuroprotection (Pan et al., 2014). In a model of RPE oxidative stress in middle-aged mice, SFN restores cone function in an Nrf2-dependent manner, underscoring that Nrf2 is the mechanistic driver in that age-relevant retinal context (Falcão et al., 2025).

Nrf2-independent and auxiliary mechanisms: RPE protection is not exclusively Nrf2-dependent. SFN has been shown to promote mitochondrial fusion and reduce Drp1-mediated fission, conferring anti-apoptotic benefit even in CRISPR/Cas9 Nrf2-depleted RPE cells (RPE-1), indicating a parallel mitochondrial protective program (Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019). SFN also engages the PARP1/SIRT1/NAD+ axis: SFN or its metabolites can blunt damage-induced PARylation via direct PARP1 interactions, alleviating SIRT1 inhibition, promoting autophagy and mitochondrial homeostasis, and decreasing inflammation in RPE models (Wang & Tang, 2022). In blue light–exposed RPE, SFN activates the SIRT1/PGC-1α/Nrf2 axis, upregulates HO-1 and thioredoxin-1, blocks NF-κB nuclear translocation, preserves mitochondrial membrane potential, and enhances autophagy, all contributing to cytoprotection; Nrf2 inhibition abrogates SFN’s protective effect in this system (Yang et al., 2021). SFN can also activate lysosome-dependent transcriptional programs that mitigate oxidative stress; KEAP1–NRF2 cross-talk with TFEB/TFE3 and macroautophagy has been documented, offering a mechanistic link between Nrf2 activation and proteostasis/autophagy enhancement relevant to RPE degeneration (Falcão et al., 2025).

Anti-inflammatory and anti-angiogenic effects: Across RPE models, SFN suppresses proinflammatory mediators and pathways (e.g., reduces ICAM-1 and MCP-1; inhibits NF-κB activation), and in TGF-β–inflamed ARPE-19 reduces VEGF secretion, which is relevant to para-inflammatory remodeling in AMD (Chang et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2021; Bergandi et al., 2024). These effects likely contribute to preservation of junctional homeostasis by dampening cytokine-driven disruption.

Cell-type and context dependence: SFN response varies by cell type, stressor, and age-dependent pathway integrity. In some models, HO-1 mRNA is not induced even when protein rises, and under reduced Nrf2 function (as may occur in aged RPE), SFN efficacy can be limited, indicating a dependence on intact Nrf2 signaling and potential post-transcriptional regulation (Chang et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2024). Dosing and context also matter: low micromolar SFN protects RPE under oxidative stress, whereas very high concentrations can be deleterious in vitro (Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019).

Evidence for barrier-relevant endpoints: The provided sources do not present RPE TEER or FITC-dextran permeability data for SFN, and do not demonstrate claudin-1 induction in RPE (Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2024). One in vitro ARPE-19 TGF-β model reports that SFN (alone and with vitamin D) restores expression of ZO-1 and claudin-4 suppressed by TGF-β, reduces ROS and VEGF, and decreases proinflammatory cytokines; however, functional permeability or TEER measures were not included, and claudin-1 was not assessed (Bergandi et al., 2024). Therefore, SFN’s capacity to restore RPE barrier function remains mechanistically plausible—through redox normalization, suppression of inflammatory junctional disruption, and potential transcriptional effects on junctional proteins—but is not directly proven by barrier-function assays in the provided literature (Bergandi et al., 2024).

Expected Effect  
Assay context and rationale: The proposed assays—RPE monolayers subjected to A2E/blue light or NaIO₃ oxidative injury—interrogate oxidative stress pathways central to dry AMD pathophysiology and RPE barrier integrity. A2E/blue light models replicate lipofuscin-photosensitized oxidative injury; NaIO₃ models RPE oxidative cytotoxicity. In both settings, ROS accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, cytokine induction, and downstream tight-junction disruption degrade barrier integrity. The expectation is that SFN will activate Nrf2, increase reductive and detoxifying capacity (GSH, NQO1, HO-1, Trx1, GST), suppress NF-κB–driven inflammation, stabilize mitochondrial function, and secondarily preserve or restore tight-junction protein expression and continuity, resulting in improved TEER and reduced macromolecular flux (FITC-dextran) across the RPE monolayer.

Support for oxidative injury protection in RPE: In A2E/blue light–injured ARPE-19 monolayers, SFN preconditioning increases cell survival, elevates GSH, and induces NQO1 and GST—outcomes mechanistically consistent with improved tolerance to phototoxic oxidative stress (Zhou et al., 2006). In RPE challenged by chemical oxidants (t-BHP, H₂O₂), SFN reduces ROS, preserves mitochondrial membrane potential, increases nuclear Nrf2 DNA-binding, and induces NQO1, HO-1 (protein), GR, and GPx1 transcripts while suppressing ICAM-1 and MCP-1, thereby addressing both oxidative and inflammatory drivers of junctional disruption (Chang et al., 2020; Ye et al., 2013).

Support for HO-1/Nrf2 and auxiliary pathways: Under blue-light exposure, SFN activates Nrf2 and HO-1 in RPE with evidence of mechanistic necessity (Nrf2 inhibitor blocks protection), promotes autophagy (LC3BII and p62), and suppresses NF-κB p65 nuclear translocation—all changes that favor barrier preservation by limiting stress signaling and enhancing cellular cleanup mechanisms (Yang et al., 2021). In ischemia–reperfusion and RPE oxidative-stress mouse models, SFN-mediated rescue of retinal function depends on Nrf2 and HO-1, supporting the expected relevance of these axes in RPE-derived stress contexts (Pan et al., 2014; Falcão et al., 2025).

Evidence for junctional protein rescue: In TGF-β–inflamed ARPE-19, a recent report shows SFN (particularly with vitamin D) restores ZO-1 and claudin-4 expression suppressed by TGF-β, reduces IL-6/IL-8, reduces VEGF, and normalizes respiratory gene overexpression; this supports the expectation that SFN can reverse cytokine-driven loss of epithelial integrity markers (Bergandi et al., 2024). However, the provided material does not report TEER or FITC-dextran for SFN in RPE, and no data are provided for claudin-1 induction or ZO-1/claudin-1 continuity in oxidative (A2E/NaIO₃) models (Bergandi et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). Thus, while SFN is expected to improve TEER and reduce FITC-dextran flux in A2E or NaIO₃ assays through Nrf2-driven antioxidant enhancement and suppression of inflammatory/junctional injury, this is an unproven extrapolation that requires direct measurement in the proposed models (Bergandi et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024).

Target expression in RPE: All principal molecular targets are expressed in human RPE. Nrf2 activation and nuclear translocation occur in SFN-treated RPE-19/ARPE-19, with demonstrated induction of NQO1, HO-1 (protein in some contexts), thioredoxin-1, GCLM, and GST, and suppression of Txnip, ICAM-1, and MCP-1, anchoring the feasibility of modulating these axes in RPE cells (Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019; Chang et al., 2020; Ye et al., 2013; Zhou et al., 2006). HO-1 induction in RPE is clearly shown under blue-light stress; in vivo retinal protection requires HO-1, reinforcing target relevance to oxidative retinal injury (Yang et al., 2021; Pan et al., 2014).

Assay caveats: The provided literature does not contain RPE TEER or macromolecular flux data for SFN, nor does it document claudin-1 induction in RPE. Therefore, while SFN is expected to preserve barrier function, success criteria must include de novo demonstration of TEER rescue, FITC-dextran flux reduction, and ZO-1/claudin-1 continuity under A2E or NaIO₃ stress; these are critical data gaps to address in the proposed work (Zhang et al., 2024).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
- Strong mechanistic foundation in RPE and retina: SFN is a well-validated Nrf2 activator that increases GSH and induces multiple ARE-dependent genes (NQO1, GSTs, Trx1, GCLM; HO-1 in several ocular contexts) in RPE and retinal models, with reproducible protection against oxidative and phototoxic injury (A2E/blue light; H₂O₂/t-BHP) and anti-inflammatory activity (reduced ICAM-1, MCP-1, NF-κB signaling) (Zhou et al., 2006; Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019; Chang et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2021). In vivo, SFN attenuates retinal light damage, improves ERG outcomes, rescues cone function in an Nrf2-dependent manner, and protects against ischemia–reperfusion via HO-1—clear evidence that the compound engages relevant biology and can deliver functional benefit in the retina (Tanito et al., 2005; Falcão et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2014).  
- Multiple converging protective axes: Beyond KEAP1–Nrf2, SFN engages SIRT1/PGC-1α signaling, suppresses PARP1 overactivation to preserve NAD+/SIRT1, promotes autophagy/lysosome programs (TFEB/TFE3 linkage), and modulates mitochondrial dynamics (promotes fusion; reduces Drp1-mediated fission) even when Nrf2 is compromised. This polymechanistic profile is advantageous in RPE, where oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired autophagy, and inflammation co-drive degeneration (Wang & Tang, 2022; Falcão et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2021; Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019).  
- Preliminary evidence for epithelial integrity marker restoration: In TGF-β–inflamed ARPE-19, SFN (especially with vitamin D) restored ZO-1 and claudin-4 expression and reduced VEGF, providing the first reported signal that SFN can reverse cytokine-driven epithelial marker loss in an RPE model (albeit without functional barrier assays) (Bergandi et al., 2024).  
- Translational plausibility and human experience: SFN is orally administrable, with documented human systemic exposure and biomarker modulation; myrosinase co-administration enhances bioavailability from glucoraphanin. Although not yet tested clinically in dry AMD, SFN has a favorable translational narrative as a nutraceutical-derived Nrf2 activator with a safety and PD data trail in other indications (Zhang et al., 2024).

Weaknesses and gaps  
- No direct RPE barrier-function data: Across the provided literature there are no TEER or FITC-dextran permeability results for SFN in RPE monolayers, and no quantified immunofluorescence continuity data for ZO-1 or claudin-1 under oxidative stress. The sole report of “restored markers” addresses ZO-1 and claudin-4 expression after TGF-β, not barrier function or claudin-1 (Zhang et al., 2024; Bergandi et al., 2024). The proposal’s assertion of TEER rescue and FITC-dextran reduction remains speculative pending direct measurement in A2E or NaIO₃ models (Bergandi et al., 2024).  
- Uncertain regulation of tight junction gene set: The provided RPE datasets document induction of canonical Nrf2 targets (NQO1, HO-1 in some contexts, Trx1, GCLM, GSTs), but do not demonstrate SFN induction of claudin-1 in RPE. Without evidence that SFN directly upregulates claudin-1 or stabilizes its junctional localization in RPE, the hypothesis of “junction-supporting gene induction” should be reframed as “junction-preserving via redox and anti-inflammatory mechanisms” until proven otherwise (Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024).  
- Delivery and exposure to the RPE: SFN’s solubility, short half-life, and first-pass metabolism limit systemic exposure; blood–ocular barriers further constrain retinal delivery. Several in vivo retinal studies used intraperitoneal dosing, and one report found i.p. more effective than oral for light-damage protection, emphasizing the need to optimize delivery for ocular targets. Nanocarriers and formulation strategies are promising but remain preclinical (Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2024).  
- Dependence on Nrf2 competence and context: In models with impaired Nrf2 (potentially mirroring aged RPE), SFN efficacy is reduced; mechanisms such as mitochondrial fusion may provide partial rescue, but translational effect sizes could be diminished in elderly patients with Nrf2 decline. Additionally, gene-level responses show discordances (e.g., HO-1 mRNA vs protein), indicating context- and post-transcriptional control that complicate predictable pharmacodynamics (Zhang et al., 2024; Chang et al., 2020).  
- Evidence base heterogeneity: Some cited studies are from lower-quality journals; while the core mechanistic findings are corroborated by higher-quality work (e.g., IOVS, Toxicology and Applied Pharmacology, PLoS ONE), the barrier-marker restoration report requires independent replication with functional readouts and in primary human RPE to solidify translational credibility (Yang et al., 2021; Pan et al., 2014; Bergandi et al., 2024).

Decision implications and next steps  
Given the robust Nrf2/ARE activation, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory efficacy in RPE/retina, and the breadth of auxiliary protective mechanisms, SFN remains a mechanistically precise candidate for RPE oxidative injury relevant to dry AMD. However, the core claim of “barrier restoration” is currently supported only by marker-expression rescue (ZO-1 and claudin-4) in an inflamed ARPE-19 model without functional permeability data, and there is no evidence for claudin-1 induction or barrier rescue in A2E or NaIO₃ models in the provided literature (Bergandi et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). To de-risk the program and validate the hypothesis, the following experiments are mandatory:  
- TEER and FITC-dextran assays in differentiated human RPE (primary or iPSC-RPE) under A2E/blue light or NaIO₃ insult, with SFN dose–response and timing; include positive controls for barrier rescue (e.g., dexamethasone) (Bergandi et al., 2024).  
- Quantitative junctional immunofluorescence (ZO-1, claudin-1, claudin-4, occludin) with continuity analysis; test whether SFN normalizes localization and expression under oxidative/cytokine stress (Zhang et al., 2024).  
- Nrf2 pathway necessity tests (ML385, Nrf2 siRNA) and HO-1 inhibition (ZnPP) to define mechanism-of-action in barrier rescue; given HO-1’s necessity in vivo, confirm whether HO-1 contributes to barrier endpoints in RPE (Pan et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2021).  
- Mitochondrial dynamics and autophagy readouts (Drp1, Mfn2, LC3BII/p62) to quantify auxiliary pathways that may mediate barrier preservation when Nrf2 is compromised (Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2021).  
- PK/PD bridging: quantify SFN (and metabolites) in plasma and ocular tissues after oral vs i.p. dosing in rodents; evaluate delivery systems (e.g., albumin nanocarriers) for RPE exposure; incorporate myrosinase co-administration for oral strategies; relate exposures to effective in vitro EC₅₀s for barrier rescue (Zhang et al., 2024).  
- Aged and Nrf2-impaired models: evaluate efficacy in aged RPE or Nrf2-hypomorphic contexts to assess translational robustness (Zhang et al., 2024; Falcão et al., 2025).

If TEER and permeability rescue are demonstrated with mechanistic attribution to Nrf2/HO-1 and/or auxiliary pathways, and if ocular PK can be solved to achieve efficacious exposures, SFN would merit progression to in vivo dry AMD–relevant models (e.g., subretinal NaIO₃, chronic photo-oxidative stress) with structural (OCT) and functional (ERG) endpoints, plus RPE barrier imaging, before considering a human proof-of-concept study.

In sum, the totality of evidence strongly supports SFN for oxidative and inflammatory protection of RPE and retina with multiple convergent mechanisms and translational plausibility. The critical missing element is direct demonstration that these effects translate into bona fide RPE barrier restoration (TEER/permeability) under AMD-relevant oxidative stress, and a practical path to deliver sufficient drug to the RPE in humans. Addressing these gaps should be the focus of immediate preclinical validation (Zhou et al., 2006; Hernández-Rabaza et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2021; Pan et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2024; Bergandi et al., 2024).
