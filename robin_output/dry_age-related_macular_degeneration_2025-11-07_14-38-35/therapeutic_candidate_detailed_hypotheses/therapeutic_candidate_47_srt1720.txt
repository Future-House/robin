Proposal for SRT1720 (Selleck S7547)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
SRT1720 is a synthetic small-molecule sirtuin-activating compound (STAC) discovered by high-throughput screening and medicinal chemistry optimization at Sirtris/GSK as part of an imidazo[1,2-b]thiazole/quinoxalyl chemotype campaign intended to yield potent, selective SIRT1 activators that surpass resveratrol’s low potency and poor pharmacokinetics (EC1.5 ≈ 0.16 µM for SRT1720 in the original labeled-peptide assay) (Granchi & Minutolo, 2018, pp. 6–8; Kumar & Chauhan, 2016, pp. 19–22). It is widely cited as representative of first-generation synthetic STACs structurally unrelated to polyphenols and designed to allosterically enhance SIRT1 deacetylase activity in a substrate-dependent manner (Granchi & Minutolo, 2018, pp. 6–8; Mazzone, 2018, pp. 74–77). The class has been used extensively in preclinical metabolic and inflammatory models to mimic caloric restriction signatures, improve glucose homeostasis, augment mitochondrial biogenesis via PGC-1α deacetylation, and suppress NF-κB–dependent inflammatory gene expression; SRT1720 and congeners have also been deployed to test vascular and lifespan endpoints in rodents (Granchi & Minutolo, 2018, pp. 6–9; Gano et al., 2014, pp. 18–23). Notably, the biochemical foundation for direct SIRT1 activation by SRT1720 has been challenged by independent studies showing that SRT1720 does not activate SIRT1 on native protein substrates and exhibits broad off-target pharmacology, although subsequent structural and mutational work established an assisted allosteric activation mechanism for certain STACs on specific substrates (Pacholec et al., 2010, pp. 1–2; Mazzone, 2018, pp. 74–77).

Therapeutic History  
SRT1720 has an extensive preclinical record outside ophthalmology. In multiple rodent metabolic disease models (diet-induced obesity, ob/ob, Zucker fa/fa), oral SRT1720 improved glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity, increased markers of mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative metabolism, and reduced inflammatory signaling, consistent with deacetylation of p53, PGC-1α, and FOXO1 and suppression of NF-κB target genes (JNK, iNOS, TRAF2) (Kumar & Chauhan, 2016, pp. 19–22; Granchi & Minutolo, 2018, pp. 6–8). It has been reported to extend mean and maximum lifespan in mice and to reduce apoptosis after ischemia/reperfusion injury, though some effects required high daily doses (up to 500 mg/kg/day), reflecting rapid metabolism and modest systemic exposure (Granchi & Minutolo, 2018, pp. 8–9). In vascular aging models, SRT1720 reversed endothelial dysfunction, normalized arterial SIRT1 activity and superoxide levels, upregulated COX-2–dependent vasodilators, and blunted NF-κB activation, thereby reducing vascular inflammation in aged mice (Gano et al., 2014, pp. 18–23). At the same time, independent cross-target panels and in vivo work raised red flags: SRT1720 failed to improve certain metabolic endpoints, exhibited promiscuous off-target activity, and in selected oncology models promoted metastasis, highlighting context-dependent biology and safety concerns (Pacholec et al., 2010, pp. 1–2; Mazzone, 2018, pp. 70–74; Bononi et al., 2022, p. 14).

With respect to retinal and ocular disease, direct RPE/outer blood–retinal barrier evidence exists for SRT1720 in vitro: in primary human adult retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) challenged with oligomerized amyloid-β1–42, SRT1720 restored barrier integrity, preserved tight junction architecture (ZO-1 and occludin), and suppressed inflammatory mediators (IL-6, IL-8) and MMP-9; these effects were lost upon SIRT1 knockdown, indicating SIRT1 dependence (Cao et al., 2013, pp. 1–2). In diabetic retinopathy models, SRT1720 activated the SIRT1–LXRα axis in the retina and in retinal endothelial/progenitor cells, deacetylating LXRα, upregulating ABCA1/ABCG1-mediated cholesterol efflux, lowering retinal cholesterol, and reducing inflammation; in vivo, SIRT1 activation (by pharmacology or fasting) preserved vascular and neural retina and improved ERG/OKR measures, though these reports are preprint and focused on diabetes rather than AMD (Hammer et al., 2019, pp. 1–4, 15–19). No clinical or veterinary use of SRT1720 for dry AMD has been reported; there are also no in vivo AMD model data for SRT1720 to our knowledge in the provided literature (Granchi & Minutolo, 2018, pp. 8–9; Cen, Youn, & Sauve, 2011, pp. 16–17). However, class support exists: the clinical-stage SIRT1 activator SRT2104 shows neuroprotective, anti-inflammatory, and p65/p53 deacetylation effects in retinal ischemia/reperfusion and optic nerve injury models, suggesting that pharmacologic SIRT1 activation can protect ocular tissues, although these results pertain to a different chemotype (Chang et al., 2024, pp. 7–8).

Mechanism of Action  
Enzymatic and structural basis. SIRT1 is a nuclear/cytoplasmic, NAD+-dependent lysine deacylase that catalyzes transfer of the acyl group from ε-acetyllysine to the 2′-OH of ADP-ribose derived from NAD+, yielding nicotinamide and 2′/3′-O-acetyl-ADP-ribose; catalysis proceeds through an imidate/dioxolium intermediate and is tightly linked to cellular NAD+ levels (Cen et al., 2011, pp. 1–2; Szczepankiewicz & Ng, 2008, pp. 2–3). SIRT1 deacetylates histones (H1/H3/H4) and numerous non-histone substrates that regulate stress responses, metabolism, survival/death, and inflammation, including p53, FOXO1/3, PGC-1α, NF-κB p65 (RelA), STAT3, HIFs, and Atg proteins (Cen et al., 2011, pp. 1–2; Sinclair & Guarente, 2014, pp. 2–4; Taurone et al., 2022, pp. 3–5). Of particular relevance to RPE barrier biology, SIRT1 deacetylates RelA/p65 at Lys310 to constrain NF-κB transcriptional activity, thereby repressing downstream inflammatory genes (e.g., IL-6, IL-8, MMP-9) that disrupt tight junctions; SIRT1 also preserves IκBα, limiting NF-κB nuclear translocation (Cao et al., 2013, pp. 1–2).

Pharmacologic modulation by SRT1720 and STACs. The initial activation claims for SRT1720 arose from fluorophore-labeled peptide assays in which SRT1720 reduced apparent Km and increased SIRT1 activity, with evidence that activator binding occurs at an allosteric N-terminal site and may require substrate engagement; mutational analysis (E230K/A) in SIRT1 abolishes STAC binding/activation, supporting an assisted allosteric mechanism for certain STAC–substrate combinations (Granchi & Minutolo, 2018, pp. 6–8; Mazzone, 2018, pp. 74–77). However, robust biochemical work using native protein substrates and label-free readouts concluded that SRT1720 and related SRTs do not activate SIRT1 on native substrates, and that “activation” with labeled peptides reflects compound interaction with the fluorophore; the same study documented extensive off-target pharmacology across receptors, enzymes, transporters, and ion channels (Pacholec et al., 2010, pp. 1–2). The broader literature reconciles these results by emphasizing substrate-dependent activation—some substrates and engineered constructs may support bona fide allosteric activation—alongside the necessity of orthogonal biochemical and cellular target-engagement assays (e.g., NMR/SPR/ITC, endogenous substrate deacetylation, genetic epistasis) to validate mechanism in each biology (Liu et al., 2023, p. 6; Cen et al., 2011, pp. 16–17; Mazzone, 2018, pp. 74–77).

Cellular pathways engaged by SRT1720. In non-ocular tissues, SRT1720 reduces acetyl-p53, enhances PGC-1α deacetylation/activation and mitochondrial biogenesis, augments oxidative metabolism, and suppresses NF-κB transcriptional activity and target gene induction; these effects are consistent with a SIRT1-centered mechanism yet may involve ancillary networks (e.g., AMPK) (Kumar & Chauhan, 2016, pp. 19–22; Granchi & Minutolo, 2018, pp. 6–9). In vascular aging, SRT1720 normalizes age-depressed SIRT1 activity in arteries, reduces superoxide generation, reverses NF-κB activation, and shifts vasodilator production toward COX-2–derived mediators—together restoring endothelium-dependent dilation (Gano et al., 2014, pp. 18–23). In retinal contexts (diabetic retinopathy), SIRT1 activation by SRT1720 deacetylates LXRα, upregulates ABCA1/ABCG1-mediated cholesterol efflux, lowers retinal cholesterol, and reduces inflammation; fasting similarly elevates SIRT1 activity and recapitulates these benefits (Hammer et al., 2019, pp. 1–4, 15–19).

RPE-specific and barrier-relevant mechanisms. In primary human adult RPE, Aβ1–42 decreases tight junction integrity through downregulation/mislocalization of occludin and ZO-1 and induces IL-6/IL-8 and MMP-9 via NF-κB activation and IκBα loss; SRT1720 restores barrier integrity, suppresses IL-6/IL-8/MMP-9, and inhibits NF-κB activation while preserving IκBα—effects that disappear with SIRT1 siRNA—demonstrating SIRT1-dependent protection of the outer blood-retinal barrier (oBRB) (Cao et al., 2013, pp. 1–2). In broader vascular/endothelial biology, SIRT1 activation is linked to stabilization of tight junction complexes (e.g., claudin-5/ZO-1), improved TEER, and reduced senescence-associated hyperpermeability, although effects can be context-dependent and paradoxical in some injury paradigms (Law et al., 2024, pp. 16–17). Importantly, one report outside the eye indicates SRT1720 can increase pulmonary microvascular endothelial tight junction permeability via RhoA/ROCK pathway inhibition, underscoring the need to confirm barrier effects in RPE specifically and under AMD-relevant stressors (Law et al., 2024, pp. 14–16).

Target expression. SIRT1 is expressed in RPE and retinal vascular cells and is downregulated by inflammatory cues (e.g., LPS lowers SIRT1 expression/activity in ARPE-19), while pharmacologic activation by resveratrol restores SIRT1/DNMT axis function and counters oxidative/inflammatory stress in RPE models; SIRT1’s antagonism of NF-κB is a recurring theme in RPE inflammation (Maugeri et al., 2018, pp. 5–7; Maugeri et al., 2018, pp. 12–14). Together with direct SRT1720 effects in primary RPE under Aβ challenge, these data establish the presence and druggability of SIRT1 in the cell type of interest (Cao et al., 2013, pp. 1–2; Maugeri et al., 2018, pp. 5–7).

Safety and selectivity considerations. Beyond the Pacholec et al. off-target panel, SRT1720 has been reported to inhibit SIRT3 potently and to exert SIRT1-independent cytotoxicity in adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma, and it promoted tumor cell migration and lung metastasis in a breast cancer model—context-dependent but salient translational risks (Mazzone, 2018, pp. 70–74; Chang et al., 2024, pp. 16–17; Bononi et al., 2022, p. 14). Systemically, SRT1720 shows rapid metabolism and low bioavailability in some species, necessitating high dosing to elicit pharmacology; ocular penetration and exposure have not been characterized in the provided literature (Granchi & Minutolo, 2018, pp. 8–9).

Expected Effect  
Assay context. The proposed assay involves aged or cytokine/Aβ-challenged human primary RPE monolayers assessed for TEER and paracellular permeability, with readouts of tight junction proteins (ZO-1 and occludin) and inflammatory mediators (IL-6, IL-8, MMP-9). Based on the RPE Aβ model and vascular aging data, SRT1720 is expected to preserve or restore TEER, reduce permeability, maintain ZO-1/occludin localization at cell–cell borders, and suppress IL-6/IL-8/MMP-9 induction via SIRT1-dependent suppression of NF-κB signaling and stabilization of IκBα; p65 Lys310 deacetylation should be demonstrable biochemically (Cao et al., 2013, pp. 1–2; Gano et al., 2014, pp. 18–23).

Mechanistic chain. In inflamed/senescent RPE, SIRT1 activation by SRT1720 should reduce acetylation of RelA/p65 at Lys310, lowering NF-κB transcriptional activity and downstream induction of MMP-9 and proinflammatory cytokines that directly or indirectly dismantle tight junctions (Cao et al., 2013, pp. 1–2). Reduced MMP-9 should attenuate extracellular matrix remodeling and junctional protein cleavage, whereas lower IL-6/IL-8 should blunt autocrine/paracrine inflammatory circuits that destabilize the oBRB (Cao et al., 2013, pp. 1–2). In addition, SRT1720 may favorably reprogram lipid handling via SIRT1–LXRα deacetylation, increasing ABCA1/ABCG1 and enhancing cholesterol efflux in retinal cells/macrophages; improved cholesterol homeostasis in the subretinal environment is directionally consistent with reduced inflammatory stress on RPE tight junctions, though this LXR axis has been shown in DR rather than AMD (Hammer et al., 2019, pp. 1–4, 15–19). Mitochondrial-supportive effects via PGC-1α deacetylation and mitophagy/autophagy enhancement could further reduce mtROS-driven junctional damage in RPE (Granchi & Minutolo, 2018, pp. 6–8; Taurone et al., 2022, pp. 3–5).

Clarification on epigenetic mechanism. The specific hypothesis that SIRT1 activation increases “histone and non-histone deacetylation at tight junction gene loci … enhancing promoter accessibility and boosting ZO-1/occludin expression” is partially mis-specified with respect to canonical chromatin biology; histone deacetylation generally correlates with chromatin compaction and reduced transcription at the affected loci (Cen et al., 2011, pp. 1–2). The best-supported mechanism for barrier rescue in RPE involves SIRT1-mediated deacetylation of non-histone transcription factors (NF-κB p65) to repress inflammatory gene programs (e.g., MMP-9, IL-6, IL-8) that destabilize tight junctions, combined with preservation of junctional protein localization and integrity; direct epigenetic upregulation of TJP1 (ZO-1) or OCLN promoters via histone deacetylation is not supported here (Cao et al., 2013, pp. 1–2). Nonetheless, SIRT1 can modulate epigenetics indirectly (e.g., DNMT1 function and DNA methylation status in RPE), and has been linked to tight junction regulation in other tissues (e.g., claudin-5 activation via KLF4 deacetylation), so secondary or cell-type–specific epigenetic contributions cannot be excluded and merit targeted ChIP-qPCR/ATAC-seq evaluation at TJ gene loci (Maugeri et al., 2018, pp. 5–7; Maugeri et al., 2018, pp. 12–14; Abbotto et al., 2022, pp. 36–38).

Dose, timing, and SIRT1 dependence. In the cited primary RPE Aβ study, pharmacologic SIRT1 activation restored barrier integrity, and the effect was abrogated by SIRT1 siRNA; similar genetic/pharmacologic epistasis using EX-527 should be included to confirm SRT1720’s SIRT1 dependence in the proposed assay (Cao et al., 2013, pp. 1–2). Concentrations around 0.3–3 µM are reasonable starting points given reported cellular activity ranges of STACs, but care is required to monitor off-target effects on barrier function, as SRT1720 increased PMEC permeability via RhoA/ROCK inhibition in a non-ocular setting (Law et al., 2024, pp. 14–16).

Auxiliary biomarkers. Mechanistic readouts should include: acetyl-RelA/p65 (Lys310) immunoblotting, IκBα levels, nuclear NF-κB p65 translocation, MMP-9 promoter activity/gelatin zymography, and ABCA1/ABCG1 mRNA (to probe LXRα axis engagement). Mitochondrial endpoints (mitochondrial membrane potential, mtROS, PGC-1α acetylation) can provide supportive evidence for SIRT1/PGC-1α pathway engagement in RPE (Cao et al., 2013, pp. 1–2; Hammer et al., 2019, pp. 1–4; Granchi & Minutolo, 2018, pp. 6–8).

Overall Evaluation  
Strength of biological rationale in RPE/oBRB. Among candidate SIRT1-targeting strategies, SRT1720 stands out because it has already demonstrated SIRT1-dependent rescue of RPE barrier integrity in a primary human Aβ1–42 challenge model, with concomitant suppression of oBRB-disruptive mediators (IL-6, IL-8, MMP-9) and restoration of tight junction protein organization (ZO-1, occludin) via NF-κB pathway inhibition and IκBα preservation (Cao et al., 2013, pp. 1–2). This is the exact pathogenic axis implicated in dry AMD—chronic para-inflammation and protease induction leading to junctional disassembly—and it directly addresses the team’s TEER/permeability endpoint. Complementary retinal data from DR models show that SRT1720 activates the SIRT1–LXRα cholesterol efflux pathway and reduces retinal inflammation while preserving neural/vascular function in vivo, underscoring the convergence of SIRT1-mediated anti-inflammatory, lipid-handling, and mitochondrial benefits across retinal compartments (albeit in diabetes, not AMD) (Hammer et al., 2019, pp. 1–4, 15–19). In vascular aging, systemic SRT1720 reduces oxidative stress and NF-κB activation and restores endothelial function, reinforcing its anti-inflammatory/antioxidant profile in age-related tissues (Gano et al., 2014, pp. 18–23). Collectively, these data support moving SRT1720 forward into rigorous, RPE-focused validation of barrier protection and mechanistic epistasis.

Critical mechanistic caveats. The activation mechanism of SRT1720 remains controversial. The strongest mechanistic caution comes from Pacholec et al., who showed that SRT1720 does not activate SIRT1 with native substrates and instead interacts with assay fluorophores; they also reported broad off-target activities and failure to elicit certain expected metabolic benefits (Pacholec et al., 2010, pp. 1–2). Later structural and mutational studies established that some STACs engage an assisted allosteric site (E230-dependent) in a substrate-dependent manner, reconciling why activation can be substrate- and assay-context specific; nonetheless, this mandates that our RPE program rigorously prove SIRT1 dependence using genetic loss-of-function and a pharmacologic antagonist (EX-527), and demonstrate endogenous substrate deacetylation (e.g., p65 Lys310) rather than relying on labeled peptide assays (Mazzone, 2018, pp. 74–77; Cen et al., 2011, pp. 16–17; Liu et al., 2023, p. 6). The proposal’s epigenetic premise—histone deacetylation at tight junction gene promoters increasing expression—does not align with canonical chromatin logic; we should pivot our primary mechanistic hypothesis to SIRT1’s non-histone deacetylation of NF-κB p65 and its downstream consequences on MMP-9 and cytokines that secondarily preserve TJ proteins (Cao et al., 2013, pp. 1–2; Cen et al., 2011, pp. 1–2). If epigenetic regulation is to be invoked, it should be tested explicitly with ChIP on histone acetyl marks (H3K9ac/H4K16ac) at TJP1 and OCLN loci and interpreted cautiously (Cen et al., 2011, pp. 1–2).

Barrier-function risk signals. While most endothelial/epithelial SIRT1 activation literature points to barrier stabilization and anti-inflammatory protection, SRT1720 increased tight-junction permeability in pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells via RhoA/ROCK inhibition in one study, suggesting compound- and cell-type-specific liabilities for barrier function (Law et al., 2024, pp. 14–16). This makes dose titration and pathway dissection essential in RPE: TEER and dextran permeability should be monitored across a broad concentration range (e.g., 0.03–10 µM), with imaging of ZO-1/occludin continuity and RhoA/ROCK signaling readouts to ensure we are not trading inflammation suppression for cytoskeletal junction destabilization in the ocular context (Law et al., 2024, pp. 14–16).

Translational gaps: PK, exposure, and ocular delivery. SRT1720 often required high systemic doses to achieve pharmacology in vivo due to rapid metabolism and low bioavailability; ocular pharmacokinetics, retinal/choroidal distribution, and RPE exposure are unknown from the provided literature (Granchi & Minutolo, 2018, pp. 8–9). Without local delivery strategies, achieving therapeutic RPE exposure while avoiding systemic off-targets may be challenging. Lessons from SRT2104 development—oral tolerability in humans but modest target engagement in short trials, and the need for targeted delivery platforms for certain tissues—argue for prioritizing ocular delivery formats (intravitreal, suprachoroidal, or sustained-release periocular) and PK/PD modeling to ensure sufficient RPE exposure with acceptable safety margins (Chang et al., 2024, pp. 10–11, 14–16).

Safety liabilities and off-targets. SRT1720 exhibits off-target pharmacology, including reported SIRT3 inhibition and SIRT1-independent cytotoxicity, and promoted metastasis in a breast cancer model—context-dependent findings that nevertheless narrow the therapeutic window and emphasize the importance of local dosing and rigorous ocular toxicity studies (Pacholec et al., 2010, pp. 1–2; Mazzone, 2018, pp. 70–74; Chang et al., 2024, pp. 16–17; Bononi et al., 2022, p. 14). Any plan to advance SRT1720 must incorporate: (1) in vitro hERG/ion channel panels, (2) ocular cell cytotoxicity and apoptosis profiling (caspase 3/7, TUNEL), and (3) in vivo ocular tolerability studies with dose-ranging in rodents (intraocular dosing) before efficacy models.

Comparators and class inference. The clear, SIRT1-dependent oBRB rescue seen with SRT1720 in Aβ-challenged primary RPE is a strong anchor for this program (Cao et al., 2013, pp. 1–2). Yet, because SRT1720’s activation mechanism is contested and its PK/safety profile is imperfect, it is prudent to include a cross-chemotype comparator with better clinical pedigree (e.g., SRT2104) to test whether “SIRT1 activation” per se confers the barrier benefit in our RPE assays; SRT2104 has demonstrated SIRT1-mediated p65/p53 deacetylation and neuroinflammation suppression in retinal/optic nerve injury models, offering mechanistic and translational relevance even though it is not SRT1720 (Chang et al., 2024, pp. 7–8). Positive concordance across SRT1720 and SRT2104 in RPE TEER and TJ preservation, both reversed by EX-527 or SIRT1 siRNA, would strongly support target validation independent of scaffold (Chang et al., 2024, pp. 7–8).

Go/No-Go experimental plan (to de-risk and validate the hypothesis)  
- Biochemical target engagement in RPE: Demonstrate decreased acetyl-RelA/p65 Lys310, preserved IκBα, and reduced nuclear p65 in cytokine/Aβ-challenged primary human adult RPE after SRT1720; reverse with EX-527 and SIRT1 siRNA (Cao et al., 2013, pp. 1–2).  
- Barrier function and junctional integrity: Quantify TEER and 4 kDa/70 kDa dextran flux; immunostain ZO-1 and occludin for continuity/line intensity along cell borders; quantify protein levels and detergent-resistant fractions; test across 0.03–10 µM SRT1720 with and without inflammatory challenge; monitor RhoA/ROCK signaling to rule out compound-induced junctional destabilization (Cao et al., 2013, pp. 1–2; Law et al., 2024, pp. 14–16).  
- Inflammatory and protease readouts: Measure IL-6/IL-8 secretion (ELISA) and MMP-9 activity (zymography) and transcription (qPCR), and NF-κB reporter assays; anticipate suppression by SRT1720, reversed by SIRT1 blockade (Cao et al., 2013, pp. 1–2).  
- Lipid handling and mitochondrial stress: In RPE under lipid or oxidative stress, measure ABCA1/ABCG1 expression (qPCR), cellular cholesterol content, PGC-1α acetylation state, mtROS, and mitochondrial function; anticipate SIRT1–LXRα and PGC-1α pathway engagement, mindful that the strongest evidence exists in DR models (Hammer et al., 2019, pp. 1–4; Hammer et al., 2019, pp. 15–19; Granchi & Minutolo, 2018, pp. 6–8).  
- Epigenetic exploration (optional, to test the proposal’s chromatin hypothesis): Perform ChIP-qPCR for H3K9ac/H4K16ac at TJP1 and OCLN promoters and ATAC-seq for chromatin accessibility; given canonical HDAC biology, do not expect histone deacetylation to increase promoter accessibility; instead, look for preserved junctional protein expression due to anti-inflammatory signaling (Cen et al., 2011, pp. 1–2).  
- In vivo proof-of-concept and PK: After establishing in vitro efficacy, evaluate a local-delivery formulation of SRT1720 in an Aβ-induced oBRB disruption model or sodium iodate low-dose RPE injury model in aged mice, with readouts of RPE barrier function (in vivo fluorescein angiography, immunostaining of ZO-1/occludin), retinal function (ERG), and ocular PK (vitreous, retina, RPE/choroid tissue levels) (Cao et al., 2013, pp. 1–2).  
- Class comparator: Run a parallel arm with SRT2104 to assess target-class translatability; prior ocular neuroprotection and p65/p53 deacetylation by SRT2104 suggest that barrier benefits, if SIRT1-driven, should not be unique to SRT1720 (Chang et al., 2024, pp. 7–8).  

Decision framework  
- Go if SRT1720 reproducibly improves TEER/permeability and preserves TJ proteins in human primary RPE under AMD-relevant stress, with clear SIRT1 dependence (genetic and pharmacologic), no deleterious barrier effects across a clinically achievable concentration range, and acceptable ocular cytotoxicity margins (Cao et al., 2013, pp. 1–2).  
- No-Go (or switch scaffold) if: (1) TEER benefit is absent or inconsistent; (2) barrier is worsened at relevant concentrations; (3) effects are not reversed by SIRT1 blockade; or (4) significant ocular cytotoxicity/off-target liabilities emerge; in that case, consider shifting to SRT2104 or next-generation STACs with improved selectivity and PK, or to NAD+ augmentation strategies (e.g., NMN/NR) that elevate SIRT1 activity indirectly (Law et al., 2024, pp. 14–16; Chang et al., 2024, pp. 10–11; Chen et al., 2020, pp. 13–14).

Balanced risk–benefit view for dry AMD  
- Strengths: Direct, SIRT1-dependent restoration of RPE barrier integrity and tight junction architecture by SRT1720 in a human primary RPE Aβ model; robust mechanistic linkage to NF-κB suppression, IκBα preservation, and MMP-9/cytokine downregulation; convergent retinal data in DR that SIRT1 activation promotes cholesterol efflux, reduces inflammation, and preserves retinal structure/function; vascular aging data showing anti-inflammatory and antioxidant actions in aged tissue (Cao et al., 2013, pp. 1–2; Hammer et al., 2019, pp. 1–4, 15–19; Gano et al., 2014, pp. 18–23).  
- Weaknesses: Ongoing controversy over SRT1720’s direct SIRT1 activation on native substrates and documented off-target pharmacology; potential barrier liability via RhoA/ROCK effects in non-ocular endothelium; PK limitations (low bioavailability, rapid metabolism) and absence of ocular PK/PD data; context-dependent tumor biology signals (metastasis promotion in breast cancer model; SIRT1-independent cytotoxicity) that elevate safety scrutiny; no in vivo AMD model data for SRT1720 in the current literature (Pacholec et al., 2010, pp. 1–2; Law et al., 2024, pp. 14–16; Granchi & Minutolo, 2018, pp. 8–9; Mazzone, 2018, pp. 70–74; Bononi et al., 2022, p. 14).  
- Opportunities: A focused, mechanistically rigorous RPE barrier program can validate SIRT1 as a tractable oBRB target and establish whether SRT1720’s RPE benefits are robust and SIRT1-dependent; class transferability testing (SRT2104) can mitigate scaffold-specific risks; local ocular delivery could maximize ocular exposure while minimizing systemic off-targets; biomarker development (acetyl-p65, IL-6/IL-8/MMP-9 suppression, junctional continuity metrics) can support translational PK/PD (Cao et al., 2013, pp. 1–2; Chang et al., 2024, pp. 7–8, 14–16).  
- Threats: If STAC-mediated barrier modulation is strongly substrate/cell-context dependent, results may be irreproducible across RPE donors and stress paradigms; if ocular exposure required for efficacy exceeds tolerable levels, systemic/off-target risk becomes unacceptable; if the benefit relies on off-target activities, clinical translation will be precarious (Liu et al., 2023, p. 6; Pacholec et al., 2010, pp. 1–2).

Conclusion  
On balance, SRT1720 merits a tightly controlled, mechanism-first validation campaign in human primary RPE aimed at confirming SIRT1-dependent rescue of TEER and tight junction integrity under AMD-relevant stress, leveraging the compelling Aβ-RPE data as a starting point (Cao et al., 2013, pp. 1–2). The proposal’s epigenetic claim should be reframed: the most defensible mechanism is non-histone deacetylation of NF-κB p65 with suppression of inflammatory/proteolytic programs that secondarily protect junctions; chromatin-level promoter activation of TJP1/OCLN by histone deacetylation is unlikely and should not be presumed (Cao et al., 2013, pp. 1–2; Cen et al., 2011, pp. 1–2). Because SRT1720’s direct activation mechanism is contested and its off-target/PK profile is imperfect, we strongly recommend including SRT2104 as a class control and prioritizing local ocular delivery and rigorous SIRT1 epistasis throughout. A Go decision would require unambiguous SIRT1 dependence, robust barrier protection without paradoxical permeability increases, and clean ocular safety at active exposures; otherwise, pivot to alternative SIRT1-targeting modalities (e.g., SRT2104 or NAD+ augmentation) with better translational properties (Chang et al., 2024, pp. 7–8; Chen et al., 2020, pp. 13–14; Chang et al., 2024, pp. 10–11).

Overall, SRT1720 is a plausible mechanistic candidate for dry AMD through SIRT1-mediated suppression of NF-κB and MMP-9 with restoration of RPE barrier function, supported by primary RPE evidence; yet its development risk is material due to mechanism controversies, PK/safety constraints, and context-dependent barrier effects that mandate rigorous validation before advancing (Cao et al., 2013, pp. 1–2; Pacholec et al., 2010, pp. 1–2; Law et al., 2024, pp. 14–16; Granchi & Minutolo, 2018, pp. 8–9; Gano et al., 2014, pp. 18–23).

Supportive details and context (selected)  
- SRT1720 restores barrier integrity, ZO-1/occludin architecture, and suppresses IL-6/IL-8/MMP-9 in Aβ-challenged human primary RPE; effects are SIRT1-dependent and mediated by NF-κB/IκBα (Cao et al., 2013, pp. 1–2).  
- SRT1720 activates SIRT1–LXRα signaling in retina/retinal cells, increases ABCA1/G1, lowers cholesterol, reduces inflammation, and preserves function in DR models; fasting reproduces these effects (preprint) (Hammer et al., 2019, pp. 1–4, 15–19).  
- Vascular aging reversal by SRT1720: normalized arterial SIRT1 activity, reduced superoxide, reversed NF-κB activation, increased COX-2 vasodilators; improved endothelial function (Gano et al., 2014, pp. 18–23).  
- Biochemical controversy: no direct SIRT1 activation on native substrates; activation in labeled assays is fluorophore-mediated; off-target promiscuity documented (Pacholec et al., 2010, pp. 1–2).  
- Assisted allosteric mechanism for STACs supported by E230 mutations and structural data; activation is substrate-dependent (Mazzone, 2018, pp. 74–77; Cen et al., 2011, pp. 16–17).  
- Off-target/safety signals: SIRT3 inhibition by SRT1720, SIRT1-independent cytotoxicity, pro-metastatic effects in a breast cancer model; high systemic dosing, rapid metabolism (Mazzone, 2018, pp. 70–74; Chang et al., 2024, pp. 16–17; Bononi et al., 2022, p. 14; Granchi & Minutolo, 2018, pp. 8–9).  
- Barrier risk: increased tight-junction permeability in pulmonary microvascular endothelium via RhoA/ROCK inhibition by SRT1720 (Law et al., 2024, pp. 14–16).  
- Class comparator SRT2104: retinal/optic neuroprotection, decreased acetyl-p65/p53/STAT3, reduced neuroinflammation; supports ocular SIRT1 activation concept (Chang et al., 2024, pp. 7–8).  
- SIRT1 expression/activity in RPE is modulated by inflammation; resveratrol restores SIRT1/DNMT functions and LINE-1 methylation; NF-κB–SIRT1 antagonism is central in RPE stress (Maugeri et al., 2018, pp. 5–7; Maugeri et al., 2018, pp. 12–14).  
- SIRT1 biology aligns with AMD mechanisms: suppression of NF-κB/AP-1/STAT3, enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis and antioxidant response, autophagy support; SIRT1 deficiency increases retinal apoptosis via p53 hyperacetylation (Taurone et al., 2022, pp. 3–5).
