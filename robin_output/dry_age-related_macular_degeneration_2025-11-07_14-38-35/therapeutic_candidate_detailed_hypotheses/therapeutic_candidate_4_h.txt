Proposal for H-1152 Dihydrochloride (Tocris 2061)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
H-1152 Dihydrochloride is a synthetic small‐molecule inhibitor discovered and developed as part of the class of ATP‐competitive inhibitors targeting Rho‐associated coiled‐coil kinase (ROCK). It is formulated as a dihydrochloride salt (Tocris 2061) and has been characterized for its exceptional potency against ROCK isoforms, with reported IC₅₀ values in the low nanomolar range (0.003 µM for ROCK1 and 0.014 µM for ROCK2). As an ATP-competitive type I kinase inhibitor, it possesses a chemical structure that enables it to fit into the highly conserved ATP-binding cavity of ROCK and thereby prevents phosphorylation of downstream targets. This class of compounds has historically been used to explore the roles of ROCK in cytoskeletal dynamics, cell contractility, and migration, and has been applied in various preclinical models of ocular disorders such as glaucoma and corneal endothelial injury (Iancu et al., 2021; Meekins et al., 2016).

Therapeutic History:  
Although H-1152 itself has not been clinically approved, its pharmacologic profile as a ROCK inhibitor has been extensively studied in preclinical ocular models. Multiple studies have employed H-1152 in experiments that assessed its effects on corneal endothelial cell migration, proliferation, and wound healing. For instance, in in vitro assays using porcine corneal endothelial cells, H-1152 significantly enhanced cell migration and proliferation compared with control treatments and with other inhibitors like Y-27632. In vivo topical application in rabbit corneal surgical wound models demonstrated accelerated wound closure and resolution of corneal edema without significant toxic effects. These findings underscore its potential utility as a topical therapeutic agent in ocular applications. Moreover, the general class of ROCK inhibitors has been clinically exploited—ripasudil, for example, has been approved for the treatment of glaucoma in Japan, and intravitreal fasudil has been explored for refractory diabetic macular oedema—establishing a safe precedent for ocular delivery of ROCK-targeting molecules. However, while ROCK inhibitors have been investigated in retinal and choroidal vascular pathologies and in conditions marked by barrier dysfunction (such as diabetic macular oedema), there is currently no direct record of H-1152 having been used specifically for dry age-related macular degeneration (dry AMD) or for RPE-specific pathologies in published clinical or preclinical studies (Mateos-Olivares et al., 2021).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, H-1152 acts by competitively inhibiting the kinase activity of both ROCK1 and ROCK2. By binding to the ATP-binding site of the kinase domain, H-1152 prevents the phosphorylation of downstream substrates such as myosin light chain (MLC) and LIM kinases, which in turn results in the reduced phosphorylation of cofilin. This cascade of events inhibits actomyosin contractility and results in the disassembly—or at least, a significant reduction—of actin stress fibers. In cellular contexts, this biochemical action translates into the disruption of stress-fiber–driven cellular contraction and a modulation of both cell–matrix and cell–cell adhesion complexes. In ocular applications, particularly within the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), such cytoskeletal reorganization is hypothesized to stabilize junctional complexes by normalizing the localization and organization of critical tight-junction proteins, such as ZO-1 and occludin. Studies in related ocular cell types (e.g., corneal endothelial cells) have documented that ROCK inhibitors induce a rapid collapse of actin stress fibers and result in a transient loss but subsequent restoration of tight-junction architecture over time. Furthermore, ROCK signaling is implicated in mediating the cellular responses to oxidative stress and in promoting VEGF-induced permeability. In models of VEGF stimulation, ROCK inhibition has been associated with a reduction in paracellular leak by preserving tight-junction integrity. Therefore, by potently inhibiting ROCK1/2, H-1152 is proposed to prevent the stress-fiber–mediated disruption of cell–cell junctions and to restore transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) in RPE cells subjected to chronic oxidative insult – a mechanism particularly relevant to dry AMD pathophysiology (Meekins et al., 2016; Yamaguchi et al., 2017).

Expected Effect:  
Based on its mechanism of action and experimental precedent in other ocular models, it is expected that H-1152 will exert a protective effect on RPE cells under conditions of chronic oxidative stress as seen in dry AMD. In vitro, in an ARPE-19 or primary RPE cell model, chronic exposure to low levels of H₂O₂ typically results in a decrease in TEER, disorganization of tight junction proteins (including ZO-1 and occludin), and the formation of abnormal F-actin stress fibers. Treatment with H-1152 in the range of 0.1–1 µM is hypothesized to partially or even substantially reverse these deleterious changes. Specifically, by inhibiting ROCK activity, H-1152 is expected to reduce the formation of stress fibers, thereby reversing the disruption of cell junctions and restoring a more normal distribution of ZO-1 and occludin at the plasma membrane. This should in turn lead to an improvement in TEER – with potential recovery to up to 60% of baseline levels in injured cells – as well as diminish the regulation of VEGF-driven paracellular leak. In addition, pharmacokinetic studies in rabbit models have demonstrated that subconjunctival dosing can achieve ocular RPE/choroid concentrations on the order of ~0.2 µM, which falls within the effective in vitro concentration range. Thus, the expected translational effect is that topical or subconjunctival administration of H-1152 will normalize RPE barrier function, mitigating one of the critical pathological hallmarks of dry AMD, which is the breakdown of the outer blood-retinal barrier that underlies RPE dysfunction and subsequent photoreceptor loss (Mateos-Olivares et al., 2021; Meekins et al., 2016; Yamaguchi et al., 2017).

Overall Evaluation:  
In summary, the therapeutic candidate H-1152 Dihydrochloride exhibits multiple promising attributes as a repurposed drug for the treatment of dry age-related macular degeneration. One of its primary strengths is its exceptional potency and selectivity toward ROCK1 and ROCK2, with IC₅₀ values in the low nanomolar range, which is critical given that only minimal inhibition may be required to elicit beneficial cellular modifications in the RPE. Its mechanism of action, relying on the inhibition of the ROCK pathway, directly addresses key pathological processes in dry AMD, notably the stress-fiber–mediated disruption of cell–cell junctions and the abnormal VEGF-driven paracellular leak. Furthermore, successful preclinical studies in corneal endothelial models have demonstrated that H-1152 can modulate cytoskeletal organization and promote wound healing, providing a proof-of-concept for its ability to restore barrier function in ocular tissues. The fact that ocular delivery via topical or subconjunctival routes can achieve therapeutic levels in the RPE/choroid is another advantage that underscores its translational potential, especially when compared with other ROCK inhibitors that have advanced into clinical trials for related ocular conditions such as glaucoma and diabetic macular oedema (Iancu et al., 2021; Meekins et al., 2016; Mateos-Olivares et al., 2021).

Nevertheless, there are several important weaknesses and uncertainties that must be addressed. First, the bulk of available preclinical evidence for H-1152 is derived from studies involving corneal endothelial cells rather than RPE cells, and therefore it remains uncertain whether the effects observed in these models will fully translate to the RPE context. The RPE possesses unique structural and functional characteristics that distinguish it from corneal endothelium, and additional targeted experiments in ARPE-19 or primary RPE cell models under conditions of chronic oxidative stress are required to confirm the hypothesized restoration of TEER and normalization of tight-junction proteins. Second, while the ROCK inhibitor class has been used in other areas of ophthalmology with an acceptable safety and tolerability profile, there is currently no direct clinical history of H-1152 being used for dry AMD or even in posterior-segment diseases; most clinical data so far pertains to ROCK inhibitors used in glaucoma or diabetic macular oedema. This means that the long-term safety, potential off-target effects, and optimal dosing regimens for H-1152 in the context of dry AMD remain to be established (Iancu et al., 2021; Mateos-Olivares et al., 2021).

Furthermore, some studies in retinal angiogenesis models have reported that certain ROCK inhibitors can produce paradoxical effects, such as increased VEGF-induced angiogenesis via alternative signaling pathways including ERK1/2 activation. Although the candidate hypothesis emphasizes barrier restoration and suppression of VEGF-driven leak, it will be critical to delineate the dose–response relationship and evaluate any potential pro-angiogenic or off-target actions of H-1152 in comprehensive preclinical studies. Establishing robust pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles is essential; while rabbit subconjunctival dosing reportedly achieved ocular RPE/choroid levels around 0.2 µM, it needs to be confirmed that such concentrations are both efficacious and safe in the long term, particularly as the dry AMD population may require chronic dosing (Mateos-Olivares et al., 2021; Yamaguchi et al., 2017).

Overall, H-1152 Dihydrochloride presents as a promising candidate for repurposing as a dry AMD therapeutic based on its mechanistic rationale, outstanding potency and selectivity for ROCK inhibition, and the supportive data from ocular surface models. The proposed molecular mechanism—restoring tight-junction integrity through suppression of stress fiber formation and subsequent normalization of ZO-1 and occludin localization—aligns well with the underlying pathology of dry AMD, in which the breakdown of RPE barrier function contributes to disease progression. However, definitive preclinical studies employing RPE-specific models and in vivo evaluations in relevant dry AMD animal models are necessary to solidify its translational potential. Additionally, careful attention must be given to its safety profile, especially given the delicate nature of the posterior segment and the potential for off-target effects on retinal vasculature. In conclusion, while H-1152 shows significant promise based on its pharmacological profile and the mechanistic logic underlying RPE barrier restoration, further research is warranted to bridge the gap between ocular surface models and the RPE-specific pathology encountered in dry AMD. Strategic preclinical investigations, including detailed assessments of TEER restoration, tight junction protein localization under oxidative stress, and the modulation of VEGF signaling, are critical next steps in validating H-1152 as a viable therapeutic candidate for this complex and currently underserved retinal disease (Meekins et al., 2016; Yamaguchi et al., 2017).
