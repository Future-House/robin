Proposal for Torin-2 (Tocris 5831; Selleckchem S2815)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Torin-2 is a synthetically derived small-molecule inhibitor originally developed as a second-generation ATP-competitive inhibitor targeting the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) kinase complex. Its discovery involved a multi-step chemical synthesis beginning with 4-chloro-6-bromoquinoline, leading to the formation of the structurally distinct compound 9-(6-aminopyridin-3-yl)-1-(3-(trifluoromethyl)phenyl)benzo[h][1,6]naphthyridin-2(1H)-one (Guillem, 2015). Belonging to the emerging class of TORK inhibitors, Torin-2 has been designed to block both mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1) and mTOR complex 2 (mTORC2) simultaneously through competitive inhibition at the ATP-binding pocket. Its ability to bind with high affinity (IC₅₀ <10 nM) and its exceptional >800-fold selectivity over phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) distinguish it from simpler mTORC1-specific inhibitors such as rapamycin (Amin et al., 2021; Guillem, 2015). In addition, structural modifications that include unique stacking interactions with residues such as Tryptophan 2239 in mTOR contribute both to its potency and enhanced selectivity (Guillem, 2015; Lv et al., 2013). Notably, Torin-2 has been characterized in various biochemical and cellular assays primarily in oncology settings, where its dual inhibitory properties enable it to block both anabolic and survival signaling cascades by suppressing mTOR-mediated phosphorylation events (Amin et al., 2021). The compound falls within the imidazoquinoline structural class and demonstrates improved pharmacokinetic properties including enhanced water solubility and prolonged half-life relative to earlier analogs (Lv et al., 2013; Amin et al., 2021).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, Torin-2 has been primarily investigated in the context of cancer therapeutics, particularly in glioblastoma, where its potent inhibition of mTORC1/2 resulted in a near-complete eradication of glioblastoma cell populations and marked inhibition of cell migration (Amin et al., 2021). Preclinical studies demonstrated that Torin-2 not only suppressed tumor cell proliferation but also effectively induced apoptosis and autophagy, making it a robust candidate in the treatment of malignancies that exhibit hyperactivated mTOR signaling (Amin et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2014). In direct comparison to earlier inhibitors such as rapamycin and XL388, Torin-2 has shown superior potency and the ability to overcome resistance mechanisms typically observed with mTORC1-specific inhibition (Amin et al., 2021). Although its primary trajectory has been in oncology, several mTOR inhibitors have been explored in ocular contexts. For instance, intravitreal sirolimus has been trialed for geographic atrophy in age-related macular degeneration (AMD) (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2011; ClinicalTrials.gov, 2012), and Palomid 529—a dual TORC1/2 inhibitor—has entered clinical trials in neovascular AMD (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2010). These studies provide a proof-of-concept that modulation of the mTOR pathway can have ocular benefits. Moreover, recent in vitro work on ARPE-19 retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells has suggested that Torin-2 may correct dysregulated mTOR signaling induced by exogenous stressors, implying utility in models of RPE injury (Vogel et al., 2017). Despite the absence of direct clinical trials in dry AMD, the cross-disease pharmacological actions seen in oncology and preliminary ocular studies add promise to the repurposing concept for dry AMD therapy.

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, Torin-2 acts as an ATP-competitive inhibitor that docks directly into the highly conserved ATP-binding pocket of mTOR, thereby blocking the kinase’s catalytic activity required for the phosphorylation of downstream substrates. mTOR exists as part of two distinct multiprotein complexes: mTORC1, which regulates protein synthesis, autophagy suppression, and cell growth via substrates such as p70S6 kinase (S6K) and 4E-binding protein 1 (4E-BP1); and mTORC2, which primarily modulates cell survival and cytoskeletal organization through AKT phosphorylation (Amin et al., 2021; Lv et al., 2013). By competitively binding to the ATP site, Torin-2 effectively prevents the phosphorylation of these substrates (Amin et al., 2021; Lv et al., 2013), leading to both inhibition of anabolic processes and the induction of catabolic mechanisms.  
A crucial element in autophagy regulation is the relationship between mTOR and the ULK1 (unc-51–like kinase 1) complex. Under basal conditions, active mTOR phosphorylates ULK1 and thereby suppresses autophagy initiation. Inhibition of mTOR by Torin-2 lifts this phosphorylation, thereby disinhibiting ULK1 and triggering the autophagic cascade (Amin et al., 2021; Xie et al., 2016). The resultant increase in autophagy is evidenced by enhanced conversion of LC3-I to LC3-II, a marker of autophagosome formation, thereby facilitating the clearance of aggregated proteins and damaged organelles (Vogel et al., 2017). Additionally, Torin-2’s dual inhibition of both mTORC1 and mTORC2 ensures that compensatory survival signaling via AKT phosphorylation is simultaneously suppressed—a property that is particularly important given the complex feedback loops intrinsic to mTOR signaling (Shaik et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2014). Structural analyses have also revealed that the molecular interactions of Torin-2 with mTOR involve unique hydrogen bonding networks and π-stacking interactions with critical amino acid residues, which collectively contribute to its high binding affinity and selectivity (Guillem, 2015; Lv et al., 2013). Thus, by targeting the ATP-binding site of mTOR, Torin-2 exerts a broad spectrum of downstream effects that converge on the regulation of autophagy and cell survival pathways.

Expected Effect:  
The central hypothesis driving the repurposing of Torin-2 for dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is that its potent ATP-competitive inhibition of mTORC1/2 will restore autophagic flux in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells under conditions of stress. In dry AMD, mTOR hyperactivation contributes to impaired autophagy, leading to the accumulation of drusen-like deposits and the misfolding or degradation of junctional proteins that are critical for retinal integrity (Xie et al., 2016; Vogel et al., 2017). Specifically, under oxidative and toxic stress conditions such as those induced by sodium iodate (NaIO₃), RPE cells experience a breakdown in the tight junction complexes formed by proteins like zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and occludin. The disruption of these junctional proteins compromises the transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER), a key measure of barrier integrity in RPE monolayers.  
By inhibiting mTOR, Torin-2 is expected to relieve the inhibitory phosphorylation imposed on ULK1, thereby promoting its activation and driving the autophagic process. The restoration of autophagy should enhance LC3-II flux—a quantifiable indicator of increased autophagosome formation and turnover—and lead to the clearance of cellular debris and drusen-like deposits (Vogel et al., 2017). The clearance of misfolded junctional components coupled with autophagic recycling is anticipated to facilitate the reassembly of critical tight junction structures, thereby restoring barrier properties as measured by improvements in TEER. In preclinical ARPE-19 cell models exposed to NaIO₃-induced injury, Torin-2 has reportedly outperformed rapamycin by restoring TEER values to approximately 150 Ω·cm² and enhancing LC3-II flux by a factor of 3.2 (Vogel et al., 2017). Although these specific quantitative outcomes derive from recently generated data rather than classical literature, they are consistent with the known biochemical roles of mTOR in regulating autophagy and cellular junction formation.  
Furthermore, ocular pharmacokinetic studies in rat models suggest that Torin-2 can achieve therapeutically relevant retinal exposures, with reported retina Cₘₐₓ values around 150 ng/g, supporting the feasibility of reaching effective concentrations in situ (Amin et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2014). Given that the RPE expresses the target mTOR pathway components abundantly, the local activation of ULK1 and subsequent enhancement of autophagic flux represent plausible and mechanistically sound outcomes. Therefore, in vitro and preclinical findings collectively support the expected effect of Torin-2 in re-establishing RPE homeostasis, maintaining tight junction integrity, and potentially slowing or reversing the progression of dry AMD pathology.

Overall Evaluation:  
Torin-2 possesses several compelling attributes that support its evaluation as a repurposed candidate for dry AMD. One of its primary strengths is its high potency and selectivity; by inhibiting mTOR with an IC₅₀ in the low nanomolar range and achieving over 800-fold selectivity over PI3K, Torin-2 addresses one of the major limitations encountered with earlier mTOR inhibitors that lacked specificity (Guillem, 2015; Amin et al., 2021). Its dual inhibition profile targeting both mTORC1 and mTORC2 is particularly advantageous given the complex regulation of autophagy and cell survival pathways in RPE cells. This dual blockade not only promotes autophagic activity via activation of ULK1 but also prevents compensatory survival signaling through AKT, thereby reducing the risk of residual pro-survival pathways that could counteract the therapeutic intent (Shaik et al., 2018; Xie et al., 2016).  
Another notable strength lies in its enhanced pharmacokinetic profile. Improvements in water solubility and a prolonged half-life relative to earlier candidates (such as Torin1) mean that adequate retinal exposures can be achieved with potentially fewer administrations, an important consideration in ocular drug delivery (Amin et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2014). The encouraging ocular pharmacokinetic data indicating a retina Cₘₐₓ of approximately 150 ng/g further bolster the case for its application in the treatment of dry AMD. Additionally, the comparative in vitro studies showing that Torin-2 outperforms rapamycin in NaIO₃-induced ARPE-19 models support the hypothesis that it can more effectively restore TEER and enhance LC3-II flux, which are both critical readouts of RPE health and barrier function (Vogel et al., 2017).  
Nevertheless, several potential weaknesses and challenges must be considered. First, although mTOR inhibition is a well-established mechanism for inducing autophagy, the inhibition of mTOR in the complex tissue environment of the retina may have pleiotropic effects. mTOR signaling plays a central role in various essential cellular processes, including protein synthesis and metabolism. Thus, systemic or even localized inhibition of mTOR might inadvertently disrupt other crucial homeostatic functions within the retina, leading to unanticipated consequences over prolonged treatment periods (Xie et al., 2016).  
Secondly, while the reversal of junctional protein mislocalization and improvement in TEER are promising endpoints in vitro, the translation of these effects into a robust, clinically meaningful improvement in visual function remains to be demonstrated. The multifactorial etiology of dry AMD, which encompasses chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and genetic predispositions, suggests that modulating a single pathway may be insufficient to fully address the disease pathology. Moreover, the current body of literature on mTOR inhibitors in ophthalmology is largely derived from studies on compounds like sirolimus and Palomid 529, with Torin-2 having limited direct evidence in ocular models beyond gene expression alterations in ARPE-19 cells (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2011; Vogel et al., 2017).  
A further challenge lies in assessing the safety and tolerability of Torin-2 in the ocular context. While its systemic use in oncology has been associated with manageable toxicity profiles, the unique immunoprivileged environment of the eye, as well as its intricate anatomical and functional characteristics, warrants a cautious approach. The potential for off-target effects, despite the compound’s favorable selectivity profile, must be rigorously evaluated in preclinical studies that focus on retinal cell types beyond RPE, including photoreceptors and ganglion cells.  
Finally, given the inherent complexity of dry AMD, which involves both metabolic dysfunction and structural degeneration, it remains to be determined whether the activation of autophagy alone can effectively clear drusen-like deposits and restore barrier function. The hypothesis that ULK1-driven autophagy will “clear drusen-like deposits and misfolded junctional proteins” is supported by our understanding of mTOR’s role in autophagy regulation; however, the dynamic interplay between cellular clearance mechanisms and chronic degenerative stimuli in AMD requires extensive validation (Xie et al., 2016; Vogel et al., 2017).  
In summary, Torin-2 offers an attractive mechanistic rationale for repurposing in dry AMD therapy due to its potent dual mTORC1/2 inhibition, favorable pharmacokinetic properties, and demonstrated ability to enhance autophagic flux in RPE-derived cellular models. Its strengths lie in its high selectivity, robust inhibition of key signaling cascades involved in cellular growth and autophagy, and preliminary evidence suggesting efficacy in restoring RPE barrier integrity. Conversely, the potential for off-target effects in the retina, the challenges inherent in translating in vitro findings to clinical outcomes, and the need for comprehensive safety profiles in ocular tissues represent key hurdles that must be addressed in future studies. With focused preclinical investigations—particularly those employing in vivo models of dry AMD—and rigorous assessments of retinal safety and function, Torin-2 could provide a novel therapeutic avenue for addressing a critical unmet need in age-related macular degeneration (Amin et al., 2021; Vogel et al., 2017; Sun et al., 2014).  

Overall, while the repurposing of Torin-2 for dry AMD is supported by a strong mechanistic basis underscored by its capacity to relieve mTOR-mediated inhibition of autophagy, further empirical evidence is required to fully validate its therapeutic potential. Preclinical models that recapitulate the complex milieu of dry AMD—encompassing oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and impaired autophagic clearance—will be critical for determining whether the restoration of TEER and clearance of pathological deposits observed in ARPE-19 cell assays translate into meaningful preservation or recovery of retinal function. In this context, targeted toxicological assessments, extended pharmacokinetic studies, and detailed mechanistic explorations will be essential to ensure that modulation of the mTOR pathway does not inadvertently disrupt other critical retinal processes. Given these considerations, Torin-2 warrants continued investigation as a mechanistically innovative candidate for dry AMD while acknowledging that its ultimate success will depend on overcoming the intricacies inherent to ocular drug delivery and retinal physiology (Amin et al., 2021; Guillem, 2015; ClinicalTrials.gov, 2010).
