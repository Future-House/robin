Proposal for Saroglitazar (Cayman 19606)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Saroglitazar is a novel, fully synthetic small‐molecule compound that functions as a dual agonist of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors, specifically targeting both the α and γ isoforms, with a pharmacological profile that heavily favors PPAR-α activation. Initially synthesized by Zydus Cadila (also referred to as Cadila Healthcare Limited) in Ahmedabad, India, saroglitazar was designed as a new chemical entity (NCE) – a groundbreaking achievement as it is regarded as India’s first NCE to reach the market (Sharma et al., 2015; Sosale et al., 2015). Chemically, it is defined as (S)-α-ethoxy-4-{2-[2-methyl-5-(4-methylthio)phenyl]-1H-pyrrol-1-yl]ethoxy}-benzenepropanoic acid magnesium salt, a structure that confers the necessary lipophilic and acidic characteristics common among PPAR agonists (Jain et al., 2015; Sharma et al., 2015). Being a member of the glitazar class, this drug group historically aimed to combine the lipid-lowering benefits of fibrates (PPAR-α agonists) with the insulin-sensitizing and anti-inflammatory effects of thiazolidinediones (PPAR-γ agonists), yet previous attempts with similar dual compounds were marred by safety concerns, including adverse cardiovascular and renal outcomes (Chatterjee et al., 2015; Derosa et al., 2018). Saroglitazar distinguishes itself by presenting a predominantly PPAR-α profile with moderate PPAR-γ activity, a fine-tuning that aims to harness the metabolic benefits while mitigating the classical side effects associated with pure PPAR-γ agonism, such as weight gain and edema (Jain et al., 2015; Chhabra et al., 2022; Sosale et al., 2015). Its synthetic origin, coupled with a well-characterized chemical structure that includes key functional groups (in particular, an α-ethoxy carboxylic acid moiety that forms hydrogen bonds with amino acid residues in the receptor binding domain), has enabled a robust mechanistic foundation from which to understand its systemic pharmacology (Joshi, 2015; Sharma et al., 2015).

Therapeutic History:  
Clinically, saroglitazar has been approved by India’s Drug Controller General (DCGI) for the treatment of diabetic dyslipidemia and hypertriglyceridemia in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) who are not adequately managed by statin therapy alone (Sosale et al., 2015; Lange et al., 2022). Extensive preclinical studies have demonstrated its lipid-lowering, insulin-sensitizing, and anti-inflammatory effects in multiple animal models, ranging from diabetic rodents (db/db mice, Zucker fa/fa rats) to nonhuman primates; these studies laid the groundwork for phase I human trials, the results of which indicated favorable pharmacokinetics and safety margins (Jain et al., 2015; Joshi, 2015; Jani et al., 2013). Subsequent phase III clinical trials—among them the PRESS V and PRESS VI studies—confirmed its efficacy in reducing serum triglyceride levels by approximately 45–47% and improving other dyslipidemic parameters in the target patient population (Joshi, 2015; Honda et al., 2021). Beyond its use in diabetic dyslipidemia, recent research efforts have extended its potential application into metabolic liver diseases such as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) (Honda et al., 2021; Vuppalanchi et al., 2022). Despite the substantial body of literature supporting its use in metabolic disorders, none of the documented clinical or preclinical studies to date have directly tested saroglitazar in ocular conditions such as dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) or similar retinal pathologies (Tan et al., 2017; Kaul et al., 2019). However, the robust clinical validation of saroglitazar’s dual PPAR-α/γ effects in liver disease and diabetic dyslipidemia provides an important translational basis for its repurposing, especially given that both chronic inflammation and disordered lipid metabolism have been recognized as central drivers in the progression of dry AMD (Sharma et al., 2015; Sosale et al., 2015).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, saroglitazar acts as a ligand for the nuclear receptor superfamily member PPAR. Once bound, the compound facilitates the heterodimerization of the activated receptor with the retinoid X receptor (RXR), and this heterodimer subsequently binds to specific peroxisome proliferator response elements (PPREs) in the promoter regions of target genes, thus initiating transcriptional regulation (Chudiwal & Mujeeb, n.d.; Joshi, 2015). The activation of PPAR-α by saroglitazar leads to an upregulation of genes involved in hepatic fatty acid oxidation pathways, including enzymes responsible for β-oxidation, which results in decreased triglyceride synthesis and secretion while enhancing the clearance of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (Patel et al., 2018a; Patel et al., 2018b). This mechanism is especially relevant for reducing systemic lipid levels—a property exploited in the treatment of diabetic dyslipidemia. Concurrently, the PPAR-γ component, though moderate in its activation by saroglitazar, plays an important role in modulating inflammatory responses and improving insulin sensitivity. Activation of PPAR-γ results in the repression of pro-inflammatory cytokine genes (including IL-6 and IL-8) and the promotion of genes associated with adipocyte differentiation and lipid storage, while also inhibiting the expression of inflammatory mediators in immune cells (Gawrieh et al., 2021). Structural biology studies indicate that the α-ethoxy carboxylic acid portion of saroglitazar forms hydrogen bonds with key amino acid residues within the ligand-binding domains of both PPAR-α and PPAR-γ, thereby stabilizing the receptor conformation for optimal transcriptional activity (Sharma et al., 2015; Joshi, 2015; Honda et al., 2021). This dual receptor engagement enables a coordinated response where lipid metabolism is corrected via PPAR-α activation while a parallel anti-inflammatory effect is mediated through PPAR-γ, ultimately leading to improved metabolic homeostasis (Honda et al., 2021; Tan et al., 2017).

In the context of dry AMD, the chronic low-grade inflammation observed within the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) along with the abnormal accumulation of lipids (drusen formation) are thought to contribute significantly to disease progression. PPAR-γ has been implicated in the modulation of inflammatory responses in various tissues, including ocular tissues, and its activation can result in the suppression of inflammatory cytokines and a reduction in oxidative stress (Kaul et al., 2019; Honda et al., 2021). In parallel, PPAR-α activation is associated with increased fatty acid oxidation and improved lipid clearance, a function that could potentially counteract the deposition of lipid-rich drusen in the outer retina (Patel et al., 2018a; Patel et al., 2018b). Thus, saroglitazar’s dual activation of these receptors offers a mechanistic rationale for addressing both inflammation and dysregulated lipid metabolism in the RPE. Given that barrier integrity in the RPE, as measured by transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER), is closely linked to the expression and organization of tight junction proteins such as zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and occludin, the anti-inflammatory actions of PPAR-γ may be critical in preserving these junctional complexes under inflammatory stress, as evidenced by experimental models where TNF-α exposure leads to barrier compromise (Chudiwal & Mujeeb, n.d.; Chhabra et al., 2022). The structural and molecular interactions described in the literature provide a detailed understanding of how saroglitazar can modulate these pathways at the transcriptional level, potentially offering both immediate and long-term benefits in maintaining RPE barrier function (Joshi, 2015; Sosale et al., 2015).

Expected Effect:  
The central hypothesis under investigation is that dual PPAR-α/γ activation by saroglitazar will exert a synergistic effect on RPE cells under conditions of inflammatory stress—a condition that mirrors the pathophysiology of dry AMD. In an in vitro model employing the ARPE-19 cell line (a well-established human RPE cell model), exposure to the pro-inflammatory cytokine tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) is known to compromise the integrity of the RPE monolayer by reducing TEER and disrupting the organization of critical tight junction proteins, specifically ZO-1 and occludin (Tan et al., 2017; Sosale et al., 2015). Preliminary data provided by the research team indicate that treatment with 1 µM saroglitazar is capable of restoring TEER by approximately 55% and significantly reducing the release of the inflammatory cytokines interleukin-6 (IL-6) and interleukin-8 (IL-8) in TNF-α-challenged ARPE-19 monolayers. This robust effect is postulated to be mediated via the activation of PPAR-γ, which leads to the suppression of inflammatory mediator expression, while the concurrent activation of PPAR-α enhances lipid catabolism and clearance. Such dual action is particularly relevant, given that chronic inflammation and impaired lipid metabolism are two principal drivers in the pathogenesis of dry AMD (Sosale et al., 2015; Chudiwal & Mujeeb, n.d.).

Moreover, the molecular basis for this anticipated benefit is supported by prior evidence in non-ocular models. For instance, in hepatic and adipose tissues, saroglitazar has been shown to enhance the expression of genes involved in fatty acid oxidation and to downregulate inflammatory pathways—effects that translate into improved metabolic profiles and reduced systemic inflammation (Gawrieh et al., 2021; Derosa et al., 2018). Although these results originate from studies primarily focused on metabolic syndrome and diabetic dyslipidemia, there is a strong mechanistic rationale that similar pathways may be operative in the RPE. It is known that PPARs are expressed in ocular tissues, including the RPE, and that their activation can modulate local inflammatory responses and cellular lipid metabolism (Gawrieh et al., 2021; Sosale et al., 2015). Thus, in the proposed experimental context, saroglitazar is expected to restore RPE monolayer integrity by preserving the ZO-1 and occludin architecture and recovering TEER—in turn, this could prevent the leakage of toxic substances and inflammatory mediators that exacerbate RPE dysfunction in dry AMD.

In addition to its effects on the RPE barrier, the anti-inflammatory properties conferred by PPAR-γ activation may also counteract the paracrine signaling that contributes to chronic inflammation in the retina; reduced secretion of IL-6 and IL-8 will likely diminish local immune cell recruitment and activation, thereby alleviating the inflammatory milieu that predisposes to cell damage and drusen formation (Chudiwal & Mujeeb, n.d.; Sosale et al., 2015). The enhanced lipid clearance mediated by PPAR-α activation may further reduce intracellular lipid accumulation, a factor closely linked to oxidative stress and subsequent RPE cell apoptosis. Consequently, the combined effects of saroglitazar could contribute to the stabilization—and potentially the restoration—of retinal homeostasis in the setting of dry AMD (Patel et al., 2018a; Patel et al., 2018b).

Overall Evaluation:  
Taken together, saroglitazar emerges as a promising repurposing candidate for the treatment of dry age-related macular degeneration based on its dual pharmacological actions. Its primary strengths lie in its well-defined mechanism of action that combines robust lipid-lowering effects with potent anti-inflammatory properties. The predominant activation of PPAR-α is particularly advantageous in enhancing fatty acid oxidation and reducing lipid deposition, which may help in curbing the formation of drusen—a hallmark of dry AMD—while the moderate activation of PPAR-γ minimizes the risk of adverse effects such as weight gain and edema that have historically accompanied other PPAR-γ-targeted therapies (Sharma et al., 2015; Sosale et al., 2015; Tan et al., 2017). The fact that saroglitazar has already undergone extensive clinical validation in metabolic disorders provides a strong translational foundation for its potential application in ocular diseases. Its established safety profile, favourable pharmacokinetic characteristics, and therapeutic efficacy in non-ocular settings imply that, with appropriate formulation adjustments to ensure adequate retinal bioavailability, saroglitazar could be repurposed with relatively low developmental risk.

However, the candidate also has notable weaknesses. Currently, there is no direct evidence from preclinical ocular models or clinical trials that explicitly investigate the efficacy of saroglitazar in dry AMD or related retinal conditions. The pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic parameters optimized for systemic metabolic control may not automatically translate to effective intraocular drug levels, and the unique immunological and biochemical environment of the retina could pose challenges that are not encountered in hepatic or adipose tissue (Chatterjee et al., 2015). Thus, there exists an imperative need for thorough preclinical testing in relevant ocular models to establish dosing regimens, evaluate retinal safety, and confirm that the molecular targets of saroglitazar are effectively engaged in the RPE.

Another potential challenge lies in drug delivery. Given that saroglitazar was developed for oral administration with systemic effects in mind, repurposing for dry AMD may require the development of novel delivery approaches. This could include exploring topical eye drop formulations, intravitreal injections, or even sustained-release implants to ensure that therapeutic concentrations are achieved in the retina while minimizing systemic side effects. The pharmacological properties documented in the metabolic studies (such as rapid absorption and predominant hepatobiliary clearance) must be re-evaluated in the context of ocular tissue penetration and retention (Patel et al., 2018b; Vuppalanchi et al., 2022).

On the strength side, the dual action on inflammation and lipid metabolism is uniquely relevant for dry AMD, where both chronic, low-grade inflammation and aberrant lipid accumulation play critical roles in disease progression. In vitro data showing that 1 µM saroglitazar can restore TEER by 55% in TNF-α–compromised ARPE-19 monolayers and decrease pro-inflammatory cytokine release (IL-6, IL-8) provide promising initial proof-of-concept evidence that warrants further investigation (Sosale et al., 2015; Chudiwal & Mujeeb, n.d.). These findings suggest that saroglitazar may not only halt the progression of RPE barrier dysfunction but might also reverse some of the deleterious cellular processes induced by chronic inflammatory cytokine exposure. Such dual benefits, if reproducible in more advanced ocular models, would represent a significant therapeutic advance in managing dry AMD—a condition for which current treatment options remain limited.

In summary, saroglitazar’s established safety and efficacy in metabolic diseases, together with its molecular capacity to modulate both inflammatory cascades and lipid metabolism, render it an attractive candidate for repurposing in dry AMD. The proposed mechanism—that dual PPAR-α/γ activation can restore RPE barrier integrity by preserving tight junction proteins (ZO-1, occludin) and enhancing transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) while simultaneously reducing inflammatory cytokine output—addresses two key pathogenic drivers implicated in the progression of dry AMD. Importantly, the translational feasibility of repurposing a drug that has already been clinically validated in humans accelerates the potential developmental timeline, provided that ocular pharmacokinetics and local target engagement are confirmed in specialized preclinical studies.

It is recommended that further studies be designed to (1) characterize the expression and functionality of PPAR-α and PPAR-γ receptors in primary human RPE cells and in animal models of dry AMD; (2) adapt the formulation of saroglitazar to optimize intraocular delivery and retention; (3) conduct detailed mechanistic studies that elucidate the molecular pathways through which saroglitazar modulates tight junction integrity and inflammatory signaling in the RPE; and (4) perform in vivo proof-of-concept studies using established animal models of dry AMD to evaluate both efficacy and safety in the ocular context (Sosale et al., 2015; Tan et al., 2017; Gawrieh et al., 2021). Addressing these points would greatly strengthen the rationale for clinical trials investigating saroglitazar as a novel therapeutic option for dry AMD.

Overall Evaluation:  
Strengths of saroglitazar as a repurposed candidate for dry AMD include its thoroughly characterized dual agonist activity, which confers both an enhanced lipid-clearing effect via PPAR-α activation and a potent anti-inflammatory effect via PPAR-γ activation. The compound’s safety profile and clinical validation in metabolic disorders, such as diabetic dyslipidemia and NAFLD, provide a robust foundation for potential repurposing, leveraging existing pharmacokinetic and toxicological data (Sharma et al., 2015; Sosale et al., 2015; Tan et al., 2017). Additionally, the preliminary in vitro evidence indicating improvements in RPE barrier function—with measurable restoration of TEER and reduction in key inflammatory markers like IL-6 and IL-8—are highly encouraging, as they suggest that saroglitazar may help halt or even reverse the RPE dysfunction that is central to dry AMD pathology (Chudiwal & Mujeeb, n.d.; Sosale et al., 2015).

Nonetheless, the weaknesses of this approach cannot be overlooked. The current literature does not offer any direct evidence of saroglitazar’s efficacy or safety in ocular applications, nor is there any previous usage of this compound (or closely related glitazars) specifically in dry AMD. The unique microenvironment of the retina, characterized by its blood-retina barrier and distinct immunologic landscape, may influence both drug delivery and receptor responsiveness in ways that differ substantially from systemic tissues such as the liver or adipose tissue (Chatterjee et al., 2015). Moreover, given its original development for oral administration targeting systemic metabolic conditions, modifications in the drug’s formulation may be necessary to achieve effective concentrations within the retinal tissues without provoking off-target systemic effects. These uncertainties emphasize the critical need for comprehensive preclinical studies focused on ocular pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, and efficacy in animal models that recapitulate dry AMD pathology (Patel et al., 2018b; Vuppalanchi et al., 2022).

In conclusion, saroglitazar presents a scientifically compelling and mechanistically plausible candidate for repurposing in the treatment of dry age-related macular degeneration. Its dual PPAR-α/γ agonism offers an integrated approach to addressing both fundamental pathogenic mechanisms of dry AMD—namely, chronic inflammation and impaired lipid clearance. While the preexisting clinical validation in metabolic diseases confers significant translational advantages, targeted ocular studies remain essential to confirm that the drug’s pharmacological actions can be effectively harnessed in the retina. Should the hurdles of ocular formulation and local target engagement be overcome, saroglitazar could offer a novel means to preserve RPE barrier integrity, reduce inflammatory damage, and ultimately slow or reverse the progression of dry AMD. Given these considerations, the proposed repurposing of saroglitazar merits further investigation through detailed preclinical ocular studies and, pending positive results, subsequent clinical trials tailored for patients with dry AMD (Sosale et al., 2015; Tan et al., 2017; Gawrieh et al., 2021).

Overall, saroglitazar’s repurposing for dry AMD is supported by a strong mechanistic rationale and promising preliminary in vitro data; however, significant challenges remain regarding its intraocular delivery, pharmacokinetic optimization, and direct demonstration of efficacy in ocular models. A balanced research strategy that addresses these issues through systematic preclinical evaluation and formulation development is warranted to fully elucidate the therapeutic potential of saroglitazar in managing dry AMD.
