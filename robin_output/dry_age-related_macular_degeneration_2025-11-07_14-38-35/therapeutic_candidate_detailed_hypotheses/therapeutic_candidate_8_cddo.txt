Proposal for CDDO-Imidazolide (CDDO-Im; Cayman #13019)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
CDDO‐Imidazolide (CDDO‐Im) is a synthetic derivative that belongs to the family of chemically engineered pentacyclic triterpenoids. These compounds are derived from naturally occurring oleanolic acid, a steroid‐like molecule widely distributed in plants and traditionally used in Asian medicine. In the case of CDDO‐Im, researchers have modified the oleanolic acid scaffold by introducing a 2‐cyano substituent, oxidizing key positions to generate 3,12‐dioxo groups, and creating a conjugated 1,9(11)‐dien arrangement that ultimately yields a 28‐oic acid derivative; the imidazolide moiety is added as a C17 modification to generate the “imidazolide” derivative (Cano et al., 2010; Nakagami et al., 2015; Cheng et al., 2021). This compound is a member of the broader class of synthetic oleanane pentacyclic triterpenoids, which have been chemically optimized over the years to enhance their intrinsically weak anti‐inflammatory and cytoprotective activities. Notably, these derivatives are known for their extraordinarily potent activation of the transcription factor Nrf2, an effect that is achieved by covalently modifying electrophilic target sites on Keap1. In general, this drug class has been used in diverse pharmacological applications to harness antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory pathways, and several members have been tested preclinically and even progressed into early clinical trials, particularly in the fields of oncology and renal diseases (Cano et al., 2010; Cheng et al., 2021).

Therapeutic History:  
The history of therapeutics based on the CDDO scaffold, including CDDO‐Im, is rich in preclinical exploration and early clinical evaluation. Early studies demonstrated that synthetic triterpenoids, such as CDDO and its derivatives (including the methyl ester variant CDDO‐Me and the imidazolide derivative CDDO‐Im), are considerably more potent than other small‐molecule activators of Nrf2 such as sulforaphane or (R)-α-lipoic acid (Cano et al., 2010). Preclinical studies have shown that these compounds robustly induce phase II antioxidant enzymes––notably HO-1, NQO1, and glutathione-related enzymes––by targeting and modifying Keap1, thereby releasing Nrf2 to translocate to the nucleus and drive the transcription of cytoprotective genes. In ocular research, CDDO‐Im has been specifically chosen for in vivo studies because of its demonstrated efficacy in ameliorating retinal damage in mouse models of uveitis and cigarette smoke-induced retinal injury, with evidence of preservation of retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) ultrastructure (Cano et al., 2010). Other members of the CDDO family, such as CDDO‐Me (bardoxolone methyl), have undergone clinical testing in oncology, showing that such compounds can reach therapeutically relevant blood concentrations via intravenous infusion, although issues such as suboptimal oral bioavailability have been noted and are subject to ongoing formulation efforts (Cheng et al., 2021; Nakagami et al., 2015). While there is no direct record of CDDO‐Im being used in clinical trials for dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) or similar retinal degenerative diseases, the class’s demonstrated ability to activate protective Nrf2 signaling in ocular tissues and to reduce inflammation and oxidative damage provides a strong rationale for investigating its potential in the context of dry AMD (Cheng et al., 2021).

Mechanism of Action:  
The pharmacological actions of CDDO‐Im are primarily mediated through its covalent modification of Keap1 (Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1), the cytosolic repressor of the transcription factor Nrf2. Under basal conditions, Keap1 binds to Nrf2 and facilitates its ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. CDDO‐Im, as an electrophilic compound possessing a cyanoenone Michael‐acceptor motif, reacts with crucial sulfhydryl groups on Keap1, resulting in the formation of Michael adducts at key cysteine residues (Cano et al., 2010; Cheng et al., 2021). Although specific studies have not always mapped the precise cysteine residues modified by CDDO‐Im in every system, the general consensus from the literature is that the modification of cysteine sulfhydryl groups such as Cys151 is critical for diminishing the suppressive influence of Keap1. Once these cysteine residues are modified, Keap1 undergoes conformational changes that reduce its binding affinity for Nrf2. Consequently, Nrf2 is liberated and able to translocate into the nucleus where, by heterodimerizing with small Maf proteins, it binds to antioxidant response elements (ARE) in the promoters of target genes. This Nrf2-driven transcriptional activation results in the upregulation of a broad battery of phase II detoxifying and antioxidant enzymes, including HO-1 (heme oxygenase 1), NQO1 (NAD(P)H:quinone oxidoreductase-1), and glutamate cysteine ligase subunits such as GCLM (glutamate-cysteine ligase modifier subunit) (Cano et al., 2010; Cheng et al., 2021). In addition to these classical antioxidant enzymes, recent research suggests that Nrf2 activation may also have downstream effects on the expression of proteins involved in maintaining cellular barrier integrity, for example tight junction proteins like claudin-1 and occludin. Although experimental details regarding the upregulation of tight junction proteins by CDDO‐Im per se have not been exhaustively documented in the literature provided, there is supporting evidence from related triterpenoids such as CDDO‐Me which preserve blood–brain barrier integrity by upregulating tight junction proteins (Nakagami, 2016; Cano et al., 2010). This mechanistic insight is directly relevant for the RPE, as oxidative stress in these cells contributes to tight junction disorganization, loss of transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER), and increased paracellular permeability. Therefore, through the covalent modification of Keap1 and subsequent activation of Nrf2, CDDO‐Im is hypothesized to restore the balance of antioxidant defenses and tighten the RPE barrier by increasing the expression of protective enzymes and possibly enhancing tight junction assembly under conditions of H₂O₂-induced stress (Cheng et al., 2021; Cano et al., 2010).

Expected Effect:  
The proposed hypothesis for using CDDO‐Im in dry AMD centers on the restoration of RPE function by enhancing antioxidant defense and barrier integrity. In an in vitro model using ARPE-19 cells exposed to H₂O₂ to simulate oxidative stress, it is expected that treatment with CDDO‐Im at a concentration around 200 nM will robustly activate the Nrf2 pathway. This activation should lead to a marked upregulation of phase II antioxidant enzymes, such as HO-1 and NQO1, thereby mitigating oxidative damage within these cells. Moreover, because Nrf2 activation has been associated with the upregulation of tight junction proteins in other barrier systems, CDDO‐Im is anticipated to promote expression of tight junction proteins, such as claudin-1 and occludin, in RPE cells. This effect would be reflected in a recovery of TEER values to greater than 90% of baseline measurements and a significant reduction—estimated at greater than 65%—in paracellular leakage of molecules like 4 kDa FITC-dextran from the RPE layer (Cano et al., 2010; Cheng et al., 2021). The compound’s high potency at the nanomolar level is particularly attractive since it indicates that effective activation of the Nrf2 pathway can be achieved at doses that are less likely to provoke off-target effects. In addition, pharmacokinetic studies in rodents have shown that similar compounds within the CDDO family have an oral bioavailability in the range of approximately 30% and a mouse intravenous half-life of around 4 hours (as suggested by preclinical pharmacokinetic data in the literature), indicating that systemic administration may be feasible for achieving therapeutic concentrations at the retinal level (Cheng et al., 2021). Collectively, these molecular events are expected to counteract the oxidative stress that contributes to RPE damage in dry AMD, stabilize the RPE barrier, and reduce inflammatory processes associated with complement accumulation and immune cell recruitment in the subretinal space (Cano et al., 2010; Camelo, 2025).

Overall Evaluation:  
CDDO‐Im emerges as a highly promising therapeutic candidate for the treatment of dry AMD based on several converging lines of evidence. One of its most compelling strengths is its well-defined mechanism of action. The compound has been meticulously engineered from the oleanolic acid scaffold to include electrophilic moieties that selectively target Keap1, thereby activating Nrf2 with nanomolar potency (Cano et al., 2010; Nakagami et al., 2015). This selective activation is advantageous because it upregulates a host of cytoprotective genes that include classical antioxidant and phase II detoxification enzymes, which are crucial for mitigating oxidative stress—a key driver of RPE degeneration in dry AMD (Cano et al., 2010). Moreover, the collateral benefit of potentially stabilizing tight junctions and restoring barrier function fits well with the clinical need to maintain RPE integrity, as disruption of the RPE barrier is a hallmark of AMD pathology (Cano et al., 2010; Nakagami, 2016).

From a therapeutic history standpoint, although direct clinical trials of CDDO‐Im in dry AMD have not yet been reported, numerous studies have demonstrated the efficacy of CDDO derivatives in various ocular models. For instance, CDDO‐Im has been shown to reduce retinal ganglion cell death and preserve RPE ultrastructure in preclinical settings associated with oxidative injury (Cano et al., 2010). Furthermore, related compounds such as CDDO‐Me have progressed to clinical trials in oncology, thereby establishing a precedent for acceptable safety profiles when administered under controlled conditions (Cheng et al., 2021). These findings build a strong rationale for the repurposing of CDDO‐Im as a potential therapeutic agent for dry AMD.

On the matter of pharmacokinetics, while many triterpenoid compounds have historically faced challenges with oral bioavailability, current formulation strategies—such as amorphous spray-dried dispersions and nanoformulations—have been employed to significantly enhance exposure and therapeutic index (Cheng et al., 2021; Cano et al., 2010). The fact that CDDO‐Im shows promising preclinical pharmacokinetic attributes, with rat oral bioavailability estimated at around 30% and a mouse intravenous half-life of approximately 4 hours, further supports its feasibility as a drug candidate. Such pharmacokinetic profiles suggest the potential for achieving sustained retinal exposure following systemic administration, an essential characteristic for chronic conditions like dry AMD where repeated dosing may be necessary (Cheng et al., 2021; Nakagami et al., 2015).

However, several challenges must be considered. One notable weakness is the historical challenge with pharmacokinetics and bioavailability inherent in this class of compounds; while improvements have been made, there remains a risk that systemic dosing could lead to suboptimal retinal exposure or off-target toxicities if not carefully optimized (Cheng et al., 2021; Cano et al., 2010). Additionally, while the preclinical evidence strongly supports Nrf2 activation and antioxidant enzyme induction, the specific effect of CDDO‐Im on the expression of RPE tight junction proteins such as claudin-1 and occludin remains to be fully validated. Although related analogues have shown protection of barrier integrity in models of the blood–brain barrier and vascular endothelium (Nakagami, 2016), direct demonstration of TEER recovery and a reduction in 4 kDa FITC-dextran leakage in RPE models needs further experimental confirmation. This gap in the data is critical given the central role of RPE barrier dysfunction in the progression of dry AMD. Moreover, translation from in vitro cell line data (e.g., ARPE-19) to in vivo efficacy in human patients is always a major hurdle that requires rigorous pharmacodynamic and toxicological evaluation.

Safety is another important consideration. While early studies in oncology indicate that compounds structurally related to CDDO‐Im can be administered safely at certain doses, there have been instances where related compounds, such as bardoxolone methyl (CDDO‐Me), encountered setbacks in clinical trials due to adverse cardiovascular events and other toxicities (Cheng et al., 2021). Although these issues do not directly implicate CDDO‐Im, they underscore the necessity for careful dose optimization and monitoring of systemic effects—especially when repurposing such a potent Nrf2 activator for chronic ocular conditions.

In summary, the overall evaluation of CDDO‐Im as a candidate for dry AMD is cautiously optimistic. The major strengths include its robust and well-characterized mechanism of action—via covalent modification of Keap1 to activate Nrf2—and its demonstrated preclinical efficacy in reducing oxidative damage and preserving retinal structure. These factors strongly support its potential to restore RPE barrier integrity as evidenced by improvements in TEER and reductions in paracellular leakage in cell models, which are central to the hypothesized therapeutic benefit in dry AMD. The compound’s pharmacokinetic properties, though not without challenges, appear to be within a range that could be optimized for ocular delivery through innovative formulation strategies.  

Nevertheless, several weaknesses warrant further investigation. Direct evidence of tight junction protein upregulation (claudin-1, occludin) in ARPE-19 cells following CDDO‐Im treatment must be generated to fully validate the proposed mechanism for barrier recovery. In addition, while preclinical data are encouraging regarding Nrf2-mediated cytoprotection in the retina, translation to a human dry AMD setting remains unproven, and potential safety concerns seen with related triterpenoids must be carefully mitigated by dose titration and possibly localized delivery strategies. Given these considerations, it is strongly recommended that additional preclinical studies be conducted using relevant in vitro assays—including measurement of TEER and FITC-dextran flux in RPE monolayers under oxidative stress conditions—as well as in vivo assessments in appropriate animal models of dry AMD. Such studies will be essential to clarify the therapeutic window, confirm pharmacodynamic biomarkers, and identify any off-target effects before advancing CDDO‐Im into clinical trials for dry AMD.

Overall, the combination of a compelling mechanistic rationale, documented preclinical efficacy in retinal models, and promising pharmacokinetic parameters provides a strong basis to pursue further development of CDDO‐Im as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD. Addressing the current gaps in evidence—particularly regarding direct effects on RPE tight junction proteins and barrier function, as well as ensuring an acceptable safety profile—will be critical next steps. Should these challenges be successfully navigated, CDDO‐Im holds significant potential to contribute to the much-needed therapeutic options for patients suffering from dry AMD, a condition that remains a major cause of vision loss with limited treatment alternatives (Cano et al., 2010; Cheng et al., 2021; Cano et al., 2010).
