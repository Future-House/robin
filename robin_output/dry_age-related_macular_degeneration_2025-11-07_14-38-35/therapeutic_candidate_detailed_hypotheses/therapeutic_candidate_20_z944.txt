Proposal for Z944 (Tocris 5649)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Z944 is a synthetic, rationally designed T‐type calcium channel blocker that emerged from a medicinal chemistry program aimed at developing first‐in‐class modulators with improved selectivity and pharmacokinetic profiles. It was derived from a piperazine‐based chemical backbone originally optimized from an N‐type calcium channel blocker scaffold, with extensive structure–activity relationship studies guiding its evolution to target T‐type channels (primarily CaV3.1, CaV3.2, and CaV3.3) with high affinity (Snutch & Zamponi, 2018). As part of the broader class of T‐type calcium channel blockers, Z944 belongs to a group of compounds that have been predominantly evaluated for their analgesic and anti‐epileptic properties. Its design emphasizes sub‐micromolar potency (with reported IC₅₀ values in the nanomolar range for its target channels) and a high degree of selectivity—demonstrating 70–100-fold preference for T-type channels over other voltage-gated channels such as CaV1.4 (a key L-type channel in retinal photoreceptors) (Sun et al., 2025). Although originally conceived to treat neuropathic pain and seizure disorders, its improved pharmacokinetic properties have prompted interest in repurposing Z944 for indications outside the central nervous system, including retinal diseases.  

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, Z944 has been developed and evaluated extensively in preclinical models focused on pain management and epilepsy, demonstrating encouraging translational potential by restoring functional neuronal circuit dynamics in both inflammatory pain and absence epilepsy models (Snutch & Zamponi, 2018; Melgari et al., 2022). Clinical studies have established that Z944 can effectively modulate T‐type calcium channel activity in human experimental models of inflammatory pain, and it has been advanced into Phase I and Phase II clinical trials targeting pain indications (Snutch & Zamponi, 2018). However, a consistent theme throughout published reports is that Z944, along with similar T‐type channel antagonists, has been primarily evaluated in the context of neurological disorders. Comprehensive literature reviews and patent analyses have yet to document any clinical or preclinical application of Z944 or related T‐type calcium channel blockers in the treatment of dry age‐related macular degeneration (dry AMD), retinal degeneration, or retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) dysfunction (Snutch & Zamponi, 2018; Sun et al., 2025; Jones, 2024). The modulator’s history in pain and epilepsy provides a solid foundation for its general pharmacological activity, yet its use in ocular settings remains uncharted territory.  

Mechanism of Action:  
Z944 exerts its pharmacological effects primarily through the selective inhibition of T‐type calcium channels. At the molecular level, T‐type channels (CaV3 family) are low–voltage‐activated channels essential for regulating neuronal excitability and rhythmic firing. Using state-dependent screening assays against the human CaV3.2 channel subunit, researchers have demonstrated that Z944 binds with high affinity (IC₅₀ in the range of 50–160 nM against various CaV3 isoforms), and its selectivity exceeds 70–100‐fold when compared to channels such as the CaV2.2 (N‐type) and CaV1.2 (L‐type) channels (Snutch & Zamponi, 2018). Recent structural studies using cryo–electron microscopy have provided insights into the binding mode of T–type blockers by revealing how similar compounds dock into the channel’s fenestration between domains—a strategy that has been pivotal in understanding the selectivity profile of Z944 (Sun et al., 2025).  

The biochemical mechanism of Z944’s action involves the attenuation of low–voltage–activated calcium currents that are normally responsible for initiating calcium influx in response to subthreshold depolarizations. In neurons, this inhibition translates into a decreased excitability and modulation of burst firing patterns. Extrapolating from these findings, one could hypothesize that, in non–neuronal cells such as RPE, Z944 would likewise reduce pathological calcium entry induced by oxidative stress. In the specific context of dry AMD, oxidative stress and the accumulation of toxic by‐products such as A2E have been implicated in the disruption of intracellular calcium homeostasis. An overload of intracellular Ca²⁺ can activate calcium‐dependent enzymes like calpain and calcineurin, which in turn mediate the proteolytic degradation of key tight junction proteins—including zonula occludens‐1 (ZO‐1) and occludin—thereby compromising the integrity of the RPE barrier (Korkka, n.d.).  

Even though Z944’s molecular interactions have been largely characterized in neuronal systems, the rationalization for its use in RPE cells is based on the observation that T‐type channels are indeed expressed in these cells. Immunostaining studies in mouse RPE tissue have confirmed the presence of CaV3.1 and CaV3.2 isoforms, indicating that T‐type channel–mediated calcium influx contributes to RPE physiology (Korkka, n.d.). While the direct mechanistic link between T‐type channel inhibition and the prevention of calpain/calcineurin activation in RPE cells remains to be experimentally verified, the well‐established role of calcium as a trigger for these enzymes suggests that Z944 could effectively mitigate calcium‐dependent tight junction degradation if pathological calcium overload is sufficiently attenuated. Moreover, evidence from related pharmacological studies suggests that at low nanomolar concentrations, Z944 can achieve significant T‐type channel blockade without affecting other voltage‐gated channels, which is crucial to avoid unintended consequences on retinal signaling mediated by CaV1.4 channels (Sun et al., 2025).  

Expected Effect:  
The central hypothesis driving the repurposing of Z944 for dry AMD is that by inhibiting T‐type channels in the RPE, Z944 will mitigate oxidative stress–induced intracellular Ca²⁺ overload. Oxidative stress is a well‐recognized contributor to RPE dysfunction and dry AMD pathogenesis, and excessive intracellular calcium can activate calpain and calcineurin enzymes. These calcium‐dependent proteases degrade tight junction proteins such as ZO‐1, leading to barrier dysfunction as evidenced by decreased transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) and disorganized ZO‐1 continuity. In vitro experiments using patient‐derived RPE cells have shown that these cells often upregulate T‐type calcium channel expression under stress conditions, thereby providing a plausible target for Z944 (Jones, 2024).  

In the proposed experimental design, RPE cells loaded with A2E (a bisretinoid that accumulates with age and contributes to oxidative stress in the RPE) will be exposed to oxidative conditions to induce intracellular Ca²⁺ overload and subsequent tight junction disruption. The addition of Z944 at concentrations around 50 nM is expected to significantly reduce this pathological calcium influx. This, in turn, should lead to a decrease in calpain and calcineurin activation, preventing the phosphorylation and degradation of occludin and disruption of ZO‐1 continuity. As a result, the integrity of the RPE barrier should be restored, evidenced by normalized TEER measurements and preserved tight junction morphology on Western blot analyses for occludin phosphorylation (Korkka, n.d.; Sun et al., 2025).  

It is important to note that while an inhibitory effect on T‐type channels in the RPE might be expected based on neuronal data, specific studies in RPE cells remain limited. Other T‐type channel inhibitors, such as ML218, have been shown to modulate phagocytosis in human embryonic stem cell–derived RPE models, albeit with a focus on processes different from tight junction maintenance (Korkka, n.d.). Therefore, the expected outcome is primarily extrapolated from broader knowledge on calcium‐dependent processes in RPE cells and the established pharmacodynamic profile of Z944 in other models. Furthermore, the selectivity for CaV3 isoforms suggests that even in the presence of secondary channel populations, Z944 is unlikely to affect L‐type channels like CaV1.4 at the low nanomolar doses proposed, thereby limiting off‐target risks that could arise from disruption of normal photoreceptor function.  

Overall Evaluation:  
From an innovation perspective, repurposing Z944 for dry AMD presents a novel approach by directly targeting a mechanism that has not been extensively explored in the field of macular degeneration. The therapeutic candidate’s high potency and selectivity for T‐type calcium channels are clear strengths, as these characteristics set it apart from older, less selective compounds such as mibefradil (Snutch & Zamponi, 2018). Its improved pharmacokinetic profile and proven translational potential in pain and epilepsy models lend additional credibility to the compound as a drug candidate with potential for ocular application. The emphasis on a mechanistic strategy—namely, reducing oxidative stress–induced intracellular Ca²⁺ overload to prevent calpain/calcineurin–mediated degradation of tight junction proteins—addresses a critical pathological process in the RPE that could underlie barrier dysfunction in dry AMD (Snutch & Zamponi, 2018).  

However, several weaknesses and challenges must be considered. First, the literature reviewed to date provides no direct evidence for the efficacy of Z944 in ocular models or specifically in dry AMD. All available data regarding Z944 pertain to its use in neurological applications, with multiple sources explicitly noting that no trials or studies have been conducted using T‐type blockers for age‐related macular degeneration, retinal degeneration, or RPE dysfunction (Snutch & Zamponi, 2018; Sun et al., 2025; Jones, 2024). This represents a significant gap in the translational pathway that must be addressed through rigorous preclinical testing in ocular models before any clinical application can be contemplated.  

Secondly, while the proposed mechanism is biologically plausible, the exact contribution of T‐type channels to calcium dysregulation in the RPE under oxidative stress conditions remains incompletely defined. Although immunostaining and electrophysiological studies have confirmed the expression of CaV3.1 and CaV3.2 in RPE cells (Korkka, n.d.), the extent to which these channels drive calpain and calcineurin activation in the context of AMD is uncertain. Most existing studies in RPE have focused on aspects such as phagocytosis or VEGF secretion (Korkka, n.d.), leaving the specific role of calcium overload in tight junction disruption relatively underexplored. This uncertainty underscores the need for detailed mechanistic studies—including TEER assays, Western blots for tight junction proteins, and calcium imaging under oxidative conditions in RPE models—to validate the proposed therapeutic approach.  

Another potential concern relates to the off‐target effects on other calcium channels, particularly those relevant to retinal function. Although Z944 boasts a 70–100–fold selectivity for T‐type channels over L‐type channels, some studies have shown that at higher concentrations or under specific conditions, Z944 can interact with CaV1.4 channels, which are critical for synaptic function in photoreceptors (Sun et al., 2025). Even though the proposed dosing (around 50 nM) is significantly below the levels that affect CaV1.4, careful dose–response studies in RPE and retinal tissue are warranted to ensure that the therapeutic window is both effective and safe. Moreover, the differences in channel expression and regulation between neuronal tissue and RPE may yield unanticipated pharmacodynamic responses that could complicate the translatability of the preclinical findings to an ocular setting.  

Strengths of the candidate include its robust preclinical profile in terms of selectivity and pharmacokinetics, and its innovative mechanism that targets a critical but relatively underexplored pathway in dry AMD pathology. The specificity for T‐type channels provides a clear molecular rationale for preventing calcium‐dependent tight junction degradation, which is an attractive approach for mitigating RPE barrier dysfunction. Furthermore, the rational design and extensive optimization that have characterized Z944’s development inspire confidence in its chemical and biological properties (Snutch & Zamponi, 2018).  

On the other hand, the weaknesses are equally notable. The lack of historical or preclinical evidence for efficacy in dry AMD represents a major translational hurdle. Before Z944 can be considered a viable candidate for repurposing in dry AMD, comprehensive studies assessing its effects on RPE barrier function, tight junction integrity (e.g., ZO-1 and occludin dynamics), and overall retinal health under stress conditions are needed. In addition, potential differences in the regulatory networks governing calcium homeostasis in RPE compared to neurons could result in unpredictable responses to T-type channel blockade. Also, even though early findings in neural models suggest that Z944 can reverse stress–induced barrier leaks at low concentrations, it remains to be seen if these effects are recapitulated in human-derived RPE cultures, particularly in the complex environment of AMD where oxidative stress and chronic inflammation play pivotal roles (Jones, 2024; Sun et al., 2025).  

In conclusion, while Z944 exhibits many properties that make it an attractive candidate from a drug development perspective—such as high potency, remarkable selectivity for T‐type channels, and a favorable pharmacokinetic profile—the repurposing of Z944 for dry age‐related macular degeneration is currently speculative. The existing literature overwhelmingly supports its use in pain and neurological disorders, and there is a conspicuous absence of data linking T-type calcium channel modulation to therapeutic outcomes in AMD or RPE dysfunction (Snutch & Zamponi, 2018; Sun et al., 2025; Jones, 2024). The proposed hypothesis that Z944 can mitigate oxidative stress–induced intracellular Ca²⁺ overload in RPE and thereby restore tight junction integrity is mechanistically plausible given the known roles of Ca²⁺ signals in RPE barrier regulation; however, the strategy remains unproven in the ocular context.  

Moving forward, it would be advisable to initiate a series of in vitro studies using patient-derived or stem-cell-derived RPE models under oxidative conditions and A2E loading, with a focus on measuring TEER, ZO-1 expression and continuity, occludin phosphorylation levels, and calpain/calcineurin activation. These studies would provide the necessary proof-of-concept data to justify in vivo preclinical work. Additionally, comparative studies with other T-type channel inhibitors (such as ML218) could help delineate whether the effects observed are class effects or specific to Z944, thereby further validating the therapeutic target.  

Overall, Z944 holds potential as a repurposed drug candidate for dry AMD, particularly from an innovative mechanistic standpoint. Its strengths in chemical design and robust pharmacological activity in non-ocular systems are promising; yet, significant gaps in the research related to RPE-specific outcomes and the translation of T-type channel inhibition to barrier protection in the retina must be addressed. Hence, while the proposed direction is intriguing and represents a novel therapeutic angle, additional preclinical studies focused explicitly on ocular models are critical to determine whether Z944 can fulfill its promise in the context of dry age-related macular degeneration (Snutch & Zamponi, 2018; Sun et al., 2025).
