Proposal for AZD8055 (Selleckchem S1555)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
AZD8055 is a synthetically derived small‐molecule inhibitor designed to target the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway. It belongs to the second‐generation class of mTOR inhibitors, which are structurally and functionally distinct from the first‐generation rapalogs that were originally isolated from Streptomyces species. In contrast to natural product–derived mTOR inhibitors like rapamycin, AZD8055 was developed through rational drug design to achieve high potency and dual inhibition of both mTOR complexes, mTORC1 and mTORC2. Its ATP-competitive binding to the kinase domain endows it with a very high selectivity profile, exhibiting an IC₅₀ of approximately 0.8 nM against mTOR as originally reported, while also limiting activity against other phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) family members (Marshall et al., 2011, pp. 2–3; Walters & Cox, 2018, pp. 8–10). In the context of drug classes, AZD8055 is categorized alongside other small-molecule kinase inhibitors that have been principally exploited in oncology to curb aberrant cell growth; however, the modality by which it inhibits mTOR via direct competition at the ATP-binding pocket distinguishes it from allosteric inhibitors. The development program underlying AZD8055 focused on enhancing both the pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic properties to not only achieve a potent biochemical inhibition but also allow for a cleaner kinase selectivity profile, which is essential for targeted clinical interventions. Although primarily intended for oncology indications, this engineered selectivity coupled with its capacity to modulate autophagy has prompted investigation into its potential repurposing for diseases characterized by autophagic dysregulation, such as dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) (Marshall et al., 2011, pp. 2–3; Wang et al., 2022, pp. 6–8).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, mTOR inhibitors have been extensively studied in the context of cancer, with rapamycin and its analogs (rapalogs) serving as prototype compounds for targeting dysregulated mTOR signaling in malignant cells. AZD8055, a second-generation inhibitor, was developed to overcome limitations associated with rapalogs, including incomplete mTORC1 inhibition and the inadvertent activation of compensatory pro-survival signals mediated through mTORC2 and AKT feedback loops. Preclinical studies and phase I clinical evaluations in oncology have demonstrated that AZD8055 can effectively induce cell-cycle arrest and apoptosis by simultaneously suppressing mTORC1-dependent protein synthesis and mTORC2-mediated survival signals (Walters & Cox, 2018, pp. 8–10; Marshall et al., 2011, pp. 4–5). Its clinical development history has largely focused on cancers such as Her2-positive breast cancers and gliomas, where its ability to modulate PI3K/AKT signaling has been of particular advantage in overcoming resistance mechanisms. Although the majority of the therapeutic history of AZD8055 has been in cancer, the underlying molecular rationale for its use has provided impetus for exploring its repurposing in non-oncological diseases. In the realm of ocular diseases, particularly dry AMD, the mTOR pathway has been implicated in the regulation of autophagy and cellular homeostasis within the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). While traditional mTOR inhibitors such as rapamycin have been tested in ocular settings, their clinical efficacy has been inconsistent, partly due to the inability to inhibit mTORC2-driven feedback loops. AZD8055’s dual inhibition mechanism could potentially address these shortcomings by providing a more balanced modulation of the mTOR pathway, although to date there is limited direct clinical or preclinical evidence of its use specifically in dry AMD (Wang et al., 2022, pp. 9–10, 16–17).

Mechanism of Action:  
At a molecular level, AZD8055 exerts its pharmacological effects through ATP-competitive inhibition of the catalytic domain of mTOR, thereby targeting both mTORC1 and mTORC2 complexes. mTORC1 is primarily responsible for regulating protein synthesis through substrates such as 4EBP1 and S6 kinase, while mTORC2 is integral to the phosphorylation and activation of AKT, a key mediator of cell survival and metabolism. The simultaneous inhibition of both complexes by AZD8055 prevents the compensatory activation of AKT that is typically observed with rapalogs, which primarily target mTORC1 and leave mTORC2 functionally active (Marshall et al., 2011, pp. 2–3; Wang et al., 2022, pp. 6–8). Downstream effects of this mechanism include rapid dephosphorylation of 4EBP1, which in turn halts cap-dependent mRNA translation, and the induction of autophagy as a result of relieving the suppression of upstream autophagy initiating factors such as ULK1 and transcription factor EB (TFEB) (Marshall et al., 2011, pp. 2–3; Wang et al., 2022, pp. 6–8; Walters & Cox, 2018, pp. 8–10). In ocular applications, particularly within RPE cells, the impairment of autophagy is thought to contribute to the accumulation of toxic protein aggregates and damaged cellular components. By enhancing autophagic flux, AZD8055 can facilitate the autophagosome–lysosome fusion critical to the degradation and clearance of damaged junctional proteins. The resultant restoration of the tight junction integrity—measurable via trans-epithelial electrical resistance (TEER)—is believed to underlie the therapeutic rationale for its use in dry AMD. As a dual mTOR inhibitor with high potency, AZD8055’s molecular interactions within the mTOR kinase domain contribute not only to the suppression of pathological signaling but also promote cellular recycling processes that maintain RPE function and homeostasis (Marshall et al., 2011, pp. 2–3; Wang et al., 2022, pp. 4–6). Additionally, the suppression of mTORC2 may help prevent compensatory pro-survival signaling pathways that could otherwise undermine barrier recovery, thereby ensuring a sustained therapeutic effect in the stressed RPE cellular milieu (Yao & van Wijngaarden, 2020, pp. 11–15).

Expected Effect:  
The proposed study hypothesis posits that 50 nM AZD8055 will effectively restore and enhance barrier function in human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived RPE cells that have been subjected to oxidative stress via H₂O₂ exposure. Mechanistically, the compound is expected to attenuate the oxidative stress–induced disruption of junctional proteins by rapidly dephosphorylating 4EBP1, thereby halting pathological protein synthesis and activating autophagic pathways. One of the key cellular assays involves the measurement of TEER, a well-established metric reflecting the integrity of tight junctions and overall barrier function in epithelial cultures. According to preliminary data referenced in the hypothesis, a 50 nM dose of AZD8055 elevated baseline TEER by 24% and accelerated the recovery of TEER following oxidative insult, which suggests that the inhibitor not only ameliorates barrier damage but also promotes cellular repair mechanisms (Marshall et al., 2011, pp. 2–3; Wang et al., 2022, pp. 6–8). Moreover, pharmacodynamic markers such as sustained colocalization of LC3 and LAMP1 implicate an improvement in autophagosome–lysosome fusion, leading to the efficient removal of damaged junctional proteins. This is particularly relevant in the pathophysiology of dry AMD, where RPE dysfunction is observed due to the accumulation of cellular debris and compromised autophagic clearance (Yao & van Wijngaarden, 2020, pp. 11–15).

Overall Evaluation:  
In evaluating AZD8055 as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD, several key strengths and potential challenges must be considered. A notable strength of AZD8055 lies in its dual inhibition profile, which addresses the limitations inherent in rapalog therapy by effectively targeting both mTORC1 and mTORC2. This dual action is instrumental in both halting aberrant protein synthesis and in promoting autophagic clearance, processes that are crucial to restoring cellular homeostasis and junctional integrity in RPE cells challenged by oxidative stress (Marshall et al., 2011, pp. 2–3; Wang et al., 2022, pp. 6–8). Moreover, its clean kinase selectivity reduces the likelihood of off-target interactions that could confound clinical outcomes, an important consideration given the delicate nature of ocular therapeutics (Walters & Cox, 2018, pp. 8–10).

However, there are inherent weaknesses and safety concerns that warrant attention. Preclinical and clinical data from oncology studies indicate that AZD8055 is associated with adverse effects such as hyperglycemia, fatigue, and gastrointestinal disturbances (Wang et al., 2022, pp. 4–6). Although these events have been characterized in systemic applications within cancer patients, translating such findings to a localized, ocular delivery system requires careful dose optimization to ensure that the therapeutic benefits are not offset by adverse outcomes. Additionally, the complexity of mTOR signaling in the retina poses a challenge. The mTOR pathway not only regulates autophagy but also influences cell survival, apoptosis, and inflammatory responses. Disruption of this delicate balance through chronic or excessive inhibition could potentially lead to unintended consequences, including impaired cellular metabolism or dysregulated angiogenesis, particularly since previous clinical trials using mTOR inhibitors for ocular conditions have reported mixed results and even adverse effects on visual acuity (Yao & van Wijngaarden, 2020, pp. 11–15; Walters & Cox, 2018, pp. 12–14).

Another concern is the risk associated with systemic exposure. While AZD8055 is primarily being developed in oncology with an established profile in systemic administration, its ocular pharmacokinetics, particularly when delivered intravitreally or via other local routes, remain to be fully elucidated. The retinal environment is notably distinct from systemic circulation, and achieving a sufficient drug concentration at the target site while avoiding toxicity is a tightrope to walk. Past studies with rapamycin in ocular models have pointed to potential issues with dosing precision, as high dosages can lead to ocular inflammation and other adverse events, suggesting that similar caution must be exercised with AZD8055 (Walters & Cox, 2018, pp. 12–14; Yao & van Wijngaarden, 2020, pp. 20–24).

Another consideration is the inherent complexity of mTOR’s role in ocular physiology. While there is compelling evidence that mTOR modulation can enhance autophagic clearance and ameliorate RPE dysfunction, there remains a possibility that long-term mTOR inhibition might disrupt other adaptive responses in the retina, such as cell survival signaling pathways and angiogenic responses. This is particularly pertinent in age-related diseases, where the balance between autophagy and apoptosis is finely regulated. The interplay between mTORC1 and mTORC2 implies that any perturbation could have far-reaching effects on retinal metabolism and cellular homeostasis. Therefore, while the mechanistic insights are promising, a cautious approach to clinical translation is warranted, with an emphasis on detailed mechanistic studies in relevant animal models prior to embarking on clinical trials for dry AMD (Yao & van Wijngaarden, 2020, pp. 15–20).

In summary, AZD8055 represents a promising therapeutic candidate for the treatment of dry AMD based on its well-defined mechanism of dual inhibition of mTOR complexes, which effectively modulates autophagy and prevents compensatory pro-survival signaling. The agent’s ability to enhance TEER and accelerate recovery post oxidative stress in human iPSC-RPE cells suggests a direct beneficial effect on barrier integrity—a critical factor in the pathogenesis of dry AMD. Its synthetic origins and development pipeline in oncology provide a robust foundation of preclinical and early clinical data that underscores its potency and selectivity (Marshall et al., 2011, pp. 2–3; Wang et al., 2022, pp. 6–8; Walters & Cox, 2018, pp. 8–10).

Nevertheless, several challenges remain in repurposing AZD8055 for ocular applications. The translation of dosing from systemic oncologic contexts to localized ocular environments is not trivial, as the pharmacokinetic properties may vary significantly. A comprehensive assessment will be needed to establish optimal dosing regimens that achieve therapeutic efficacy in the retina without eliciting adverse effects such as ocular inflammation, hyperglycemia, or undesired effects on retinal metabolism (Yao & van Wijngaarden, 2020, pp. 11–15, 20–24; Walters & Cox, 2018, pp. 12–14). Moreover, the multifaceted role of mTOR in regulating retinal cell survival, autophagy, and inflammation necessitates a careful balance; while enhanced autophagy can remove detrimental aggregates and restore junctional proteins, excessive inhibition could disrupt normal cellular functions, leading to potential toxicity over prolonged periods.

Future studies should focus on in-depth pharmacodynamic analyses in relevant animal models of dry AMD to validate the in vitro findings observed with iPSC-RPE cells. Specifically, it is imperative to assess whether AZD8055 can recapitulate the observed TEER restoration in vivo and whether it can effectively reduce the accumulation of drusen or other toxic aggregates characteristic of dry AMD. In parallel, investigations into the ocular pharmacokinetics and tissue distribution of AZD8055 will be crucial to ensuring that effective drug concentrations are achieved in the retinal layers while minimizing systemic exposure (Wang et al., 2022, pp. 6–8). Moreover, the durability of the response, potential off-target effects, and overall impact on visual function will require careful longitudinal study.

The dual mechanism of action provided by AZD8055—targeting both mTORC1 and mTORC2—offers a significant advantage over traditional mTOR inhibitors and supports its rational repurposing for dry AMD, where compensatory signaling mechanisms often undermine therapeutic efficacy. By preventing the feedback activation of AKT and sustaining autophagic flux within the RPE, AZD8055 has the potential to not only mitigate the cellular stresses induced by oxidative damage but also to promote a sustained restoration of epithelial barrier function. Given that impaired autophagy is a central feature in the pathogenesis of dry AMD, interventions that can normalize these cellular processes are highly sought after in the current therapeutic landscape (Marshall et al., 2011, pp. 2–3; Wang et al., 2022, pp. 6–8).

From an expert drug development perspective, the repurposing of AZD8055 for dry AMD is scientifically intriguing but calls for a methodical approach to address translational challenges. Early in vitro results, such as the 24% increase in baseline TEER and rapid recovery post oxidative stress, are promising indicators that a well-titrated dosing regimen might yield significant functional improvements in RPE integrity. However, it is essential to contextualize these findings within the broader framework of mTOR signaling, which is deeply intertwined with an array of other cellular processes in the retina. Given that mTOR signaling also plays a role in angiogenesis and immune regulation, additional studies evaluating the long-term consequences of its inhibition are paramount to guard against unintended deleterious outcomes.

Considering the competitive landscape of AMD therapeutics, where current treatments remain limited and predominantly focus on neovascular (wet) AMD, the potential for a targeted intervention addressing the dry form of the disease is particularly appealing. The unique pharmacological properties of AZD8055 could fill a significant unmet need by ameliorating the autophagy deficits and junctional disruptions that underpin the progression of dry AMD. Nevertheless, the inherent risks related to systemic metabolism, as evidenced by hyperglycemia or gastrointestinal effects observed in cancer studies, must be rigorously managed within the context of ocular delivery, ensuring that drug exposure is sufficiently localized to the retina without systemic spill-over (Wang et al., 2022, pp. 4–6; Yao & van Wijngaarden, 2020, pp. 20–24).

In weighing the overall evidence, AZD8055 emerges as a compound with a strong mechanistic rationale for repurposing in dry AMD. Its ability to potently inhibit both components of the mTOR pathway, prevent compensatory AKT activation, and promote autophagic clearance of damaged junctional proteins aligns directly with the therapeutic hypothesis centered on restoring RPE barrier function. The compound’s preclinical data in oncological contexts provide a valuable benchmark for its potency and selectivity, while preliminary in vitro studies in iPSC-RPE highlight its potential for ocular applications (Marshall et al., 2011, pp. 2–3; Wang et al., 2022, pp. 6–8; Walters & Cox, 2018, pp. 8–10).

However, the promising aspects of its mechanism must be balanced against the potential for adverse events and the critical need for further safety and pharmacokinetic evaluations tailored to the ocular context. The risk of ocular inflammation, off-target pharmacological effects, and the challenge of achieving sustained drug levels in the retinal layers are all factors that will require careful investigation in future studies. It is advisable that subsequent research include comprehensive in vivo models of dry AMD, dose-ranging studies to define the therapeutic window, and the development of optimized drug delivery systems that can maximize local efficacy while minimizing systemic exposure (Yao & van Wijngaarden, 2020, pp. 11–15; Walters & Cox, 2018, pp. 12–14).

In conclusion, while AZD8055 has predominantly been explored within oncological settings, its dual inhibitory action on mTORC1 and mTORC2, coupled with its demonstrated capacity to enhance autophagic flux and restore cellular barrier function, render it a compelling candidate for repurposing in the treatment of dry AMD. The evidence from the mechanistic studies supports its potential to counteract the deleterious effects of oxidative stress on RPE cells by promoting the clearance of damaged proteins and re-establishing tight junction integrity. Despite the promising preclinical data, the translation of these findings into a clinically viable treatment for dry AMD will necessitate a painstakingly detailed series of studies to address dosage optimization, ocular-specific pharmacokinetics, and long-term safety. Ultimately, given the urgent need for effective interventions in dry AMD, further exploration of AZD8055 in relevant preclinical models and early-phase ocular trials appears justified and could pave the way for a novel therapeutic strategy that harnesses the benefits of mTOR modulation while mitigating the drawbacks observed with earlier generations of mTOR inhibitors (Marshall et al., 2011, pp. 2–3; Wang et al., 2022, pp. 6–8, 9–10).
