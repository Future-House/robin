Proposal for PXS-5153A (MedChemExpress HY-111217)

This report evaluates the potential of PXS-5153A—a synthetic small‐molecule inhibitor originally developed as a selective LOXL2/LOXL3 inhibitor—for repurposing in dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD). The hypothesis under evaluation is that inhibition of LOXL2 by PXS-5153A will prevent pathogenic collagen IV and fibronectin crosslinking in Bruch’s membrane (BM), reduce matrix stiffness, and by indirectly alleviating abnormal mechanical tension on retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cell receptors, stabilize RPE tight junction anchors (ZO-1/occludin). This mechanism is expected to enhance transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) in co-culture barrier assays and thereby preserve or restore outer blood–retina barrier integrity. The following sections provide a detailed analysis of the candidate’s origins, therapeutic history, mechanism of action, expected effects in relevant assays, and a balanced overall evaluation based on the current literature.

I. Overview of Therapeutic Candidate  
PXS-5153A is a synthetically derived molecule that belongs to the class of selective lysyl oxidase inhibitors. In its design, medicinal chemistry efforts focused on optimizing potency and selectivity for LOXL2, along with activity against LOXL3, two members of the lysyl oxidase family that catalyze the oxidative deamination of lysine residues in collagens and elastin to promote covalent crosslink formation. These crosslinks confer the mechanical strength and stiffness of extracellular matrices (ECMs) such as Bruch’s membrane. Synthetic routes to compounds like PXS-5153A typically rely on structure-based design and iterative optimization to achieve potent enzyme inhibition with favorable pharmacokinetic properties (Lin et al., 2020). The compound, commercially available under MedChemExpress HY-111217, is representative of a new therapeutic class that has been primarily explored in the context of fibrosis and tumor microenvironment modulation. Given that ECM stiffening is implicated in the pathogenesis of dry AMD, targeting LOXL2 represents a novel approach compared to conventional anti-inflammatory or anti-angiogenic strategies.

II. Therapeutic History  
The LOXL2 inhibitor class, including molecules such as PXS-5153A, has been extensively tested in preclinical models of fibrosis and cancer. For example, within non-ocular systems, LOXL2 activity has been linked to increased tissue stiffness that fosters metastatic progression and desmoplastic reactions in tumors, and selective inhibition of LOXL2 has been shown to reduce crosslink density, attenuating fibrotic remodeling (Lin et al., 2020). Experimental inhibition of lysyl oxidase activity using non-selective agents like β-aminopropionitrile (BAPN) has also been applied to in vitro and in vivo models to modulate ECM assembly, with effects on barrier function observed in RPE cultures co-treated with endothelial cells (Benedicto et al., 2017). Although LOXL2 inhibitors have not traditionally been used to treat dry AMD, evidence underscores the centrality of aberrant ECM crosslinking in BM thickening observed during aging and AMD progression (Wang et al., 2025). In particular, the endothelial cell–RPE crosstalk studies demonstrate a critical role for lysyl oxidase activity in regulating collagen bundle formation and in establishing RPE barrier properties (Benedicto et al., 2017). Accordingly, while there is no direct clinical or preclinical record of PXS-5153A in AMD, the antifibrotic profile of the compound and its impact on extracellular matrix mechanics in other disease contexts serve as a compelling rationale to explore its application in dry AMD.

III. Mechanism of Action  
At the molecular level, LOXL2 catalyzes the oxidative deamination of ε-amino groups of lysine or hydroxylysine residues present in collagens and elastin. This reaction is critical for the formation of covalent crosslinks that stabilize the ECM. Overexpression or hyperactivity of LOXL2 results in an excessively crosslinked and stiff matrix. In the eye, particularly within Bruch’s membrane—which is a complex, stratified ECM structure located between the RPE and the choroid—aberrant collagen IV and fibronectin crosslinking are known to increase mechanical tension. Such mechanical changes are thought to adversely affect RPE cell function by interfering with integrin-mediated cell–matrix interactions and perturbing the proper localization of tight junction proteins, including ZO-1 and occludin (Benedicto et al., 2017).

PXS-5153A functions by binding to the catalytic pocket of LOXL2, thereby preventing its enzymatic activity. By blocking the oxidative deamination step, PXS-5153A reduces the formation of both immature and mature crosslinks in ECM proteins. The anticipated outcome is a lower crosslink density in Bruch’s membrane, resulting in a softer, more compliant matrix environment. This reduction in mechanical stiffness is expected to diminish abnormal mechanosignaling through β1 integrins, which are integral to the assembly and maintenance of tight junctions on RPE cells (Benedicto et al., 2017). The downstream consequences of reduced integrin activation include dampening of Rac1 and RhoA/ROCK signaling pathways, which in turn facilitate the stabilization and proper localization of tight junction proteins at the RPE cell membranes (Benedicto et al., 2017). Restoration of these junctional complexes correlates with improved barrier function, typically measured as an increased transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) in cell culture models. This mechanism is supported by studies in which inhibition of lysyl oxidase activity—by pharmacological agents such as BAPN—led to alterations in RPE barrier properties and tight junction morphology (Benedicto et al., 2017). Thus, by directly targeting LOXL2, PXS-5153A is proposed to correct the pathological ECM remodeling that underlies BM stiffening in dry AMD while re-establishing proper RPE barrier integrity.

IV. Expected Effect  
The expected therapeutic effect of PXS-5153A in dry AMD centers on normalization of the BM ECM and concomitant restoration of RPE barrier function. In the proposed experimental model—an in vitro co-culture system where human fetal RPE (hfRPE) cells are grown on a substrate mimicking BM with defined stiffness—the application of PXS-5153A at concentrations around 5 µM is anticipated to yield a number of favorable outcomes.

First, by reducing LOXL2-mediated crosslinking of collagen IV and fibronectin, the drug should decrease the mechanical stiffness of the BM analog. A less rigid BM reduces aberrant integrin activation on the basolateral surfaces of RPE cells, which has been shown to be critical for proper tight junction assembly. In several studies, when the extracellular matrix is normalized—such as by altering substrate stiffness—there is an improvement in the localization of key tight junction proteins, specifically occludin and ZO-1, at the cell membrane (Benedicto et al., 2017).  
Second, the reduced matrix stiffness is expected to alleviate the mechanical stress experienced by RPE cells, thereby facilitating the re-establishment of cell polarity and intercellular junction continuity. The stabilization of junctional complexes is measurable by TEER. In previous EC–RPE co-culture experiments, modulation of ECM mechanical properties directly correlated with increased TEER values, reflecting improved barrier function (Benedicto et al., 2017).  
Moreover, the proper organization of the basement membrane is not only a structural consideration; the biochemical signaling through β1 integrin receptors—critical for maintaining cell adhesion and survival—would return toward homeostatic levels, further supporting the integrity of the RPE layer (Benedicto et al., 2017). These integrin-mediated signals influence downstream effectors such as Rac1 and RhoA/ROCK, which have been implicated in regulating tight junction dynamics (Benedicto et al., 2017). Progress in these pathways is expected to manifest as an overall improvement in the barrier phenotype, which is central to preventing the leakage of fluid and other deleterious solutes into the subretinal space—a hallmark of dry AMD pathology.

In summary, it is anticipated that treatment with PXS-5153A in appropriate in vitro (and ultimately in vivo) models will lead to:  
• Reduced collagen IV and fibronectin crosslinking in BM, yielding a matrix with decreased stiffness (Benedicto et al., 2017).  
• Normalization of β1 integrin signaling and subsequent reorganization of RPE tight junctions with improved localization of proteins such as occludin and ZO-1 (Benedicto et al., 2017).  
• Restoration of barrier function as evidenced by increased TEER measurements and improved paracellular permeability profiles (Benedicto et al., 2017).  

Thus, PXS-5153A is expected to address one of the core aspects of dry AMD pathogenesis by targeting ECM remodeling, which in turn indirectly stabilizes RPE cell function.

V. Overall Evaluation  
The concept of using PXS-5153A to modulate ECM pathology in dry AMD is compelling given the mechanistic insights derived from studies on lysyl oxidase activity in epithelial barrier function and ECM assembly. Among the strengths of this therapeutic candidate is its robust preclinical evidence in fibrosis and tumor microenvironment models. LOXL2 has been implicated in promoting tissue stiffening by catalyzing the crosslinking of collagen and fibronectin in various tissues (Lin et al., 2020). The pathophysiological parallels between fibrotic ECM remodeling in cancers and the stiffening of Bruch’s membrane in dry AMD provide a strong rationale for repurposing a LOXL2 inhibitor in an ocular context. Numerous studies have demonstrated that extracellular matrix integrity is essential for optimal RPE barrier function, and that even subtle changes in ECM mechanics can disrupt junctional complexes and lead to barrier dysfunction (Benedicto et al., 2017). Thus, by targeting a critical enzymatic step in ECM crosslinking, PXS-5153A may confer dual benefits: it could both reduce the pathological stiffening of Bruch’s membrane and promote the reorganization of tight junction complexes, ultimately resulting in enhanced TEER and stabilization of the outer blood–retina barrier.

However, several important weaknesses or uncertainties must be considered. First, while numerous studies (using agents such as BAPN) have illustrated that lysyl oxidase inhibition affects RPE barrier function, direct evidence with selective LOXL2 inhibitors—specifically in ocular models of dry AMD—is lacking (Buniello et al., 2025). In fact, the Open Targets Platform indicates only a weak direct association between LOXL2 and age-related macular degeneration, based on limited literature evidence (Buniello et al., 2025). This raises the possibility that, although LOXL2 plays a demonstrable role in ECM crosslinking in other tissues, its specific involvement in dry AMD pathogenesis may not be as pronounced or may be influenced by other factors not recapitulated in non-ocular models.

Furthermore, potential off-target effects or the consequences of broadly altering ECM crosslinking in the eye must be carefully examined. The structural integrity of Bruch’s membrane is critical not only for its barrier function but also for nutrient transport between the choroidal circulation and the RPE. Overinhibition of LOXL2, or interference with normal ECM turnover, may inadvertently lead to basement membrane disorganization, as has been observed when lysyl oxidase activity was inhibited during critical periods of retinal development (Benedicto et al., 2017). This underscores the need for precise dose optimization and targeted delivery, ideally restricted to the ocular environment, to avoid systemic side effects.

Another consideration is the complexity of AMD pathogenesis. In addition to ECM stiffening, dry AMD involves multifactorial processes including oxidative stress, complement activation, inflammation, and the accumulation of lipoprotein deposits. Thus, while normalizing matrix stiffness is likely to improve RPE barrier function, it may be insufficient to fully arrest or reverse the disease process on its own. A combination therapy that also addresses the inflammatory and degenerative components of AMD may eventually be required (Benedicto et al., 2017; Caban et al., 2022).

From a development perspective, there is significant strength in the repurposing strategy because agents like PXS-5153A already have a detailed preclinical profile in non-ocular fibrotic models. This existing data can accelerate preclinical validation in ocular models, with studies planned in RPE–BM co-culture systems using TEER as a functional readout (Benedicto et al., 2017). Furthermore, leveraging well-characterized in vitro assays (such as those involving decellularized ECM and substrate stiffness modulation as described in the context) offers a clear experimental framework to evaluate whether the normalization of BM stiffness by PXS-5153A is sufficient to restore RPE tight junction integrity (Benedicto et al., 2017).

Nonetheless, one notable weakness is the limited direct evidence linking LOXL2 specifically to dry AMD. Although elevated lysyl oxidase activity and changes in collagen crosslinking have been observed in the context of RPE barrier function and BM integrity (Benedicto et al., 2017), the genetic and translational data associating LOXL2 with AMD are not robust. The relatively low association score (Buniello et al., 2025) suggests that additional studies are needed to clarify LOXL2’s role in the eye and to determine whether its inhibition will yield a therapeutically meaningful benefit in dry AMD patients.

In conclusion, repurposing PXS-5153A for dry AMD represents an innovative strategy that leverages the known antifibrotic effects of LOXL2 inhibition to address the deleterious ECM remodeling observed in aged Bruch’s membrane. The candidate’s synthetic origins and prior applications in fibrosis and cancer studies provide a solid foundation for its use, while the mechanistic rationale—centered on reducing pathological crosslinking and restoring RPE barrier function—is strongly supported by evidence from ECM modulation studies (Benedicto et al., 2017). However, challenges remain due to the lack of direct ocular data, potential risks of overmodulating ECM integrity, and the multifactorial nature of AMD pathology. Future research must focus on rigorous in vitro validation in RPE–BM co-culture systems and in vivo studies in relevant AMD animal models to establish the efficacy and safety profile of PXS-5153A in the context of dry AMD. Overall, despite these uncertainties, PXS-5153A holds promise as a novel ECM-targeted therapeutic candidate that could complement existing and emerging treatment strategies for this currently unmet clinical need (Lin et al., 2020; Buniello et al., 2025).

Taken together, the strengths of a clear mechanism of action, potent enzyme selectivity, and an innovative approach toward ECM normalization position PXS-5153A as an attractive candidate warranting further investigation. Nonetheless, a careful reappraisal of the molecular role of LOXL2 in the ocular microenvironment, along with detailed studies on dosing, delivery, and potential off-target effects, will be critical in determining whether this candidate can be successfully repurposed for treating dry AMD.
