Proposal for Ripasudil (Y-39983; Selleckchem S8443)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Ripasudil, also known by its research designation Y-39983 and commercially available as Selleckchem S8443, is a synthetic small‐molecule inhibitor developed originally as a Rho‐associated coiled‐coil containing protein kinase (ROCK) inhibitor. It belongs to a class of therapeutics that act in an ATP‐competitive manner to inhibit the activity of ROCK isoforms, enzymes that are critical regulators of actomyosin contractility and cell adhesion. Ripasudil was synthesized following the structural optimization of earlier compounds such as fasudil and Y-27632, with the aim of increasing both potency and ocular tolerability. Its chemical origins are linked to the rational design strategies that sought to improve enzyme inhibitory selectivity and to enhance the pharmacokinetic profile for local ocular application. Members of the ROCK inhibitor class have seen prior use in settings that require modulation of actin cytoskeleton dynamics, such as the treatment of glaucoma via enhanced aqueous humor outflow, and have also been explored in contexts involving wound healing and anti-fibrotic effects in various ocular tissues (Chatzimichail et al., 2025; Halasz & Townes-Anderson, 2016).

Therapeutic History:  
Clinically, ripasudil has been approved in Japan for the treatment of glaucoma and ocular hypertension, where it is delivered topically in the form of eye drops, demonstrating a well-established safety profile and a low incidence of systemic adverse effects. In preclinical models, it has demonstrated efficacy by increasing ocular blood flow, modifying trabecular meshwork cell contractility, and reducing intraocular pressure, thereby serving as a stepping stone and proof-of-concept for the utility of ROCK inhibitors in ocular disorders. Beyond its approved use in glaucoma, investigations of ROCK inhibitors—including ripasudil—have extended into experimental applications in corneal endothelial regeneration and have been evaluated in combination with anti-VEGF therapies for diabetic macular edema. Although direct clinical evidence specifically for dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is not available, multiple lines of preclinical research have shown that the ROCK inhibitor class modulates pathways which are believed to underlie the pathogenesis of dry AMD. Notably, the mechanistic studies have identified the role of ROCK signaling in cytoskeletal reorganization, matrix contractility, and fibrosis, all of which are processes particularly relevant to retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) barrier dysfunction seen in dry AMD (Singh & Singh, 2023; Halasz & Townes-Anderson, 2016; Mateos-Olivares et al., 2021). Moreover, the ocular pharmacokinetic profile of ripasudil—demonstrated by rabbit intravitreal studies showing a half-life of approximately 14 hours and negligible systemic exposure—is supportive of repurposing this candidate for posterior segment indications, including dry AMD (Yamaguchi et al., 2017; Mateos-Olivares et al., 2021).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, ripasudil exerts its therapeutic activity by competitively inhibiting the ATP-binding site of ROCK isoforms, thereby preventing the phosphorylation of downstream effectors such as myosin light-chain (MLC), myosin-binding subunit of MLC phosphatase (MYPT1), and LIM kinase. These phosphorylation events normally lead to enhanced actomyosin contractility, stress-fiber formation, focal adhesion assembly, and ultimately contribute to a contractile, stiffened cell phenotype. In the context of the retinal pigment epithelium, this contractility is thought to be exacerbated under conditions of oxidative stress, contributing to disassembly or mislocalization of tight junction proteins such as zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and occludin, which are essential for maintaining transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) and barrier integrity. By inhibiting ROCK, ripasudil reduces actomyosin-driven contraction and stress-fiber formation, thereby promoting cortical F-actin remodeling and facilitating the proper membrane relocalization of tight-junction proteins. The specificity of ripasudil has been demonstrated by its greater than 100-fold selectivity against other kinases such as protein kinase C (PKC) and myotonic dystrophy kinase-related Cdc42-binding kinase (MRCK), highlighting the mechanistic precision by which it modulates ROCK-dependent signaling cascades (Halasz & Townes-Anderson, 2016). Additionally, while several studies indicate that the ROCK inhibitor class generally targets both ROCK1 and ROCK2 isoforms, quantitative biochemical data have shown that ripasudil exhibits potent enzyme inhibitory activity with IC₅₀s in the low nanomolar range (with values reported around 51 nM for ROCK1 and 19 nM for ROCK2 in some studies, though the candidate hypothesis posits values of approximately 29 nM for ROCK1 and 68 nM for ROCK2). This inhibitory profile is particularly relevant because emerging research indicates that ROCK2, in particular, is upregulated in the pathological conditions of dry AMD and plays a key role in mediating cytoskeletal changes that disrupt RPE tight junction integrity (Adams & Papillon, 2020; Cheng et al., 2025). The inhibition of ROCK thereby is expected to have a dual benefit of cytoprotection and restoration of cell junction architecture, ultimately leading to improved barrier function (Ghenciu et al., 2025; Singh & Singh, 2023).

Expected Effect:  
The central hypothesis for repurposing ripasudil for dry AMD is that by blocking ROCK activity in oxidatively stressed retinal pigment epithelium cells, the compound will lead to a reduction in actomyosin contractility, thereby alleviating the formation of pathological stress fibers that otherwise disrupt the organization of tight junction complexes. Under normal physiological conditions, tight junction proteins such as ZO-1 and occludin are localized at the plasma membrane in a continuous, belt-like manner that is essential to maintain TEER and restrict paracellular permeability. However, in dry AMD, oxidative damage, inflammation, and mechanical stress lead to cytoskeletal reorganization and a discontinuous distribution of these critical proteins, resulting in impaired barrier function and contributing to the pathogenesis of the disease. Preclinical studies, particularly those employing ARPE-19 cell models subjected to toxic insults such as paraquat or sodium iodate (NaIO₃), have demonstrated that ROCK inhibitors can dose-dependently restore TEER (with reported improvements up to 70%) and re-establish the membrane continuity of ZO-1, suggesting that these agents can “rescue” the RPE barrier phenotype under injurious conditions (Singh & Singh, 2023; Halasz & Townes-Anderson, 2016). It is expected that ripasudil, with its potent ROCK-inhibitory activity and favorable ocular pharmacokinetics (including intravitreal retention times supportive of sustained action), will similarly promote the cortical redistribution of F-actin, leading to the re-localization of junctional molecules back to the cell membrane. This mechanistic restoration should result in improved transepithelial electrical resistance and a concomitant reduction in paracellular permeability, effectively reinforcing the RPE barrier—a central therapeutic target in dry AMD. Moreover, the activation of protective cellular pathways through ROCK inhibition may also confer additional anti-apoptotic and anti-inflammatory effects, further contributing to the survival and functional integrity of the RPE under chronic oxidative stress (Yamaguchi et al., 2017; Mateos-Olivares et al., 2021; Halasz & Townes-Anderson, 2016).

Overall Evaluation:  
In evaluating ripasudil as a candidate for repurposing in dry age-related macular degeneration, several strengths become apparent. First, its established clinical use for glaucoma means that its safety, tolerability, and ocular pharmacokinetic properties are well characterized—attributes that significantly lower the translational barrier for its application in other ocular diseases. The intravitreal pharmacokinetic data, with a half-life of approximately 14 hours in rabbit models and negligible systemic exposure, suggest that therapeutic concentrations capable of modulating ROCK activity in the retina can be achieved with acceptable dosing regimens (Yamaguchi et al., 2016). Second, the mechanistic rationale is compelling: ROCK signaling is intimately involved in mediating actomyosin contractility, stress-fiber formation, and subsequent disruption of tight junction architecture in epithelial cells. Given that ROCK2 in particular is implicated in the pathological processes observed in the RPE in dry AMD, the ability of ripasudil to inhibit this pathway could translate into measurable improvements in barrier function, as indicated by preclinical assays demonstrating TEER restoration and the re-localization of ZO-1 in ARPE-19 cells (Singh & Singh, 2023; Halasz & Townes-Anderson, 2016). Third, the compound exhibits a favorable selectivity profile, with negligible off-target activity against other kinases such as PKC and MRCK, which minimizes the risk of unintended effects in non-target tissues. This specificity is critical when repurposing drugs for chronic conditions such as dry AMD, where long-term ocular safety is paramount (Halasz & Townes-Anderson, 2016).

Despite these strengths, there are notable weaknesses and gaps that must be addressed before ripasudil can be confidently advanced as a therapeutic for dry AMD. A primary limitation is the paucity of direct experimental evidence in RPE models of dry AMD. While the hypothesis is supported by data from related in vitro systems (e.g., ARPE-19 cells subjected to oxidative stress), robust in vivo studies in animal models that recapitulate the full spectrum of dry AMD pathophysiology are still needed. In particular, data demonstrating that ripasudil can restore tight junction integrity and improve TEER in the context of chronic, progressive RPE dysfunction remain limited (Yamaguchi et al., 2017; Mateos-Olivares et al., 2021). Furthermore, although the compound’s ROCK inhibitory activity is well documented, the available literature does not consistently detail the isoform-specific effects in RPE cells; given that ROCK1 and ROCK2 may play distinct roles in the pathogenesis of dry AMD, clarifying the relative contributions of each isoform is essential. The candidate hypothesis posits specific IC₅₀ values for ROCK1 and ROCK2, yet discrepancies exist across reported studies regarding the precise inhibitory concentrations, which indicates that further biochemical characterization is warranted (Adams & Papillon, 2020; Cheng et al., 2025). Another consideration is the potential for adverse effects when repurposing a drug originally optimized for anterior-segment diseases for posterior-segment applications. Although the ocular safety profile of ripasudil in glaucoma is well established, the long-term impact of ROCK inhibition on RPE cell biology in an aged population—especially in the context of dry AMD—remains uncertain. Off-target cytoskeletal effects in other retinal cell types, possible modulation of inflammatory responses, and the risk of inadvertently triggering compensatory signaling pathways are all factors that must be carefully evaluated in preclinical studies (Iancu et al., 2021; Tan et al., 2025). Lastly, while the pharmacokinetic data support retinal exposure after intravitreal or possibly topical administration, optimizing the delivery method to ensure sustained and localized drug levels in the RPE without causing ocular surface toxicity (such as conjunctival hyperemia or corneal epithelial disturbances) will be critical. Innovative delivery systems, such as sustained-release intravitreal implants or depot formulations, may offer solutions but require further development (Yamaguchi et al., 2016; Singh & Singh, 2023).

In conclusion, the repurposing of ripasudil for the treatment of dry age-related macular degeneration is supported by a strong mechanistic rationale and a favorable safety profile derived from its established use in glaucoma. The compound’s capacity to inhibit ROCK-mediated actomyosin contraction and promote cortical F-actin remodeling positions it as a promising agent for restoring RPE barrier function—one of the key pathological features of dry AMD. However, the current literature reveals significant gaps in direct evidence regarding its effects on RPE tight junction proteins, TEER restoration, and the isoform-specific roles of ROCK in the RPE under oxidative stress. To advance ripasudil as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD, additional preclinical investigations—ideally incorporating robust animal models of dry AMD and detailed biochemical analyses of ROCK isoform activity in RPE cells—are required. Additionally, optimizations in drug delivery methodology to achieve sustained posterior-segment exposure while minimizing adverse ocular events will be imperative. Overall, ripasudil presents a compelling candidate for repurposing in dry AMD, with its strengths in mechanistic specificity, favorable ocular pharmacokinetics, and established clinical safety underscoring its potential. Nonetheless, the outlined weaknesses and knowledge gaps necessitate a rigorous program of targeted research to validate its efficacy in restoring RPE barrier integrity and to ensure its long-term safety in the intended patient population (Chatzimichail et al., 2025; Halasz & Townes-Anderson, 2016; Singh & Singh, 2023; Yamaguchi et al., 2017; Adams & Papillon, 2020; Mateos-Olivares et al., 2021).
