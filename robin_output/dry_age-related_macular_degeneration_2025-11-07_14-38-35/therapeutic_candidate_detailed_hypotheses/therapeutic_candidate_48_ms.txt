Proposal for MS-275 (Entinostat; Selleck S7100)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
MS-275, also known as Entinostat and available from Selleck (S7100), is a fully synthetic compound that belongs to the benzamide class of histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACi). Its chemical structure is defined as 3-pyridylmethyl-N-{4-[(2-aminophenyl)carbamoyl]-benzyl}carbamate, and it was originally developed by research teams including those at Mitsui Pharmaceuticals in the late 1990s (Knipstein & Gore, 2011, pp. 2–3; Rossetti, 2023, pp. 31–34). Unlike hydroxamate-based HDAC inhibitors, the benzamide motif in MS-275 engages the catalytic zinc ion in a manner that is less potent in chelation yet confers a higher degree of class I selectivity, particularly toward HDAC1 and HDAC2 (Hofny et al., n.d., pp. 7–11). Benzamides have been extensively investigated for their epigenetic modulation properties in oncology, where selective inhibition of HDACs is pursued to reprogram aberrant gene expression profiles in cancer cells (Connolly et al., 2017, pp. 1–2; Carafa et al., 2011, pp. 3–5). As a representative of its class, MS-275 has a well-defined synthetic origin and benefits from a non-natural product, medicinal chemistry–driven discovery process that emphasizes targeted inhibition of chromatin-modifying enzymes for therapeutic reactivation of silenced genes (Rossetti, 2023, pp. 31–34; Knipstein & Gore, 2011, pp. 1–2).

Therapeutic History:  
Clinically, MS-275 has been developed and evaluated primarily in the context of oncology. It has advanced into multiple Phase I and II clinical trials, particularly for solid tumors such as breast cancer, hematologic malignancies like acute leukemia, and various other cancers where deregulated HDAC activity plays a role (Hofny et al., n.d., pp. 9–11; Knipstein & Gore, 2011, pp. 2–3). Preclinical studies have demonstrated its capacity to induce cell-cycle arrest, promote apoptosis, and trigger differentiation in tumor cell lines by restoring a balance in histone acetylation (Hofny et al., n.d., pp. 7–9; Hanikoglu et al., 2018, pp. 2–3). Despite its extensive evaluation in cancer, there is no published evidence to date directly applying MS-275 to treat dry age-related macular degeneration (dry AMD) or related ocular diseases. Nonetheless, the mechanistic rationale underlying its use in oncology—namely, reactivation of key transcriptional programs via enhanced histone acetylation—is similar to the epigenetic strategy proposed for rescuing retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) barrier function (Hofny et al., n.d., pp. 9–11; Rossetti, 2023, pp. 31–34). In this sense, repurposing MS-275 for dry AMD would constitute a novel application, relying on the molecule’s ability to reverse inflammation-induced transcriptional repression, analogous to its effects in cancer cells (Connolly et al., 2017, pp. 1–2; Carafa et al., 2011, pp. 3–5).

Mechanism of Action:  
MS-275 functions by selectively inhibiting class I HDACs, particularly HDAC1 and HDAC2, through a zinc-binding mechanism that is distinct from that of hydroxamate inhibitors. The benzamide moiety in MS-275 interacts with the catalytic zinc ion within the active site of HDACs; however, due to its non-hydroxamate nature, the mode of coordination involves a bidentate interaction primarily via the amino group with additional, albeit weaker, engagement through the carbonyl oxygen (Hofny et al., n.d., pp. 7–11). This selective inhibition results in the accumulation of acetylated histones H3 and H4, thereby maintaining a relaxed chromatin state that is permissive for transcription. The proposed inflammatory stress in RPE cells—induced by tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)—is thought to lead to the recruitment of HDAC1/2 to promoters of tight junction genes such as TJP1, OCLN, and CLDN19, effectively silencing their expression through histone deacetylation (Hanikoglu et al., 2018, pp. 2–3; Connolly et al., 2017, pp. 8–10). By inhibiting HDAC1/2, MS-275 is expected to restore global histone acetylation levels, thereby reactivating transcription of these tight junction genes. The resulting upregulation and stabilization of proteins such as zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and occludin are hypothesized to reconstruct intercellular junctions and improve transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER), which is a measure of barrier integrity (Hofny et al., n.d., pp. 7–9; San José-Enériz et al., 2019, pp. 8–10). This mechanism is supported by extensive biochemical studies in cancer models, where similar epigenetic reprogramming has led to reactivation of silenced genes, although direct data in ocular models remains to be generated (Rossetti, 2023, pp. 31–34; Hofny et al., n.d., pp. 9–11).

Expected Effect in the Proposed Assay:  
In the experimental model using ARPE-19 cells subjected to TNF-α stress, the central hypothesis is that treatment with 0.5 µM MS-275 will be sufficient to inhibit HDAC1/2 activity and restore histone acetylation at tight junction gene promoters. This, in turn, should lead to a reactivation of transcription for TJP1 (encoding ZO-1), OCLN (encoding occludin), and CLDN19 (encoding claudin-19), resulting in an increased synthesis of these key proteins. As tight junction proteins come back online, the structural integrity of the RPE monolayer is expected to improve, which will be reflected by a significant increase in TEER measurements—quantitatively, it is predicted that junction protein levels might be restored by at least twofold, corresponding to a recovery of barrier function (Zhang et al., 2015, pp. 2–3; Ruiz et al., 2015, pp. 2–3). ARPE-19 cells are an established model for studying RPE barrier function and are known to express these tight junction components; thus, the assay is well poised to test whether the epigenetic modulation by MS-275 can counteract the deleterious effects of TNF-α (Hofny et al., n.d., pp. 17–18; San José-Enériz et al., 2019, pp. 8–10). Moreover, the selective profile of MS-275 against HDAC1/2 is critical because these isoforms have been directly implicated in the repression of gene transcription in inflammatory contexts (Connolly et al., 2017, pp. 1–2; Hofny et al., n.d., pp. 7–9). Consequently, a restored acetylation pattern is expected to facilitate the re-expression of the tight junction gene set and consequently normalize TEER, which is a proxy for the functional integrity of the RPE barrier (Hanikoglu et al., 2018, pp. 2–3; Connolly et al., 2017, pp. 8–10).

Overall Evaluation:  
The repurposing hypothesis for MS-275 in dry AMD is scientifically intriguing and grounded in a well-established mechanism of epigenetic modulation. One of the principal strengths of MS-275 is its robust clinical characterization in oncology, which provides a wealth of safety and pharmacokinetic information, albeit in cancer patients rather than in an ocular context (Knipstein & Gore, 2011, pp. 2–3; Connolly et al., 2017, pp. 1–2). Its selective inhibition of HDAC1 and HDAC2 is particularly attractive because these isoforms are known to be involved in the aberrant epigenetic silencing observed under conditions of inflammatory stress, and their inhibition has been shown to restore transcriptionally active chromatin states (Hofny et al., n.d., pp. 7–11). Additionally, the reversible nature of epigenetic modifications mediated by HDACs means that therapeutic intervention can potentially restore normal gene expression patterns without permanently altering the genomic landscape, an advantage that is particularly relevant in chronic diseases such as dry AMD (Rossetti, 2023, pp. 31–34; Knipstein & Gore, 2011, pp. 1–2).

However, there are notable challenges as well. Although MS-275 has a well-defined mechanism in cancer, there is a lack of direct preclinical data supporting its use in ocular diseases, and specifically in models of RPE tight junction disruption. This represents a significant gap in the literature that would need to be addressed through targeted studies in ocular cell lines and animal models. Furthermore, MS-275’s pharmacokinetic profile, which has been characterized in the context of systemic cancer therapy, may not directly translate to effective drug delivery or retention in ocular tissues. For instance, issues such as blood–retina barrier penetration, potential ocular toxicity, and the need for specialized formulation strategies (e.g., local delivery through intravitreal injection or topical administration) are important considerations that have not yet been systematically evaluated for this compound (Knipstein & Gore, 2011, pp. 2–3; Ruiz et al., 2015, pp. 1–2). In addition, the broad spectrum of gene reactivation induced by HDAC inhibition may carry the risk of off-target effects, and while the selective inhibition of HDAC1/2 is a strength in terms of targeted epigenetic modulation, chronic inhibition of these enzymes could potentially disrupt normal cellular functions in the retina if not carefully controlled (Hofny et al., n.d., pp. 7–11).

Another weakness is the variability inherent in the clinical data for MS-275 observed in oncology, where interpatient differences and dose-dependent toxicities (such as gastrointestinal, hematologic, and metabolic abnormalities) have been reported (Rossetti, 2023, pp. 31–34; Weber, 2015, pp. 33–34). Although these adverse effects were generally considered manageable in the cancer setting, the risk–benefit balance for treatment of a chronic, non-lethal condition like dry AMD would have to be carefully re-evaluated. It is also worth noting that while MS-275’s epigenetic mechanism is promising for restoring tight junction gene expression, the complex interplay of inflammatory cytokines and multiple HDAC isoforms in the RPE may require combinatorial approaches or more selective targeting strategies than those currently afforded by MS-275 (McCabe, 2019, pp. 23–27; Hanikoglu et al., 2018, pp. 2–3).

In conclusion, MS-275 (Entinostat) presents a compelling candidate for repurposing in dry AMD based on its established epigenetic mechanism, clinical profile in oncology, and the plausible hypothesis that HDAC1/2 inhibition in RPE cells under inflammatory stress can restore tight junction integrity. Its strengths lie in its synthetic origin, clear mechanism of increasing histone acetylation, and potential to reactivate genes suppressed by pro-inflammatory signaling. However, significant preclinical research is needed to validate its efficacy and safety in ocular models, to optimize its delivery to retinal tissues, and to ensure that its broad effects on gene expression do not result in adverse outcomes when used chronically in a non-oncologic context (Hofny et al., n.d., pp. 7–11; Rossetti, 2023, pp. 31–34; Zhang et al., 2015, pp. 2–3). Overall, while the candidate is promising, a cautious, step-wise approach involving detailed in vitro and in vivo studies in ocular systems is warranted before advancing to clinical trials for dry AMD (Connolly et al., 2017, pp. 1–2; San José-Enériz et al., 2019, pp. 8–10; Hofny et al., n.d., pp. 9–11).
