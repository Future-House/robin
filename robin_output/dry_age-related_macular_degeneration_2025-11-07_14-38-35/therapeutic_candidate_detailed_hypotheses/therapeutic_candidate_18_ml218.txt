Proposal for ML218 (Tocris 5056)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
ML218 is a synthetic small‐molecule calcium channel inhibitor originally developed as an antagonist targeting T‐type CaV3 channels. It was discovered in efforts to design selective modulators of low‐voltage‐activated calcium channels and is commercially available (Tocris 5056). Its chemical structure – featuring a 3,5‐dichlorobenzamide moiety attached to an azabicyclic fragment – places it in the class of benzamide-derived T‐type channel blockers. The compound was designed to interact with hydrophobic fenestration sites in voltage‐gated calcium channels, and its development emerged from scaffold‐hopping strategies derived from earlier hits in high‐throughput calcium flux assays. ML218 thus belongs to a family of investigational agents that have been primarily used as pharmacological tools to dissect T‐type channel function in neuronal systems. Previous studies have focused on its ability to inhibit CaV3 channels in central nervous system models, including experiments in cell lines and primary neurons where it modulated firing patterns and synaptic excitability (Sun et al., 2025; Xiang et al., 2011). In addition to its intended activity on CaV3 channels, detailed analyses have revealed that ML218 also exhibits significant cross‐activity with CaV1.4 channels by adopting an alternative binding mode that involves the III‐IV fenestration—an observation that distinguishes it from classical dihydropyridines (Sun et al., 2025). This dual binding behavior highlights both the promise and the challenge of using ML218 as a selective tool in complex tissues where multiple calcium channel subtypes coexist. Overall, ML218 is a promising lead compound representing the synthetic, small‐molecule approach to targeting voltage‐dependent calcium channels, with a structure–activity relationship that suggests potential repurposing for non‐neuronal applications such as retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) barrier stabilization (Sun et al., 2025; Xiang et al., 2011).

Therapeutic History:  
ML218 has been primarily explored within experimental models predominantly focused on the central nervous system. Several studies have employed ML218 as a pharmacological probe to inhibit T‐type calcium currents in neuronal populations. For example, in rodent models of Parkinson’s disease, ML218 demonstrated robust inhibition of T-type currents in subthalamic nucleus (STN) neurons, leading to marked alterations in burst firing patterns (Xiang et al., 2011; Manzar et al., 2025). Electrophysiological investigations using whole‐cell patch clamp techniques in HEK293 cells transfected with CaV3.2 channels have shown that ML218 can achieve potent block, with reported IC₅₀ values ranging from approximately 24 nM (in one report) to 150–310 nM in different assay settings (Sun et al., 2025; Xiang et al., 2011). These preclinical studies validated ML218’s mechanism of action within neural substrates and its potential as a centrally active agent. In parallel, investigations in non‐neuronal cells have emerged. In particular, ML218 has been applied to studies involving human embryonic stem cell‐derived retinal pigment epithelium (hESC‐RPE) where it was used at a concentration of 5 μM to elucidate the functionality of voltage‐gated calcium channels expressed in these cells—primarily focusing on assays of phagocytosis and VEGF secretion (Korkka et al., 2019; Korkka, n.d.). Although these studies did not specifically examine barrier function parameters, they established that RPE cells express functional T‐type calcium channels and that pharmacological modulation with ML218 could alter cellular processes relevant to retinal physiology. Notably, there is no recorded or clinical history of ML218 being employed to treat dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) or related ocular diseases; its usage to date has centered on neurological models and in vitro assays. Consequently, repurposing ML218 for dry AMD would represent a novel application, extending its utilization from central nervous system studies to ocular tissue. The concept of drug repurposing in ocular diseases has been gaining traction in recent years due to the overlapping roles of calcium signaling in diverse tissues, and ML218’s history in rigorously characterizing calcium channel pharmacology makes it a candidate for further exploration in RPE models (Xiang et al., 2011; Korkka et al., 2019).

Mechanism of Action:  
ML218 functions primarily by blocking T‐type calcium channels, with an emphasis on CaV3.2 subtypes. The compound’s mechanism involves its entry into the transmembrane fenestrations of the channel complex, where it interrupts the normal ion‐conducting pathway. Detailed structural studies have revealed that in CaV3 channels, ML218 docks within the II‐III fenestration; in this binding mode, the phenyl head group of the molecule extends into the hydrophobic channel region while its aliphatic tail lies within the central pore, thereby imposing a state‐dependent block (Sun et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2024). This interaction presumably stabilizes the channel in a closed state, reducing low‐voltage‐activated Ca²⁺ influx into the cell. Notably, ML218’s binding is not entirely exclusive to CaV3 channels, as its molecular conformation adapts in CaV1.4 channels. In these channels, non‐conserved residues preclude the canonical II‐III fenestration binding; instead, ML218 binds at a distinct site in the III‐IV fenestration that overlaps with the dihydropyridine (DHP) binding pocket. Mutagenesis studies have confirmed the importance of this alternate binding mode by demonstrating substantial changes in inhibitory potency upon modification of key residues such as T1007 (Sun et al., 2025). In electrophysiological assays carried out in HEK293 cells, ML218 has been shown to be highly potent against CaV3.2 channels (with reported IC₅₀ values as low as 24 nM in certain studies) and to display over 80-fold selectivity over CaV1.4 channels, although its cross‐reactivity remains a consideration at higher concentrations (Sun et al., 2025). The molecular basis for its selectivity lies in the differential organization of channel domains between CaV3 and CaV1 families, with ML218’s binding being modulated by specific residues located in the channel pore and fenestration regions. From a biochemical standpoint, the blockade of CaV3.2 channels results in a decreased calcium influx during membrane depolarizations, which is critical for controlling intracellular calcium levels. In tissues such as the RPE, where calcium signaling plays a pivotal role in maintaining cellular homeostasis and barrier integrity, aberrant Ca²⁺ influx can activate calcium‐dependent proteases such as calpains. Calpains, once activated, can cleave junctional proteins including zonula occludens protein 1 (ZO-1) and occludin, leading to compromised barrier integrity. Thus, by selectively inhibiting CaV3.2‐mediated calcium entry, ML218 is hypothesized to prevent calpain activation and subsequent disruption of tight junctions in RPE cells—a mechanism that has been indirectly supported by studies showing that inhibitors of T-type channels reduce cellular excitability and junctional leak in alternative epithelial models (Sun et al., 2025). Furthermore, the compound’s dual action—including a modest agonist effect at specific concentrations—adds complexity to its pharmacological profile; however, in the context of RPE barrier function, the primary desired effect would be its antagonist action that limits pathological calcium entry under oxidative or inflammatory stress. The state‐dependence of ML218’s block (i.e., preferential inhibition when channels are in an open or inactivated state) may further enhance its capacity to target pathological calcium influx while sparing normal physiological signaling to some extent, although this property will require careful titration in a diseased state (Sun et al., 2025).

Expected Effect:  
The working hypothesis for repurposing ML218 in dry AMD is that its inhibition of CaV3.2 channels in retinal pigment epithelium will reduce pathological intracellular calcium accumulation under conditions of oxidative and inflammatory stress. Elevated Ca²⁺ levels in RPE cells are known to activate calpain, a calcium‐dependent protease that mediates the proteolytic cleavage of critical junctional proteins such as ZO-1 and occludin. Disruption of these proteins leads to a compromised RPE barrier, as reflected in reduced transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) and increased paracellular permeability (measured by FITC-dextran flux assays). By blocking the CaV3.2 channels, ML218 is expected to limit this pathological Ca²⁺ influx and thereby prevent calpain activation, preserving the integrity of the ZO-1/occludin belt in RPE cells (Sun et al., 2025). This preservation of junctional complexes should translate into restored TEER and reduced permeability of the RPE monolayer in in vitro models simulating oxidative or inflammatory injury that mimic conditions encountered in dry AMD. Moreover, patch-clamp electrophysiology and Fluo-4 calcium imaging assays in RPE cells are proposed as means of confirming target engagement and quantifying the extent of CaV3.2 current inhibition by ML218 (Korkka et al., 2019; Korkka, n.d.). It is anticipated that effective blockade of pathological calcium signaling will also reduce downstream events associated with dry AMD pathophysiology, such as aberrant vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) secretion, which contributes to the progression of atrophic changes in the retina. Although previous studies using ML218 in hESC-RPE have focused more on phagocytosis and secretory functions rather than barrier integrity per se, the presence of functional T-type channels identified in these cells (with CaV3.2 localized, for instance, to the basolateral domain) supports the rationale that modulation of these channels will have a direct impact on barrier properties (Korkka et al., 2019; Korkka et al., 2019).

Overall Evaluation:  
In evaluating ML218 as a therapeutic candidate for dry age‐related macular degeneration, several strengths and weaknesses emerge from the current literature. One significant strength of ML218 is its well‐documented and robust activity in inhibiting T‐type calcium channels, particularly CaV3.2, as demonstrated by comprehensive electrophysiological studies in both neuronal and heterologous systems (Sun et al., 2025; Xiang et al., 2011). This potent inhibition forms a logical mechanistic basis for targeting pathological calcium influx in RPE cells, where calcium overload and subsequent activation of proteases like calpain are implicated in junctional protein degradation and barrier dysfunction. Furthermore, the detailed structural insights into ML218’s binding—its differential interactions in CaV3 and CaV1 channels—provide a strong foundation for understanding its pharmacodynamic activity. The ability of ML218 to modulate calcium signaling with state‐dependent block enhances its therapeutic promise by potentially allowing it to target pathological overactivity while sparing baseline physiological function (Sun et al., 2025).  

On the other hand, several potential weaknesses must be considered. The literature reveals that ML218 is not exclusively selective for CaV3.2 channels; it exhibits cross‐reactivity with CaV1.4 channels, albeit at lower potency (with reported IC₅₀ values for CaV1.4 in the micromolar range compared to nanomolar ranges for CaV3.2) (Sun et al., 2025). Such cross‐reactivity could theoretically lead to off‐target effects in tissues where L‐type channels play critical roles in calcium signaling, potentially complicating its utility in RPE cells that also express other calcium channel subtypes. Furthermore, while the proposed hypothesis for dry AMD is biologically plausible, there is a notable gap in the literature regarding direct evidence for ML218’s efficacy in preserving RPE barrier function under oxidative or inflammatory conditions—most studies with ML218 have been performed in neuronal contexts or in assays focused on phagocytosis and secretory aspects of RPE physiology (Korkka et al., 2019; Korkka, n.d.). Thus, extrapolation of its barrier‐stabilizing properties from other epithelial systems (or even from neural models) requires cautious interpretation and further dedicated experimentation.  

Another concern pertains to the reported potency of ML218. Although optimistic hypotheses suggest an IC₅₀ of approximately 7 nM for CaV3.2 channel block, the available data across various assay platforms indicate that effective concentrations are more likely to be in the tens to low hundreds of nanomolar range (Sun et al., 2025; Xiang et al., 2011). This discrepancy necessitates further pharmacological profiling in RPE models to accurately define the therapeutic window and ensure that the concentrations used to restore barrier integrity do not inadvertently trigger off‐target effects or toxicity. Additionally, the dual binding mode of ML218—while advantageous for providing mechanistic insight—raises the possibility that subtle differences in channel conformations within RPE cells (compared to neurons) could influence its selectivity and efficacy, thereby complicating dose optimization for ocular applications.  

Despite these challenges, the conceptual framework for repurposing ML218 in dry AMD has several attractive features. The targeting of T‐type calcium channels to mitigate calpain activation and to preserve tight junction protein integrity represents a novel therapeutic approach that departs from traditional anti‐inflammatory or anti‐angiogenic strategies. In dry AMD, where chronic, low‐grade oxidative and inflammatory stress contribute to RPE dysfunction and barrier breakdown, a compound like ML218 could theoretically reduce intracellular calcium overload and, consequently, the cascade of events leading to junctional degradation. Preliminary evidence from hESC-RPE studies confirms that these cells express functional T-type channels and respond to modulation by calcium channel blockers, lending support to the notion that ML218 can be effective in this tissue context (Korkka et al., 2019; Korkka, n.d.).

In summary, ML218 emerges as an intriguing candidate for repurposing in dry AMD due to its potent T-type channel inhibitory activity, its well‐characterized mechanism at the molecular level, and the theoretical basis linking Ca²⁺ dysregulation to RPE barrier dysfunction. However, the current body of literature does not provide direct evidence in RPE models under conditions that mimic dry AMD pathology, such as oxidative or inflammatory injury. Moreover, the compound’s off‐target activity on CaV1.4 channels and the variability in reported potencies across studies pose significant challenges that will require thorough in vitro and eventually in vivo validation. Future investigations should prioritize comprehensive electrophysiological and imaging studies in RPE cells, specifically measuring TEER, FITC‐dextran flux, and tight junction protein localization following exposure to oxidative stress, to determine whether ML218 can indeed restore barrier integrity as hypothesized. Dose‐ranging studies and careful evaluation of potential off‐target effects will also be critical to establishing a safe and effective therapeutic profile for ocular application.  

Overall, while ML218 holds promise as a tool compound with the potential for repurposing in dry AMD, its advancement into this new therapeutic area will depend on addressing key knowledge gaps through rigorous experimental validation. This includes confirming its selective inhibition of CaV3.2 channels in RPE cells, establishing the correlation between calcium influx blockade and tight junction preservation, and ensuring that its pharmacological behavior in RPE is consistent with the requirements for stabilizing the RPE barrier under pathological conditions. Therefore, although the mechanistic rationale is compelling, additional targeted preclinical studies are required before ML218 can be confidently moved forward as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD (Sun et al., 2025; Xiang et al., 2011; Korkka et al., 2019).
