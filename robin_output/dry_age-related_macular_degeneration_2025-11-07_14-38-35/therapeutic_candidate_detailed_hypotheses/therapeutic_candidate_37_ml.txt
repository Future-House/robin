Proposal for ML-SA1 (Tocris 5291)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
ML‐SA1 (Tocris 5291) is a synthetic, small‐molecule agonist that targets the lysosomal cation channel TRPML1 (mucolipin‐1). It was originally identified through screening efforts aimed at discovering modulators of lysosomal Ca²⁺ release and has been instrumental as an in vitro pharmacologic tool to probe TRPML1 function in diverse cellular systems, including retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) models. ML‐SA1 belongs to a broader class of TRP (transient receptor potential) channel modulators that have been used extensively in basic research to examine ion channel regulation of intracellular Ca²⁺ stores, autophagy regulation, and lysosomal exocytosis. In several studies using primary mouse RPE, human embryonic stem cell‐derived RPE (hesRPE), and ARPE‐19 cells, ML‐SA1 has been shown to robustly elevate cytoplasmic Ca²⁺ via direct activation of TRPML1, with the effect being rapid, reversible, and reproducible, thereby establishing its utility as a selective TRPML1 agonist (Más Gómez et al., 2018).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, ML‐SA1 has been employed in multiple biochemical and cellular studies as a probe to activate TRPML1-mediated lysosomal Ca²⁺ release. In the literature, its application in RPE cell models has helped delineate the contributions of lysosomal Ca²⁺ signals to downstream processes such as lysosomal exocytosis, TFEB (transcription factor EB) activation, and oxidative stress regulation. Experimental evidence derived from studies using ML‐SA1 demonstrates that the induced Ca²⁺ release is essentially independent of extracellular Ca²⁺ and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) Ca²⁺ storage; rather, the release originates from lysosomal stores—as confirmed by the abrogation of ML‐SA1 responses upon lysosomal disruption with glycyl‐L‐phenylalanine‐naphthylamide (GPN) and by the lack of response in TRPML1-null RPE models (Más Gómez et al., 2018). To date, ML‐SA1 has not been used clinically or in veterinary settings and has not yet been evaluated in the context of dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) or other retinal degenerative diseases; its use is so far confined to experimental paradigms that study the fundamental aspects of TRPML1 activity and lysosomal function (Más Gómez et al., 2018). This candidate is thus considered a repurposed pharmacologic agent, repositioned from a research tool to a potential therapeutic based on its mechanistic actions in RPE cells.

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, ML‐SA1 exerts its action by binding to and directly activating TRPML1 channels located on the lysosomal membrane. Upon binding, it triggers an efflux of Ca²⁺ from the lysosomal lumen into the cytoplasm, which has been quantitatively measured using ratiometric Ca²⁺ indicators such as Fura-2 (Más Gómez et al., 2018). This Ca²⁺ release is independent of extracellular Ca²⁺ and is not affected by the depletion of ER Ca²⁺, indicating that the source is specifically lysosomal (Más Gómez et al., 2018). The released Ca²⁺ can then activate downstream signaling pathways including the calcineurin-TFEB pathway, where increased cytosolic Ca²⁺ activates calcineurin, leading to dephosphorylation and subsequent nuclear translocation of TFEB. Nuclear TFEB acts as a master regulator of autophagy and lysosomal biogenesis by upregulating a network of genes that control these pathways (Más Gómez et al., 2018). Notably, experimental evidence indicates that when the function of TRPML1 is compromised—such as in the context of lipid accumulation in lysosomes or genetic knockout of TRPML1—the Ca²⁺ response to ML‐SA1 is significantly diminished, underscoring both the specificity of ML‐SA1 for TRPML1 and the modulation of its efficacy by the lysosomal lipid environment (Más Gómez et al., 2018). In addition to stimulating Ca²⁺ release, TRPML1 activation by ML‐SA1 has been linked to beneficial downstream effects such as the induction of lysosomal exocytosis and the enhancement of cathepsin activity, which together contribute to the restoration of lysosomal function and cellular homeostasis (Karema-Jokinen et al., 2023; Más Gómez et al., 2018).

Expected Effect:  
Based on the current hypothesis, ML‐SA1 is expected to act on RPE cells by enhancing lysosomal Ca²⁺ release through TRPML1 activation, thereby stimulating the calcineurin-TFEB signaling cascade. This cascade is anticipated to upregulate the transcription of autophagy and lysosome-related genes, leading to improved autophagic flux and lysosomal biogenesis. In practical assays, such as those employing lipofuscin-loaded RPE monolayers, ML‐SA1 should facilitate the clearance of misfolded or aggregated tight junction proteins—including ZO-1—thereby restoring disrupted tight junction belts and improving transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurements. The activation of cathepsins, as part of the enhanced lysosomal function, is also expected to increase the degradation of accumulated cellular debris, further supporting cellular clearance (Falcão et al., 2025; Más Gómez et al., 2018). In experimental models of epithelial barrier restoration, previous data using TRPML1 agonists have shown that such compounds can rescue autophagic flux and reduce lysosomal storage, which is associated with improved barrier integrity; this supports the mechanism by which ML‐SA1 is expected to normalize the function of RPE cells relevant to dry AMD (Más Gómez et al., 2018). The candidate’s reported effective concentration (EC₅₀ around 45 µM for MCOLN1/TRPML1 activation) further underlines its potential to achieve a pharmacologically relevant activation of the TRPML1 channel in RPE cells, thereby triggering the downstream beneficial effects (Más Gómez et al., 2018).

Overall Evaluation:  
ML‐SA1 represents a mechanistically innovative therapeutic candidate with a well‐defined mode of action that directly targets lysosomal dysfunction—a key pathological feature in dry AMD. One of the major strengths of ML‐SA1 is its ability to rapidly induce lysosomal Ca²⁺ release by specifically activating TRPML1, which has been documented robustly in various RPE cell models (Más Gómez et al., 2018). This lysosomal Ca²⁺ signaling is crucial for the activation of the calcineurin-TFEB pathway, which in turn upregulates lysosomal and autophagy gene networks; such activation is essential for clearing cellular debris, misfolded proteins, and aggregated tight junction components that compromise the integrity of the RPE barrier (Más Gómez et al., 2018). The anticipated effect of restoring TEER in lipofuscin-loaded monolayers is particularly important because impaired barrier function is a hallmark of RPE dysfunction in dry AMD (Falcão et al., 2025). Moreover, the potential to enhance cathepsin activity and thereby boost lysosomal degradation pathways positions ML‐SA1 as a promising candidate to counteract the autophagy deficits that are observed in diseased RPE cells.

However, there are notable limitations and challenges that must be addressed. First, while the mechanistic data are compelling, ML‐SA1’s efficacy appears to be significantly modulated by the lysosomal lipid environment; in conditions where lipofuscin or other lipid accumulations are prominent, the Ca²⁺ response to ML‐SA1 has been shown to be reduced, suggesting that its beneficial effects might be attenuated in advanced dry AMD where lipid accumulation is extensive (Más Gómez et al., 2018). Second, despite its robust in vitro activity in various RPE models, ML‐SA1 has not yet been advanced into clinical or in vivo models of dry AMD, and thus there is a lack of safety, toxicity, and pharmacokinetics data relevant to chronic administration in the context of retinal diseases (Más Gómez et al., 2018). Additionally, although ML‐SA1 is reported to have an EC₅₀ around 45 µM for TRPML1 activation, the dosing window and potential off-target effects remain to be thoroughly characterized, particularly with respect to a multifactorial disease such as dry AMD that involves chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and photoreceptor degeneration (Más Gómez et al., 2018).

From a drug development perspective, ML‐SA1’s novel lysosomal-targeted mechanism offers significant promise by addressing a critical aspect of RPE dysfunction through the restoration of lysosomal Ca²⁺ signaling and autophagy. Its ability to potentially clear misfolded tight junction proteins and recover ZO-1 belts, thereby restoring TEER, could translate into improved barrier integrity—a key determinant of RPE health in the context of dry AMD (Más Gómez et al., 2018).

In conclusion, while ML‐SA1 is a promising candidate for repurposing as a therapy for dry AMD, the current state of evidence is largely preclinical and mechanistic. The compound’s ability to activate TRPML1 and thereby enhance lysosomal Ca²⁺ release—coupled with its downstream activation of calcineurin-TFEB signaling and the potential to restore autophagy and barrier function in RPE—positions it as a strong candidate for further investigation. However, significant challenges remain in translating these in vitro findings into effective in vivo therapies. Future studies should focus on addressing the impact of lysosomal lipid accumulation on ML‐SA1 efficacy, determining the long-term safety and pharmacokinetic profiles in animal models of dry AMD, and establishing whether the observed cellular benefits translate into functional improvements in retinal health and vision preservation. Overall, ML‐SA1’s lysosomal-targeted mechanism and its demonstrated capacity to rescue key RPE functions provide a compelling rationale to pursue this candidate further, albeit with the caveat that extensive preclinical validation is required before clinical development can be contemplated (Falcão et al., 2025; Más Gómez et al., 2018).
