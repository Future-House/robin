Proposal for Navitoclax (ABT-263; Selleck S1001)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Navitoclax (ABT-263) is a small‐molecule, orally bioavailable agent originally developed in the oncology arena as a BH3‐mimetic that targets several anti‐apoptotic members of the BCL-2 family, most notably BCL-2, BCL-xL, and BCL-W. This synthetic compound was designed through structure–activity relationship optimization to overcome the limitations of earlier compounds such as ABT-737, and it has since been classified as a senolytic agent owing to its ability to selectively induce apoptosis in senescent cells while sparing normal, quiescent cells. In the context of aging and degenerative diseases, the protective yet dysfunctional senescent cell phenotype is characterized by the upregulation of anti‐apoptotic proteins; therefore, compounds like ABT-263 that block these survival pathways have been repurposed as candidates to eliminate such senescent cells. The compound belongs to a broader class of therapeutics targeting the BCL-2 family, which have historically been exploited in various anti-cancer strategies and are now emerging as promising candidates for addressing age-associated disorders such as dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) (Ryu et al., 2023).

Therapeutic History:  
Navitoclax has a well-documented preclinical and clinical history in oncological models; its senolytic potential was first noted in studies where it selectively killed therapy-induced senescent tumor cells and other cell types by inhibiting anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins. Early clinical trials in small-cell lung cancer and lymphoid malignancies established proof-of-concept that targeting BCL-2/BCL-xL could result in tumor regression through apoptotic induction, although its clinical use was somewhat limited by dose-dependent toxicities such as thrombocytopenia. More recently, preclinical studies have extended its use beyond oncology: in in vitro studies and in mouse models where retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells were rendered senescent by doxorubicin treatment, navitoclax was shown to selectively induce apoptosis in the senescent cell population while largely sparing non-senescent RPE, as evidenced by decreased markers of senescence (p16, p21) and increased apoptotic markers such as cleaved caspases. Additionally, these studies demonstrated that oral administration of ABT-263 in mice resulted in reduced senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining and improved structural and functional retinal outcomes, including increased outer nuclear layer thickness and recovery of electroretinographic responses, which are highly encouraging for its repurposing in dry AMD (Ryu et al., 2023). Despite these promising preclinical findings, navitoclax has not yet been tested in clinical trials for dry AMD or similar retinal degenerative diseases, and its history in ocular conditions remains limited to proof-of-principle studies in animal models (Ryu et al., 2023; Khan et al., 2023).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, navitoclax exerts its senolytic action by competitively binding to the hydrophobic groove of anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins, particularly BCL-2 and BCL-xL, thereby displacing pro-apoptotic proteins such as BAX and BIM. Under normal conditions, senescent cells upregulate these anti-apoptotic proteins as part of a survival mechanism against various stressors; the binding of navitoclax disrupts these protective interactions, leading to the activation of the intrinsic apoptotic cascade. This biochemical disruption sets off a cascade of events culminating in mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, cytochrome c release, and the subsequent activation of caspases (notably caspase-9 and caspase-3), which orchestrate programmed cell death. Experimentally, cells treated with navitoclax have demonstrated an increased BAX/BCL-2 ratio along with elevated levels of cleaved caspases as measured by immunoblots, paralleling the reduction of canonical senescence markers such as p16 and p21. In the context of retinal degeneration, senescent RPE cells are known to secrete a deleterious senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP)—including proinflammatory cytokines like IL-6 and IL-8 and matrix metalloproteases such as MMP2 and MMP9—that can undermine the integrity of junctional proteins such as ZO-1, leading to increased paracellular permeability. By eliminating these p16^Ink4a-positive senescent cells, navitoclax is hypothesized to reduce the inflammatory burden on the tissue microenvironment, thereby promoting the repopulation of healthy RPE cells and the restoration of tight junction continuity (Ryu et al., 2023).

Expected Effect:  
The central hypothesis driving the repurposing of navitoclax for dry AMD is that the senolytic removal of p16^Ink4a-positive, senescent RPE cells will lead to a diminution of the SASP and its associated cytokines, such as IL-6 and IL-8, which are known to contribute to the degradation of tight junction proteins and compromise RPE barrier function. Experimental reports on navitoclax in senescent RPE models have indicated that treatment with ABT-263 not only leads to a reduction in senescence markers and SASP factor expression but also is accompanied by increased markers of cellular proliferation (e.g., Ki67) and partial recovery of structural features, such as an approximately 10% increase in outer nuclear layer (ONL) thickness in mouse models. While direct measurements of transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) and quantification of ZO-1 localization remain limited in these studies, immunofluorescence data do suggest improvements in junctional integrity following treatment with navitoclax. Thus, the expected biological effect is that clearance of senescent RPE will result in reduced paracrine inflammatory signaling, the reestablishment of stable ZO-1 belts, and overall restoration of the barrier properties of the RPE layer—a critical factor in the progression of dry AMD (Ryu et al., 2023).

Overall Evaluation:  
Navitoclax represents a promising therapeutic candidate as a repurposed senolytic agent for the treatment of dry AMD based on a convergence of preclinical biochemical, cellular, and animal model data. One of its primary strengths is its clear mechanism of action; by targeting a well-characterized survival pathway that is upregulated in senescent cells, navitoclax offers a rational strategy for the selective removal of dysfunctional p16^Ink4a-positive RPE cells. The preclinical evidence suggests that this mechanism translates into meaningful improvements in retinal structure and function, including reductions in senescence-associated markers and partial restoration of indicators of cellular proliferation and tissue integrity (Ryu et al., 2023).

However, there are notable weaknesses and challenges that must be addressed before navitoclax can be considered a viable treatment for dry AMD. Foremost among these is the concern regarding its safety profile. BCL-xL inhibition is known to cause dose-limiting thrombocytopenia and neutropenia in systemic applications, as evidenced by cancer trial data. In an elderly population that typically comprises dry AMD patients, where comorbidities and polypharmacy are frequent, these hematologic toxicities could limit the clinical utility of navitoclax unless strategies are devised to minimize systemic exposure or target delivery specifically to the eye. While local delivery (for example, via intravitreal injection) might mitigate some systemic risks, the optimal dosing regimen and formulation remain to be determined (Ryu et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2025).

Another significant limitation is the current gap in direct evidence for the selective clearance of p16^Ink4a-positive RPE cells in models that accurately recapitulate the pathophysiology of human dry AMD. Many of the preclinical studies have used doxorubicin-induced senescence models, which, although useful as proof-of-principle systems, may not fully capture the chronic, age-associated accumulation of senescent RPE cells observed in patients with dry AMD. Moreover, while improvements in junctional protein distribution—such as ZO-1 localization—have been reported, there are no robust quantitative data on TEER or direct measurements of paracellular permeability that would definitively demonstrate restoration of barrier function. These issues underscore the need for more refined preclinical assays that not only assess the removal of senescent cells but also rigorously quantify the re-establishment of functional barrier properties in the RPE (Ryu et al., 2023).

Furthermore, the repurposing strategy is based on the assumption that SASP-mediated disruption of tight junctions is a primary driver of barrier dysfunction in dry AMD. While inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-8 have been implicated in the degradation of junctional proteins, the complex interplay between the SASP, local immune responses, and other degenerative processes in dry AMD remains incompletely understood. Thus, while the hypothesis that senolytic clearance could reduce SASP levels and facilitate barrier recovery is compelling, it will be critical to validate these effects in models that integrate the multifactorial aspects of AMD pathogenesis (da Silva, 2021; Terao et al., 2022).

In summary, navitoclax has significant strengths as a candidate for senolytic therapy in dry AMD, notably its well-characterized mode of action and demonstrated efficacy in preclinical models that show improved retinal structure and function following the clearance of senescent cells. However, challenges such as its known systemic hematologic toxicities, the need for more disease-relevant preclinical models, and the lack of direct functional assays of RPE barrier restoration remain hurdles that need to be overcome before progressing to clinical trials. Addressing these issues may involve the development of targeted delivery systems, dose optimization studies, and rigorous functional assays that can collectively establish whether the removal of p16^Ink4a-positive senescent cells via navitoclax leads to durable improvements in RPE barrier integrity and, consequently, visual function in patients with dry AMD (Ryu et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2025; Al-Naggar et al., 2020).

Critical next steps would include:  
1. Developing or validating animal models that more accurately recapitulate the chronic senescence observed in dry AMD;  
2. Refining delivery strategies to minimize systemic exposure and associated toxicities while maximizing ocular bioavailability;  
3. Implementing rigorous, quantitative assays (such as TEER measurements and detailed immunolocalization of tight junction proteins) to confirm the restoration of barrier function following senolytic treatment; and  
4. Carefully assessing the long-term outcomes of senolytic therapy on retinal structure, immune modulation, and visual performance in aged subjects (Ryu et al., 2023).

The current body of evidence lends credence to the hypothesis that navitoclax-induced senolysis in RPE could alleviate key pathological mechanisms underlying dry AMD, including SASP-mediated junctional disruption, while promoting the repopulation of healthier RPE cells (Ryu et al., 2023; Sreekumar et al., 2020). Given the strengths in its molecular targeting and the encouraging preclinical retinal data, navitoclax remains a promising candidate in the portfolio for dry AMD drug development. However, its ultimate success will hinge on overcoming the noted challenges through comprehensive preclinical validation and strategic clinical study design. Continued investigation into the precise role of p16^Ink4a-positive RPE cells in AMD, together with detailed pharmacodynamic studies of navitoclax within the ocular microenvironment, will be essential to determine whether this agent can truly fulfill the promise of restoring RPE barrier function and, by extension, visual acuity in patients suffering from dry AMD (Ryu et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2025; Carpenter et al., 2021).
