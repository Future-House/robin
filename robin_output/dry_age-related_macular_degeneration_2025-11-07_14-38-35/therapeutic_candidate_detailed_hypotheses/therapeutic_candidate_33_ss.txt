Proposal for SS-31 (Elamipretide; Selleckchem S8185)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
SS-31, also known as Elamipretide, is a synthetic, cell‐permeable tetrapeptide that was originally discovered serendipitously during efforts to optimize opioid receptor peptide analogs and has since been rationally designed to target mitochondria by binding the lipid cardiolipin in the inner mitochondrial membrane (Sabbah et al., 2025, pp. 1–3). Its chemical structure, typically denoted as D-Arg-Dmt-Lys-Phe-NH₂—with “Dmt” representing 2,6-dimethyltyrosine—confers a unique alternating pattern of cationic and aromatic residues that endows the compound with high membrane permeability and preferential accumulation in the mitochondrial inner membrane (Tung et al., 2025, pp. 2–5). The molecular weight of SS-31 is approximately 639.8 g/mol, and its synthesis is generally performed via solid-phase peptide synthesis, which allows for efficient production and structural modification; this synthetic origin situates SS-31 within the class of mitochondria-targeting peptides or “stealth peptides” that have been extensively studied for their bioenergetic and cytoprotective properties (Tung et al., 2025, pp. 1–2). In addition to its capacity to target cardiolipin, the compound’s design leverages both electrostatic attraction—owing to its positively charged arginine and lysine residues—and hydrophobic interactions from its aromatic side chains to facilitate a stable binding to the anionic membranes of mitochondria (Sabbah et al., 2025, pp. 1–3; Tung et al., 2025, pp. 1–2). As a member of a broader therapeutic class initially developed to improve mitochondrial function, SS-31 has been applied across various models of mitochondrial dysfunction, making it a promising repurposed candidate for diseases where mitochondrial impairment is a key pathological driver, such as dry age-related macular degeneration (Sabbah et al., 2025, pp. 1–3; Lenin et al., 2023, pp. 11–13).

Therapeutic History:  
The therapeutic history of SS-31 is extensive and spans several preclinical and early-phase clinical investigations across a range of indications characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction. Preclinical studies across more than 150 peer-reviewed publications have demonstrated that SS-31 exerts robust mitochondrial protection in models of cardiac and skeletal muscle myopathies, where it improves mitochondrial structure, enhances ATP production, reduces reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and ultimately ameliorates organ dysfunction (Sabbah et al., 2025, pp. 1–3, 10–11). In the cardiovascular arena, animal models of heart failure and ischemia-reperfusion injury have documented that SS-31 treatment leads to improved left ventricular function and reduced tissue biomarkers of injury, effects that are attributed to its ability to stabilize cardiolipin and maintain the integrity of the electron transport system (Sabbah et al., 2025, pp. 8–10; Tung et al., 2025, pp. 10–12). Further, in neuromuscular and metabolic disease models, the compound has been shown to enhance mitochondrial ATP production and reduce oxidative stress, with early clinical studies in rare mitochondrial disorders such as Barth syndrome (a condition involving cardiolipin abnormalities) and primary mitochondrial myopathy providing supportive translational evidence (Sabbah et al., 2025, pp. 10–11, 16–22). More recently, SS-31 has been explored in the context of ocular diseases, including nonneovascular age-related macular degeneration (dry AMD) and diabetic macular edema, where preclinical studies in murine models report that subcutaneous administration of SS-31 can reverse electroretinographic deficits, facilitate the clearance of sub-retinal pigment epithelium (sub‐RPE) deposits, and ultimately reverse vision loss (Kim & Lad, 2021, pp. 4–5; Lenin et al., 2023, pp. 11–13). Clinical trials—such as the Phase I ReCLAIM study in intermediate AMD and the ongoing Phase II ReCLAIM-2 trial—have begun to assess SS-31’s safety profile, pharmacokinetics, and preliminary efficacy in preserving visual function, thereby positioning it as a promising candidate for repurposing in dry AMD (Kim & Lad, 2021, pp. 4–5; Ji et al., 2021, pp. 5–6). Although its use in dry AMD is relatively novel compared to its established preclinical record in other mitochondrial disorders, the existing scientific and regulatory trajectory supports its potential repurposing for diseases in which mitochondrial dysfunction is a central pathogenic mechanism (Nashine, 2021, pp. 9–11, 16).

Mechanism of Action:  
The mechanism by which SS-31 exerts its cytoprotective effects is centered on its highly specific interaction with cardiolipin, an anionic phospholipid unique to the mitochondrial inner membrane that plays a pivotal role in maintaining the structural integrity of cristae and the proper assembly and function of electron transport chain complexes (Sabbah et al., 2025, pp. 1–3; Tung et al., 2025, pp. 1–2). Detailed biophysical studies, including nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and molecular dynamics simulations, have demonstrated that SS-31 binds to cardiolipin via both electrostatic interactions (through its basic residues) and hydrophobic interactions (via its aromatic side chains), resulting in the “neutralization” of the negative surface charge on mitochondrial membranes and a favorable modulation of membrane fluidity and curvature (Mitchell et al., 2019, pp. 1–4; Sabbah et al., 2025, pp. 16–22). This binding not only stabilizes cardiolipin against oxidative peroxidation but also promotes the optimal assembly of electron transport chain complexes into respiratory supercomplexes, thereby enhancing electron flow, reducing electron leakage, and ultimately decreasing the generation of ROS (Sabbah et al., 2025, pp. 4–6, 15–16). In addition, SS-31 has been shown to mitigate proton leakage and restore pH stress resistance in aged mitochondria by enhancing the function of critical proteins such as the adenine nucleotide translocator (ANT), which is central to ADP/ATP exchange and overall mitochondrial bioenergetics (Tung et al., 2025, pp. 10–12). Although SS-31 was initially characterized as an antioxidant, its primary mode of action is now understood to be the preservation of mitochondrial structure and function through cardiolipin stabilization rather than direct radical scavenging alone (Sabbah et al., 2025, pp. 1–3; Mitchell et al., 2019, pp. 1–4). In the context of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) under oxidative stress—a key pathological hallmark of dry AMD—the hypothesis posits that by stabilizing mitochondria via cardiolipin binding, SS-31 will maintain normal electron transport chain efficiency, preserve ATP production, and limit ROS production, thereby protecting the integrity of RPE cells (Tong et al., 2022, pp. 14–16). Although direct experimental evidence linking SS-31 to the preservation of tight junction proteins such as ZO-1/occludin or to the restoration of transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) in RPE cells is limited in the current literature, the well-documented mitochondrial protection afforded by SS-31 in other cellular models suggests that secondary benefits on cellular barrier function are plausible given the tight coupling between mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and loss of epithelial integrity (Fisher, 2022, pp. 36–40; Ji et al., 2021, pp. 5–6). In sum, the molecular interactions of SS-31 with mitochondrial membranes serve not only to optimize respiratory chain efficiency and restore bioenergetic balance but also to reduce the biochemical milieu of oxidative stress that is known to compromise cell–cell junctions, thereby providing a strong mechanistic rationale for its application in protecting RPE barrier function in dry AMD (Sabbah et al., 2025, pp. 1–3, 15–22).

Expected Effect:  
Based on its molecular mechanism, the expected effect of SS-31 in an oxidative-challenge assay using RPE cells is multifaceted. In an experimental setting where RPE cells are exposed to oxidative stress—for example, through hydrogen peroxide treatment—the administration of SS-31 is anticipated to result in the selective binding to cardiolipin within RPE mitochondria; this interaction should stabilize inner mitochondrial membrane architecture, preserve cristae morphology, and enhance the efficiency of electron transport, leading to robust ATP production and reduced ROS levels (Sabbah et al., 2025, pp. 1–3; Tung et al., 2025, pp. 1–2). As mitochondrial homeostasis is a critical determinant of RPE cell function, the preservation of mitochondrial bioenergetics is expected to translate into decreased apoptosis and improved cell survival in the face of oxidative challenges (Sabbah et al., 2025, pp. 8–11). Beyond these direct mitochondrial effects, maintenance of intracellular energy status and lower levels of ROS are essential for the proper localization and preservation of tight junction proteins such as ZO-1 and occludin; oxidative stress is known to disrupt these junctions, leading to loss of transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) and compromised RPE barrier integrity (Ji et al., 2021, pp. 5–6; Fisher, 2022, pp. 36–40). Therefore, treatment with SS-31 should also help maintain or restore TEER in RPE monolayer assays, indicating a preservation of barrier function that is crucial for retinal homeostasis and prevention of drusen formation and RPE degeneration associated with dry AMD (Kim & Lad, 2021, pp. 4–5; Ji et al., 2021, pp. 5–6). Moreover, preclinical studies in animal models of retinal degeneration—in which SS-31 has been shown to reverse vision loss, improve clearance of sub-RPE deposits, and enhance electroretinographic responses—further support the expectation that SS-31 can exert protective effects in the ocular environment by mitigating mitochondrial dysfunction and the subsequent cascade of oxidative damage (Kim & Lad, 2021, pp. 4–5; Lenin et al., 2023, pp. 11–13). Taken together, the anticipated experimental outcome is that SS-31 will significantly attenuate oxidative damage in RPE cells, preserve mitochondrial function, maintain tight junction integrity (as evidenced by stable ZO-1/occludin expression and restored TEER), and ultimately contribute to the functional rescue of RPE cells under conditions that mimic the oxidative insults observed in dry AMD (Sabbah et al., 2025, pp. 1–3; Tong et al., 2022, pp. 14–16).

Overall Evaluation:  
Overall, SS-31 (Elamipretide) presents as a compelling therapeutic candidate for dry age-related macular degeneration, with several key strengths that support its potential utility and a few noteworthy challenges that will need to be addressed in further research. One of the primary strengths of SS-31 is its well-characterized mechanism of action; its ability to bind cardiolipin and thereby stabilize mitochondrial inner membrane structure, enhance electron transport chain efficiency, and reduce ROS generation is supported by robust preclinical data across multiple models of mitochondrial dysfunction, including cardiac, skeletal muscle, and retinal systems (Sabbah et al., 2025, pp. 1–3; Tung et al., 2025, pp. 1–2; Mitchell et al., 2019, pp. 1–4). This mitochondria-targeted mechanism is particularly attractive in the context of dry AMD, where mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress are recognized as major drivers of RPE degeneration and subsequent photoreceptor loss (Sabbah et al., 2025, pp. 1–3; Lenin et al., 2023, pp. 11–13). Furthermore, early-phase clinical studies in ocular disease have demonstrated that SS-31 is safe and well tolerated and have hinted at functional improvements in visual acuity and reduced progression of retinal degeneration, which supports its translational viability (Ji et al., 2021, pp. 5–6; Kim & Lad, 2021, pp. 4–5). The repurposing of SS-31 from conditions such as heart failure and primary mitochondrial myopathy to an ocular application leverages its established bioenergetic benefits and broad mitochondrial stabilization properties, thereby enhancing its appeal as a novel intervention for dry AMD (Sabbah et al., 2025, pp. 10–11, 16–22).

However, there are several challenges and weaknesses that merit consideration. First, while the molecular mechanism of SS-31—centered on cardiolipin binding and mitochondrial stabilization—is well supported, direct evidence linking its activity to the preservation of tight junction proteins (such as ZO-1/occludin) and the restoration of transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) in RPE models remains limited in the current literature (Ji et al., 2021, pp. 5–6; Fisher, 2022, pp. 36–40). This represents a key knowledge gap given that the hypothesis posits that protection of RPE barrier integrity is a critical outcome in preventing disease progression in dry AMD. Additional preclinical studies that specifically measure the effects of SS-31 on RPE tight junction organization, using immunostaining for ZO-1 and occludin as well as quantitative TEER assays, would be essential to fully validate its proposed mechanism in the context of epithelial barrier protection (Fisher, 2022, pp. 36–40; Ji et al., 2021, pp. 5–6). Second, although early-phase clinical trials have shown promise in improving functional visual metrics, some clinical studies in related indications (e.g., Phase 2 trials in cardiac conditions) have produced mixed results with primary endpoints not consistently met, suggesting that optimal dosing regimens, treatment durations, and patient selection criteria may need further refinement (Tung et al., 2025, pp. 8–10; Tung et al., 2025, pp. 9–10). In the case of dry AMD, where the pathological changes evolve over extended periods, longer-term studies and careful endpoint selection will be critical to demonstrate sustained therapeutic benefit (Kim & Lad, 2021, pp. 4–5; Ji et al., 2021, pp. 5–6). Third, while the pharmacokinetics and biodistribution of SS-31 appear favorable for reaching mitochondrial targets, ensuring sufficient ocular penetration and accumulation in the RPE—whether via systemic subcutaneous administration or topical formulations—remains a logistical and pharmacological challenge that must be addressed in future clinical development (Tong et al., 2022, pp. 14–16; Ji et al., 2021, pp. 5–6).

In conclusion, SS-31 (Elamipretide) exhibits a strong mechanistic underpinning based on its targeted interaction with cardiolipin to restore mitochondrial function and mitigate oxidative stress—a mode of action that is directly relevant to the pathogenesis of dry AMD owing to the central role of mitochondrial dysfunction in RPE degeneration (Sabbah et al., 2025, pp. 1–3; Tung et al., 2025, pp. 1–2). The compound’s extensive preclinical validation in models of mitochondrial diseases, coupled with encouraging early clinical data, makes it a promising repurposed drug candidate for dry AMD. Nonetheless, key gaps in directly demonstrating its effects on RPE barrier integrity and tight junction preservation (as measured by ZO-1/occludin levels and TEER) necessitate further targeted preclinical studies. Additionally, addressing challenges related to optimal dosing, treatment duration, and efficient ocular delivery will be paramount for the success of subsequent clinical trials. Overall, given its robust mitochondrial protective effects and plausible secondary benefits on epithelial barrier function, SS-31 warrants further investigation as a novel therapeutic strategy for dry age-related macular degeneration (Sabbah et al., 2025, pp. 1–3; Ji et al., 2021, pp. 5–6; Kim & Lad, 2021, pp. 4–5; Tung et al., 2025, pp. 10–12).
