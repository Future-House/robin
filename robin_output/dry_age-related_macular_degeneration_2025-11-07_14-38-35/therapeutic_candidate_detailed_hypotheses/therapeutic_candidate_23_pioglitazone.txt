Proposal for Pioglitazone (Sigma PHR 1043)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
Pioglitazone is a synthetic thiazolidinedione (TZD) that was initially discovered and developed as an antidiabetic agent for type 2 diabetes mellitus. As a member of the glitazone family, its structure is centered on a thiazolidinedione ring that is critical for binding to the nuclear receptor peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) (Fischer, 2016, pp. 23–26). Its discovery by medicinal chemists led to its widespread clinical use as an insulin sensitizer, with the molecule synthesized through conventional organic synthesis methods that allow for high purity and reproducibility. The compound is commercially available from research suppliers, such as Sigma (PHR 1043), and has been extensively studied not only in metabolic disorders but also for its vascular protective and anti‐inflammatory properties. Within its drug class, thiazolidinediones have been used successfully in managing hyperglycemia in diabetic patients and have also been investigated in various preclinical models for their potential in modulating inflammatory responses and oxidative stress, capabilities that are hypothesized to be beneficial in chronic degenerative conditions like dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) (Fischer, 2016, pp. 23–26; Choi, 2019, pp. 25–31).

Therapeutic History:  
Clinically, pioglitazone is approved for the treatment of type 2 diabetes, where its activation of PPARγ leads to enhanced insulin sensitivity, alterations in adipocyte differentiation, and modulation of lipid metabolism. Over the years, its favorable safety profile in diabetic populations has been well documented. Outside of metabolic disease, growing interest in its pleiotropic effects has led to investigations of its potential for vascular protection and anti‐inflammatory actions in ocular tissues. Some studies have proposed that PPARγ agonists may have a role in retinal diseases due to the expression of this nuclear receptor in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells (Fischer, 2016, pp. 23–26; Simpson-Haidaris et al., 2010, pp. 18–19). However, when specifically tested in dry AMD models, such as oxLDL-induced RPE cell death assays, pioglitazone—as well as other PPARγ agonists like ciglitazone and rosiglitazone—did not afford significant protection, whereas troglitazone was the only compound to show marked cytoprotective effects (Choi, 2019, pp. 25–31, 37–42). In the clinical arena, a study conducted in diabetic patients with neovascular AMD receiving anti-VEGF therapy showed that thiazolidinedione use was associated with some beneficial anatomical changes in subretinal fluid and tissue complex thickness (Core et al., 2023, pp. 2–4). Nonetheless, these clinical investigations have been primarily confined to the context of neovascular AMD rather than dry AMD, and there is limited direct clinical evidence for pioglitazone being repurposed for dry AMD. Thus, while the drug’s history in diabetes and its secondary vascular effects offer a rationale for repurposing, its track record specifically in dry AMD remains inconclusive.

Mechanism of Action:  
Pioglitazone exerts its primary pharmacological activity through binding to and activating PPARγ, a ligand-dependent transcription factor that modulates the expression of genes involved in glucose metabolism, lipid handling, and inflammatory responses (Fischer, 2016, pp. 23–26). Activation of PPARγ typically leads to downstream transcriptional events that include the upregulation of antioxidant enzymes such as Cu/Zn-superoxide dismutase (SOD) and suppression of NADPH oxidase expression, thereby reducing oxidative stress (Fischer, 2016, pp. 23–26). In theory, within RPE cells, PPARγ activation could also promote the expression of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α), a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis. PGC-1α is known to drive mitochondrial replication, increase the expression of genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation, and upregulate a suite of antioxidant defenses; these effects have been associated with enhanced cell survival under oxidative stress conditions in RPE models (Iacovelli et al., 2016, pp. 1–2, 12–13). Moreover, because RPE cells are characterized by high metabolic demand and are particularly susceptible to ROS-induced damage, induction of mitochondrial biogenesis could mitigate oxidative stress and preserve intracellular energy metabolism. 

The central hypothesis for the repurposing of pioglitazone in dry AMD is that PPARγ activation by this agonist will induce a PGC-1α–driven mitochondrial biogenesis pathway in RPE cells under oxidative challenge (e.g., H₂O₂ stress). This induction is expected to lead to enhanced mitochondrial function, lower mitochondrial ROS (mito-ROS) production, and an overall reduction in oxidative damage. By decreasing ROS levels, the cascade that normally leads to the degradation of key junctional proteins—specifically claudin-19 and ZO-1, which are critical for maintaining the integrity of the outer blood-retinal barrier—could be interrupted. Thus, preservation or restoration of these junctional complexes could recover the transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) and barrier integrity of the RPE (Fischer, 2016, pp. 23–26). However, existing in vitro studies in RPE models have shown that among several tested PPARγ agonists, pioglitazone did not replicate the cytoprotective effects seen with troglitazone, and the beneficial effects in these studies were found to be independent of PPARγ activation (Choi, 2019, pp. 25–31, 37–42). This discrepancy raises questions about whether pioglitazone can induce the PGC-1α pathway effectively in this context. Nonetheless, the mechanistic rationale remains centered on the possibility that reducing oxidative stress through enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis might preserve cell junction integrity in the RPE, an approach that is biologically plausible based on the roles of both PPARγ and PGC-1α in other tissues and in some retinal models.

Expected Effect:  
According to the proposed hypothesis, when ARPE-19 cells are challenged with H₂O₂ to simulate oxidative stress, treatment with 1 µM pioglitazone is expected to initiate a cascade of molecular events: First, pioglitazone should activate PPARγ, which in turn would upregulate PGC-1α expression. This upregulation is anticipated to enhance mitochondrial biogenesis and function, resulting in a measurable decrease in mitochondrial ROS production as detected by MitoSOX fluorescence assays. In addition, by lowering ROS levels, the compound is expected to protect against the degradation of cell junction proteins such as claudin-19 and ZO-1. These proteins are essential for the formation and maintenance of the tight junction belts in RPE cells, contributing to the integrity of the outer blood-retinal barrier. The experimental readouts proposed include a 45% recovery in TEER, a surrogate marker for barrier integrity, suggesting that pioglitazone would restore functional barrier properties that are compromised under oxidative stress conditions (Fischer, 2016, pp. 23–26). It is important to note, however, that while these effects have been observed in some isolated experiments, the majority of the literature on PPARγ agonists in RPE models has primarily highlighted that only troglitazone, rather than pioglitazone, yielded significant cytoprotection against oxidative insults (Choi, 2019, pp. 25–31, 37–42). Thus, while the expected effect is biologically coherent with the ideal model of reducing ROS-mediated junction protein degradation and restoring TEER, the existing experimental evidence raises concerns regarding whether pioglitazone is capable of achieving these outcomes under the conditions outlined.

Overall Evaluation:  
In summary, the repurposing strategy of using pioglitazone for the treatment of dry AMD is based on a sound mechanistic rationale that integrates several interrelated pathways: the PPARγ-mediated modulation of inflammatory and oxidative stress responses, the PGC-1α–driven enhancement of mitochondrial biogenesis, and the consequent protection of RPE junctional integrity. The well-established clinical safety profile of pioglitazone in diabetic populations is an important strength, as it facilitates potential rapid translation to clinical trials for new indications. Additionally, the notion that mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS overproduction play central roles in RPE degeneration and barrier disruption in dry AMD provides a compelling target for therapeutic intervention (Fischer, 2016, pp. 23–26; Iacovelli et al., 2016, pp. 1–2).

However, there are several weaknesses and limitations to consider. First, while pioglitazone has demonstrated vascular protective and anti-inflammatory properties in other contexts, the literature on its direct effects in RPE cells under oxidative stress is mixed. Notably, in multiple in vitro studies investigating PPARγ agonists in models of oxidative stress-induced RPE cell death, pioglitazone—as well as other compounds such as ciglitazone and rosiglitazone—failed to confer significant cytoprotection, with only troglitazone showing robust protective effects (Choi, 2019, pp. 25–31, 31–37, 37–42). This discrepancy suggests that either pioglitazone does not effectively induce the mitochondrial biogenesis pathway via PGC-1α in RPE cells or that its mechanism of action may not directly translate into the preservation of tight junction proteins like claudin-19 and ZO-1 under oxidative stress. Second, the hypothesis assumes that PPARγ activation by pioglitazone would sufficiently upregulate PGC-1α to promote mitochondrial biogenesis; however, there is little direct evidence in the current literature that pioglitazone can induce such an effect in RPE cells, as opposed to direct genetic overexpression or the use of other PGC-1α–targeting drugs (Iacovelli et al., 2016, pp. 1–2, 9–12, 12–13). Third, while a reported experimental observation of a 45% recovery in TEER in H₂O₂-challenged ARPE-19 cells treated with 1 µM pioglitazone is promising, it is in marked contrast to the majority of findings in similar oxidative stress assays, calling for cautious interpretation and independent validation (Choi, 2019, pp. 25–31).

Given these considerations, the overall evaluation of pioglitazone as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD is mixed. Its strengths lie in its well-characterized safety profile, established use as a PPARγ agonist, and the plausible mechanistic link between reduced ROS production and preservation of barrier integrity in RPE cells. However, the weaknesses are notable—most prominently, the inconsistent preclinical efficacy observed in RPE models of oxidative stress and the lack of robust evidence demonstrating that pioglitazone can drive PGC-1α–mediated mitochondrial biogenesis in this context. The literature does not strongly support the hypothesis that pioglitazone will prevent ROS-mediated degradation of junction proteins or restore TEER in RPE cells, as only alternative compounds (e.g., troglitazone) have shown such effects in some studies (Choi, 2019, pp. 25–31, 37–42).

In conclusion, while the molecular rationale for targeting mitochondrial dysfunction and junctional breakdown in dry AMD is compelling, and the repurposing of a clinically validated drug like pioglitazone is attractive from a safety and translational standpoint, the current experimental evidence raises significant doubts about its efficacy in this specific application. Further comprehensive studies are needed to resolve these discrepancies—ideally through optimized in vitro models that replicate the oxidative and metabolic challenges of dry AMD, and through detailed mechanistic studies that can confirm or refute the induction of PGC-1α and subsequent mitochondrial biogenesis by pioglitazone in RPE cells. Moreover, alternative PPARγ agonists or combination strategies that pair pioglitazone with agents known to specifically enhance mitochondrial function might offer a more viable therapeutic strategy. Until such additional data become available, the repurposing of pioglitazone for dry AMD should be considered with caution, and resources may better be allocated towards candidates with more consistent preclinical efficacy in relevant RPE models (Fischer, 2016, pp. 23–26; Choi, 2019, pp. 25–31).
