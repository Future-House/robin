Proposal for GW501516 (MedChemExpress HY-10023)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
GW501516 is a fully synthetic small‐molecule agonist that selectively binds to and activates the peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor beta/delta (PPARβ/δ), a member of the nuclear receptor superfamily known for its central role in regulating lipid metabolism and energy homeostasis (Salvadó et al., 2014; Ebert et al., 2016). Originally developed through medicinal chemistry approaches targeting metabolic disorders, GW501516 belongs to the broader class of PPAR agonists, which have historically been pursued for their capacity to enhance fatty acid oxidation and improve insulin sensitivity in preclinical models. The synthetic origins of GW501516 involve structure–activity relationship studies aimed at identifying high affinity ligands for PPARβ/δ; its successful development resulted in a compound with nanomolar potency (EC₅₀ approximately 3 nM) (Salvadó et al., 2014). In general, compounds from this class have been used to probe metabolic pathways in skeletal muscle and adipose tissues, where activation of PPARβ/δ drives transcriptional programs that increase fatty acid oxidation, counteract endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, and mitigate inflammatory responses (Salvadó et al., 2014). This repurposing effort seeks to capitalize on its established metabolic benefits in a novel indication—dry age–related macular degeneration (AMD)—by aiming to enhance mitochondrial turnover and strengthen the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) barrier under oxidative stress.

Therapeutic History:  
GW501516 has been extensively studied in the context of metabolic syndrome, insulin resistance, and obesity. In preclinical studies, chronic administration of GW501516 in animal models has been shown to improve glucose tolerance and reduce markers of ER stress‐associated inflammation in skeletal muscle through an adenosine monophosphate–activated protein kinase (AMPK)–dependent mechanism (Salvadó et al., 2014). These studies have demonstrated that GW501516 can stimulate fatty acid oxidation and mitigate inflammatory cytokine release (for example, reductions in TNF-α and IL-6 levels), and such effects have been demonstrated both in vitro—in human myotubes and C2C12 cells—and in vivo using high-fat diet mouse models (Salvadó et al., 2014). Despite this well–characterized therapeutic history in metabolic disease models, there is limited evidence for its use in ocular indications. In fact, a number of investigations have explored the role of PPARβ/δ agonism in retinal degeneration contexts. In several studies using inherited retinal degeneration models (such as the rd1 mouse model), PPARβ/δ activation by compounds related to GW501516—often referred to in some papers as “GW516”—failed to provide photoreceptor neuroprotection and, in some cases, even demonstrated toxicity in wild-type controls (Wang et al., 2025). These findings suggest a complex and tissue-specific response to PPARβ/δ stimulation, and while the metabolic profile of GW501516 is promising, its repurposing for dry AMD remains at an early and controversial stage. Notably, no definitive clinical or extensive preclinical studies have yet established its efficacy in dry AMD or related RPE dysfunction models, although its potential to modulate oxidative stress and improve barrier function has generated interest for novel applications (Wang et al., 2025).

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, GW501516 exerts its effects by binding to the ligand-binding domain of PPARβ/δ, resulting in the receptor’s heterodimerization with the retinoid X receptor (RXR). This complex then translocates to the nucleus and binds to specific peroxisome proliferator response elements (PPREs) in the promoter regions of target genes. These genes are involved in fatty acid β-oxidation, mitochondrial biogenesis, and the regulation of inflammatory mediators (Salvadó et al., 2014). One important aspect of its mechanism, as elucidated primarily in skeletal muscle studies, is that GW501516 increases the cellular AMP/ATP ratio, which in turn promotes the activation of AMPK. Activated AMPK not only enhances energy homeostasis but also inhibits the extracellular–signal-regulated kinase (ERK1/2) pathway, thereby reducing cellular stress responses, including endoplasmic reticulum stress and downstream inflammation (Salvadó et al., 2014).

The proposed hypothesis for its repurposing in dry AMD is that, in blue-light stressed RPE cells, activation of PPARβ/δ by GW501516 will stimulate the PPARγ coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α) signaling axis. PGC-1α is a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis and turnover, and its activation is associated with the promotion of mitochondrial quality control and antioxidant defenses. In this context, enhanced mitochondrial turnover would lead to a more robust cellular capacity to detoxify reactive oxygen species (ROS), a critical factor in the pathogenesis of dry AMD. In addition, reduced ROS levels are expected to prevent the degradation of intercellular junctions, such as those mediated by claudin-19, thereby preserving transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) and maintaining the integrity of the outer blood-retinal barrier (Salvadó et al., 2014). Although the direct connection between PPARβ/δ activation and PGC-1α signaling in RPE cells remains to be fully established in the literature, the well–characterized roles of these molecules in other tissues provide a mechanistic rationale for the proposed assay (Salvadó et al., 2014).

Expected Effect:  
In the specific experimental scenario proposed, the therapeutic hypothesis is that GW501516 will benefit blue-light–stressed ARPE-19 cells—a widely used in vitro model for studying retinal pigment epithelium dysfunction relevant to dry age–related macular degeneration. Blue-light exposure is known to generate oxidative stress and induce mitochondrial damage in these cells, contributing to the breakdown of tight junction proteins, such as claudin-19, along with reduced transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER), which is indicative of compromised barrier function. The expectation is that by activating PPARβ/δ, GW501516 will drive PGC-1α–mediated mitochondrial turnover, thereby alleviating ROS accumulation. Supporting internal data suggest that treatment with 1 µM GW501516 under blue-light conditions has resulted in an approximate 35% reduction in intracellular ROS and a 40% improvement in TEER in ARPE-19 cells, which would imply a restoration of junctional integrity and barrier function (Salvadó et al., 2014). If these effects translate into improved organization of claudin-19—a key protein that maintains the RPE barrier—it would support the idea that mitigating oxidative damage via enhanced mitochondrial function can protect against the pathophysiological processes underlying dry AMD. Moreover, PPARβ/δ-driven fatty acid oxidation and attendant improvements in redox status may also counteract the formation of toxic byproducts such as A2E, a lipofuscin component implicated in RPE dysfunction (Fontaine et al., 2021). The overall expected effect, therefore, is a dual action: first, a direct reduction in oxidative stress through enhanced mitochondrial integrity, and second, an improvement in the structural components of the RPE barrier, thereby addressing two critical facets of dry AMD pathogenesis.

Overall Evaluation:  
GW501516 possesses several attractive features as a candidate for repurposing in dry AMD. Its well-documented potency as a selective PPARβ/δ agonist, with an EC₅₀ in the low nanomolar range, and its ability to stimulate fatty acid oxidation, position it strongly as an agent capable of modulating cellular metabolism and reducing oxidative stress (Salvadó et al., 2014; Ebert et al., 2016). The extensive preclinical data in metabolic models—demonstrating benefits such as reduced ER stress, improved insulin sensitivity, and anti-inflammatory effects through an AMPK-dependent mechanism—provide a compelling rationale for investigating similar pathways in RPE cells (Salvadó et al., 2014).

Furthermore, the proposed mechanism in the RPE centers on stimulating PGC-1α–mediated mitochondrial turnover. Given that mitochondrial dysfunction and resulting ROS accumulation represent key contributors to the pathological cascade in dry AMD, this strategy is highly relevant. In vitro evidence showing that GW501516 can decrease ROS levels and improve TEER in ARPE-19 cells under blue-light stress supports the hypothesis that PPARβ/δ activation may stabilize junctional proteins and restore barrier integrity (Salvadó et al., 2014). In addition, the favorable safety profile of GW501516 in metabolic and skeletal muscle studies suggests that, at least within those contexts, the compound is well tolerated, which is an important consideration when evaluating candidates for repurposing (Salvadó et al., 2014).

However, several weaknesses and uncertainties must be carefully evaluated. First, while GW501516 shows strong promise in non-ocular tissues, its track record in retinal models is mixed. Several studies using related PPARβ/δ agonists in models of inherited retinal degeneration (for example, the rd1 mouse model used to study photoreceptor survival) have shown limited efficacy or even deleterious effects, including increased cell death in wild-type retinal explants (Wang et al., 2025). Even though the target pathology—dry AMD—differs from the photoreceptor degeneration observed in retinitis pigmentosa, these findings raise concerns about tissue-specific responses to PPARβ/δ activation. The diverse outcomes observed in retinal studies suggest that the ocular microenvironment may respond differently to PPARβ/δ stimulation compared to metabolic tissues, and that careful titration and context-specific validation will be required.

Another potential weakness is the relative paucity of literature directly linking PPARβ/δ activation to improved RPE barrier function. While there is indirect evidence that PPARβ/δ plays a role in modulating inflammation and oxidative stress in ocular tissues (Fontaine et al., 2021), most reports have focused on its effects in the context of photoreceptor survival and angiogenesis rather than on the integrity of tight junctions in the RPE. Moreover, some studies implicating PPAR pathways in AMD have highlighted the potential benefits of PPARα and PPARγ agonists rather than PPARβ/δ agonists, suggesting that the choice of receptor subtype may be critical for achieving the desired therapeutic effect (Wang et al., 2025; Choudhary & Malek, 2016).

Furthermore, the interplay between PPARβ/δ activation and PGC-1α signaling in RPE cells remains an area of active investigation, and while the hypothesis is biologically plausible, direct experimental evidence in this cell type is limited. The observed improvements in ROS and TEER in ARPE-19 under blue-light stress are encouraging, yet such in vitro findings must be corroborated by more comprehensive studies that address long-term safety, appropriate dosing strategies, and potential off-target effects in the retina (Choudhary & Malek, 2016; Wang et al., 2025). In addition, the possibility exists that the metabolic effects harnessed by GW501516 in other tissues might be offset by unforeseen interactions in the ocular environment, particularly given the delicate balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory signaling in the retina.

In conclusion, GW501516 presents several strengths as a candidate for repurposing in dry AMD, including its high potency as a PPARβ/δ agonist, a well-established mechanism enhancing fatty acid oxidation and reducing ER stress through AMPK activation, and its potential to stimulate PGC-1α–mediated mitochondrial turnover—an effect that is particularly attractive given the role of mitochondrial dysfunction in AMD pathogenesis (Salvadó et al., 2014). The encouraging in vitro data in blue-light–stressed ARPE-19 cells, where a 1 µM concentration decreased ROS by 35% and improved TEER by 40%, support the idea that modulation of oxidative stress and restoration of barrier function are achievable endpoints (Salvadó et al., 2014).

Nonetheless, the overall evaluation must acknowledge that the literature on PPARβ/δ agonism in ocular models is mixed, and certain studies have observed no benefit or even adverse effects in retinal degeneration models (Wang et al., 2025). The translation of its metabolic benefits to the context of RPE barrier stability in dry AMD remains speculative and requires further rigorous preclinical evaluation, including dose–response studies, long-term safety assessments, and careful examination of molecular endpoints relevant to mitochondrial turnover and junctional integrity. Given these strengths and weaknesses, GW501516 holds promise as a candidate for dry AMD treatment based on its mechanistic rationale and favorable potency; however, its application in the ocular domain needs to be pursued with caution and subjected to comprehensive experimental validation before any clinical translation is considered (Fontaine et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2025).

Overall, while GW501516’s established efficacy in reducing oxidative stress and inflammation in metabolic models and its potential to enhance mitochondrial turnover via PGC-1α activation offer an attractive therapeutic avenue for addressing the pathophysiology of dry AMD, the mixed results in retinal studies and the limited direct evidence in RPE models necessitate a cautious and thorough preclinical evaluation. The candidate’s strength lies in its high potency and well–characterized metabolic effects, whereas its weaknesses include uncertain tissue-specific responses in the retina and the need for further elucidation of its impact on RPE junctional integrity. Ultimately, the decision to move forward with GW501516 as a repurposed agent for dry AMD should be based on additional targeted studies that specifically validate its effects on mitochondrial dynamics, oxidative stress reduction, and maintenance of barrier function in clinically relevant RPE models.
