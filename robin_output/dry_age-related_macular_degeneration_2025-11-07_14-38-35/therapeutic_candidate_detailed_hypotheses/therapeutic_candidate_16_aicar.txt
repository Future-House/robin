Proposal for AICAR (Selleckchem S1802; Sigma A9978)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
AICAR, chemically known as 5‐aminoimidazole‐4‐carboxamide ribonucleotide, is an endogenous purine biosynthetic intermediate that has been harnessed as a synthetic, cell‐permeable AMP analog to activate AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK) pharmacologically (Hyttinen et al., 2011). Originally identified as a naturally occurring metabolic intermediate, AICAR gained prominence when researchers recognized that its structural similarity to AMP enables it to mimic cellular energetic stress conditions, thereby initiating AMPK‐mediated cellular responses (Green, 2013). In its synthetic format, AICAR is produced and purified to high standards by vendors such as Selleckchem and Sigma (S1802; A9978), and belongs to the broader class of nucleoside analogs used as metabolic regulators. Compounds belonging to this class have historically been employed to interrogate and modulate energy sensing and substrate utilization in diverse cell types, ranging from skeletal muscle to vascular smooth muscle (Pokrywka et al., 2014). This same class of metabolic modulators has demonstrated efficacy in experimental models of mitochondrial disease and muscle wasting by stimulating AMPK‐dependent pathways that augment mitochondrial biogenesis and restore energy balance (Green, 2013). Owing to its capacity to influence the phosphorylation of key enzymes and transcription factors, AICAR has found applications not only in elucidating fundamental bioenergetic pathways but also as a candidate to ameliorate conditions where energy homeostasis is disrupted. In the context of retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells, where the maintenance of tight junction integrity is crucial for barrier function and homeostasis, AICAR’s mechanism of action offers a rationale for its repurposing in dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) (Hyttinen et al., 2011).

Historically, metabolic modulation via AMPK activators has been extensively investigated in various systems, and AICAR has been a workhorse agent in these studies owing to its reliable pharmacological profile (Pokrywka et al., 2014). Biochemically, AICAR has been widely used to stimulate AMPK in cell culture and animal models, resulting in a host of downstream effects that include enhanced fatty acid oxidation, increased glucose uptake, and modulated lipid synthesis (Green, 2013). In addition, AICAR’s ability to promote autophagy through the inhibition of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) cascade has been observed across various cell types, including those derived from retinal tissues (Hyttinen et al., 2011). Clinical investigations have predominantly focused on its metabolic effects in conditions such as diabetes and ischemia, although its application in ocular diseases has been comparatively less explored (Pokrywka et al., 2014). In the field of ophthalmology, research into the role of AMPK in retinal health has uncovered that AMPK activation influences key RPE functions including autophagy, inflammatory cytokine production, and tight junction integrity (Qin, 2012). Notably, investigations in RPE-related models have demonstrated that pharmacologic modulation of AMPK can either protect or impair barrier function, depending on the context and isoform engaged (Qin & De Vries, 2008). While AICAR itself has not been approved explicitly for the treatment of dry AMD, its well–established use in preclinical studies, combined with its demonstrated ability to rescue barrier integrity in RPE under oxidative stress conditions, supports its potential repurposing for this indication (Hyttinen et al., 2011; Villarroel et al., 2011). Thus, the accumulated literature demonstrates that AMPK activators, and AICAR in particular, offer promising mechanistic pathways to address cellular energy deficits and junctional disintegration, both critical features of dry AMD pathology (Hyttinen et al., 2011; Qin & De Vries, 2008).

Mechanism of Action:  
The molecular mechanism by which AICAR exerts its therapeutic effects begins with its cellular uptake through nucleoside transporters such as ENT1 and ENT2, a process that ensures its intracellular availability (Dolinar et al., 2018). Once internalized, AICAR is rapidly phosphorylated by adenosine kinase, converting it into 5‐aminoimidazole‐4‐carboxamide ribonucleotide monophosphate (ZMP) (Hyttinen et al., 2011; Green, 2013). ZMP, as an AMP analog, binds to the γ subunit of AMPK, eliciting conformational changes that not only enhance its allosteric activation but also protect it from dephosphorylation (Hyttinen et al., 2011; Green, 2013). Concurrently, the binding of ZMP to AMPK facilitates the phosphorylation of the catalytic α‐subunit at Thr172, thereby augmenting the kinase’s catalytic activity to phosphorylate downstream targets such as acetyl‐CoA carboxylase (ACC) (Qin & De Vries, 2008). The phosphorylation and inactivation of ACC lead to decreased fatty acid synthesis and enhanced fatty acid oxidation, contributing significantly to improved energy homeostasis (Qin & De Vries, 2008; Green, 2013). Notably, in the retinal pigment epithelium, robust AMPK activation is implicated in the stabilization of tight junction proteins and in the promotion of autophagy, a process that is essential for removal of damaged organelles and protein aggregates under oxidative stress conditions (Hyttinen et al., 2011; Qin, 2012).

Furthermore, the role of AICAR in modulating tight junction dynamics is complex and intersects with its influence on autophagic flux; AMPK activation can downregulate mTOR signaling, which not only contributes to increased autophagic clearance but also appears to correlate with improved organization of junctional proteins, ultimately enhancing transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) (Hyttinen et al., 2011; Qin, 2012). In experimental settings using RPE cells, the administration of AICAR under oxidative or high‐glucose stress conditions leads to a cascade of phosphorylation events that foster an environment conducive to tight junction assembly while simultaneously stabilizing cellular energy stores (Miyagishima et al., 2021; Qin & De Vries, 2008). This biochemical cascade is further verified by the reversal of the observed effects upon the use of compound C, an AMPK inhibitor, which underscores the specificity of AICAR’s action being mediated through AMPK activation (Miyagishima et al., 2021; Villarroel et al., 2011).

It is also critical to note that the effects of AICAR on AMPK activity are influenced not only by its rapid intracellular conversion but also by the intrinsic cellular context—including the balance of energy substrates, the presence of other nucleosides, and the isoform‐specific roles of AMPK (Qin & De Vries, 2008). The resultant activation of AMPK leads to multifaceted downstream responses, such as cellular adaptation to metabolic stress, regulation of inflammatory mediators, and modulation of cytoskeletal elements that cooperate in the maintenance of cell–cell adhesion within the RPE monolayer (Villarroel et al., 2011). This detailed mechanistic insight provides the foundation for hypothesizing that AICAR may effectively drive energy homeostasis and promote tight junction assembly in RPE cells, addressing one of the core pathological features of dry AMD (Hyttinen et al., 2011).

Expected Effect in Dry AMD:  
In the context of dry age‐related macular degeneration, the primary pathological hallmark is the degeneration of the RPE accompanied by impaired tight junction integrity and compromised barrier function, resulting in increased paracellular permeability and accumulation of metabolic waste. Based on the observed effects of AICAR in in vitro RPE models, it is expected that AICAR will allosterically activate AMPK within RPE cells, leading to enhanced energy homeostasis and improved assembly of tight junctions (Hyttinen et al., 2011). Experimental data have demonstrated that when induced with oxidative stress, as is common in AMD, treatment with 500 µM AICAR increased TEER from 90 to 135 Ω·cm²—a clear indicator of enhanced barrier integrity (Miyagishima et al., 2021). This increase in TEER is paralleled by a 2.8‐fold elevation in phosphorylated AMPK levels, which confirms that AICAR is effectively engaging its intended target in these cells (Miyagishima et al., 2021; Hyttinen et al., 2011).

The underlying mechanism for this improved barrier function is thought to be twofold. First, through ACC inhibition—mediated by AMPK activation—there is a shift in the cellular lipid economy, reducing the deleterious lipid accumulation that can disrupt tight junction architecture (Qin & De Vries, 2008). Second, the activation of autophagic pathways facilitates the clearance of oxidatively damaged proteins and organelles, thereby enhancing the overall health of the RPE and reinforcing its capacity to maintain tight junction complexes (Hyttinen et al., 2011; Qin, 2012). This dual action not only addresses the immediate energetic deficit induced by oxidative stress but also contributes to the structural resilience of the RPE monolayer.

Moreover, the observed reversal of AICAR’s protective effects by compound C serves as strong evidence that the benefit is specifically mediated through the AMPK pathway rather than off‐target effects (Miyagishima et al., 2021; Sid et al., 2014). This mechanistic specificity is critical when evaluating a compound for clinical translation, as it suggests that downstream effects such as TEER enhancement, tight junction stabilization, and modulation of paracellular flux are directly attributable to AMPK activation. Additionally, AICAR‐mediated autophagy has been implicated in maintaining cellular integrity under chronic oxidative stress—a condition that is intrinsically linked to the progression of dry AMD (Hyttinen et al., 2011).

Given the central role of AMPK in RPE physiology, particularly in the regulation of junction protein stability and energy metabolism, the expected therapeutic effect of AICAR in dry AMD is to restore and preserve RPE barrier function. This restoration is anticipated to limit paracellular fluid leakage, reduce the deposition of extracellular debris, and ultimately slow the degenerative processes characteristic of dry AMD (Hyttinen et al., 2011; Villarroel et al., 2011). The projected outcome is an improvement in cellular homeostasis and reduced progression of retinal degeneration, which translates into a potential clinical benefit for patients with dry AMD (Hyttinen et al., 2011; Villarroel et al., 2011).

Therapeutic History and Prior Applications:  
AICAR’s journey from a metabolic intermediate to a pharmacologically active compound has been well documented across a range of biomedical research areas. In preclinical studies, AICAR has been extensively utilized to activate AMPK in various cellular models, thereby providing critical insights into the regulation of energy balance, mitochondrial biogenesis, and lipid metabolism (Green, 2013; Pokrywka et al., 2014). Its early applications in skeletal muscle and cancer cells underscored its ability to stimulate glucose uptake and shift cellular energy dynamics in favor of catabolic pathways (Dolinar et al., 2018; Longnus et al., 2003).

In cardiovascular research, AICAR was instrumental in elucidating the mechanisms underlying glycogenolysis and vasorelaxation, where its conversion to ZMP led to the activation of AMPK in vascular smooth muscle cells, resulting in beneficial metabolic adaptations (Pyla et al., 2022). Although these studies did not directly target ocular tissues, they established a robust framework for understanding how AMPK activation can mediate protective responses under conditions of metabolic stress.

The transition of AICAR into the realm of ophthalmological research was driven by the observation that AMPK is a pivotal regulator of RPE function and tight junction assembly. Publications have demonstrated that oxidative stress—a significant contributor to AMD pathology—can impair RPE barrier integrity by disrupting the organization of tight junction proteins, while AMPK activation appears to counteract these deleterious changes (Qin, 2012; Villarroel et al., 2011). In one study, treatment with AICAR in RPE cells under H₂O₂‐induced stress not only increased TEER but also elevated levels of phosphorylated AMPK, thereby indicating enhanced barrier stability (Miyagishima et al., 2021).

Furthermore, the dual role of AMPK in regulating autophagy and lipid metabolism has been a focal point in dry AMD research, as dysfunctional autophagy and lipid accumulation are hallmarks of RPE degeneration (Hyttinen et al., 2011). The protective effects of AMPK activators in other models of cellular stress, such as in cardiomyocytes and hepatocarcinoma cells, lend additional support to the hypothesis that similar mechanisms may be beneficial in preserving RPE function (Kawashima et al., 2020; Sid et al., 2014).

Despite these promising findings, it is important to note that the therapeutic history of AICAR in the context of ocular diseases remains in the preclinical phase, with limited direct clinical evaluation for dry AMD. Some studies have even suggested that AMPK activation in the RPE could have dual outcomes, potentially inhibiting phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments under oxidative conditions (Qin & De Vries, 2008). This observation highlights the intricacies of AMPK signaling in retinal cells and underscores the need for rigorous dose–response and context–specific studies. Nonetheless, the accumulated preclinical data from both ocular and non‐ocular systems provide a strong rationale for further exploring AICAR as a repurposed therapeutic candidate for dry AMD (Hyttinen et al., 2011; Qin, 2012).

Expected Impact on RPE Function and Dry AMD Pathology:  
The central hypothesis underpinning the repurposing of AICAR in dry AMD is that its ability to activate AMPK will lead to a concerted improvement in RPE barrier function and energy homeostasis—two crucial aspects compromised in AMD. In vitro models of RPE, particularly those derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSC‐RPE), have shown that exposure to oxidative stress causes significant deterioration in tight junction assembly and barrier integrity, as measured by transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) (Miyagishima et al., 2021).

When these stressed RPE cells are treated with AICAR, a rapid increase in TEER from approximately 90 Ω·cm² to 135 Ω·cm² has been observed, indicating a substantial enhancement in tight junction integrity (Miyagishima et al., 2021; Qin & De Vries, 2008). At the molecular level, this improvement correlates with a 2.8‐fold rise in phosphorylated AMPK, which is directly associated with the activation of energy restoration pathways and the stabilization of tight junction proteins (Miyagishima et al., 2021; Hyttinen et al., 2011).

The benefits conferred by AMPK activation are expected to be multifaceted. On one hand, the inhibition of ACC through phosphorylation is anticipated to reduce aberrant lipid synthesis and promote fatty acid oxidation, thereby alleviating metabolic stress within RPE cells (Qin & De Vries, 2008). On the other hand, enhanced autophagic flux driven by AMPK is likely to facilitate the removal of oxidatively damaged proteins and organelles, contributing to cellular repair processes that are pivotal in slowing the progression of dry AMD (Hyttinen et al., 2011; Qin, 2012).

These laboratory observations have practical implications. In a dysregulated retinal environment—characterized by oxidative damage, inflammatory cytokine presence, and disrupted tight junctions—the activation of AMPK by AICAR could serve as a countermeasure to re‐establish and maintain the critical barrier functions of the RPE (Villarroel et al., 2011). Moreover, by diminishing paracellular leak and preserving the integrity of junctional complexes, AICAR may reduce the accumulation of metabolic waste products and drusen, which are pathological hallmarks of dry AMD (Hyttinen et al., 2011; Villarroel et al., 2011).

It is also noteworthy that the experimental reversal of AICAR’s protective effects by compound C—a well‐known AMPK inhibitor—provides definitive evidence that the beneficial outcomes observed in RPE cells are mediated specifically through AMPK signaling (Miyagishima et al., 2021; Sid et al., 2014). This on‐target specificity increases the confidence that the observed improvements in TEER are attributable to the intended mechanism rather than off‐target pharmacological actions. Consequently, the expected therapeutic effect in dry AMD would involve enhanced RPE cell viability, reduced cellular stress, and a restored barrier function that together could slow or even halt the progressive degeneration observed in patients (Hyttinen et al., 2011; Villarroel et al., 2011).

Overall Evaluation:  
When evaluating AICAR as a repurposed drug candidate for dry AMD, several strengths are immediately apparent, along with a number of challenges that must be carefully addressed during further development. One of the foremost strengths of AICAR is its well–characterized mechanism of action. By serving as an AMP analog that is intracellularly converted to ZMP, AICAR robustly activates AMPK—a key cellular energy sensor that orchestrates a wide range of metabolic responses (Hyttinen et al., 2011; Green, 2013). This activation is tightly coupled to downstream effects such as ACC inhibition, enhanced fatty acid oxidation, and the promotion of autophagy, which are all beneficial in counteracting the metabolic and oxidative stresses inherent in dry AMD (Qin & De Vries, 2008; Qin, 2012).

Furthermore, the experimental data indicating a significant increase in TEER and a concomitant rise in phosphorylated AMPK levels in oxidatively stressed iPSC‐RPE cells provide compelling evidence that AICAR can restore RPE barrier function (Miyagishima et al., 2021; Qin & De Vries, 2008). This is particularly important because tight junction integrity and paracellular barrier properties are central to the maintenance of retinal homeostasis and are critically impaired in dry AMD (Villarroel et al., 2011). The demonstration that the beneficial effects of AICAR are reversible by an AMPK inhibitor further reinforces the concept that the drug’s actions are on–target and biologically relevant (Miyagishima et al., 2021; Sid et al., 2014).

Nevertheless, several potential weaknesses require cautious evaluation. First, while AMPK activation is generally associated with enhanced cellular resilience, some studies have reported that excessive or isoform‐specific activation of AMPK in RPE cells may lead to unintended consequences, such as the inhibition of photoreceptor outer segment phagocytosis—a process vital for retinal renewal (Qin & De Vries, 2008). Such a dualistic role of AMPK creates a delicate balance whereby the same pathway that is intended to improve barrier function might also impede essential RPE functions if not precisely modulated.

Additionally, the data in the literature suggest that under certain circumstances, AMPK activation might even contribute to increased epithelial permeability rather than a strengthening of tight junctions (Villarroel et al., 2011; Qin & De Vries, 2008). This paradoxical effect raises concerns about potential context–dependent outcomes of AICAR treatment. For example, while low‐to‐moderate activation of AMPK might support tight junction assembly and autophagy, hyperactivation under severe metabolic or oxidative stress could exacerbate junctional disorganization, thereby counteracting any beneficial effects (Villarroel et al., 2011).

Another challenge is the potential for systemic side effects arising from AICAR’s widespread actions in other tissues. Given that AICAR has been employed in models of diabetes, cardiac metabolism, and even cancer, its pleiotropic effects could impose additional safety considerations when repurposed for ocular use (Pokrywka et al., 2014; Viglino et al., 2019). There is also the issue of dosage; concentrations of 500 µM are effective in vitro, but translating such dosages to in vivo ocular applications requires careful pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic studies to ensure that therapeutic levels are achieved locally without eliciting undesirable systemic responses (Miyagishima et al., 2021; Asby et al., 2015).

Moreover, given the complex interplay between AMPK signaling, autophagy, and cellular differentiation processes, it is essential to thoroughly understand the long‐term implications of chronic AMPK activation in the RPE. While short‐term activation may be protective, persistent stimulation could lead to energy depletion or maladaptive remodeling of the RPE cytoskeleton, which in turn could affect visual function (Hyttinen et al., 2011; Qin, 2012). This underscores the need for extensive preclinical studies that evaluate both the efficacy and the safety profile of AICAR over prolonged periods.

Finally, the inherent variability in patient responses due to genetic, metabolic, and environmental factors presents an additional hurdle. The RPE’s response to oxidative stress, and hence its sensitivity to AMPK modulation, may vary widely among individuals with dry AMD. It will be crucial to identify biomarkers that predict response to AICAR treatment and to tailor dosing regimens accordingly (Hyttinen et al., 2011; Qin & De Vries, 2008).

In summary, while AICAR shows considerable promise as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD by virtue of its well‐established mechanism of AMPK activation, experimental data demonstrating tight junction rescue in RPE cells, and its ability to promote autophagic processes that counteract oxidative stress, its repurposing is tempered by concerns regarding the dualistic effects of AMPK signaling in the RPE, potential systemic side effects, and the challenges associated with dosage translation from in vitro to in vivo applications (Villarroel et al., 2011). The overall evaluation is that AICAR exhibits several compelling advantages—including mechanistic clarity, on‐target efficacy, and a robust preclinical rationale—that substantiate further investigation through rigorous preclinical and, eventually, clinical studies. With a careful approach that addresses the aforementioned weaknesses, AICAR may ultimately emerge as a valuable addition to the therapeutic arsenal against dry AMD (Hyttinen et al., 2011; Qin, 2012).

Comprehensive Future Directions and Considerations:  
To fully validate AICAR’s potential for treating dry AMD, a series of detailed investigations is warranted. First, further elucidation of the dose–response relationship in RPE cell cultures will be critical. This includes not only establishing the optimal concentration of AICAR to achieve maximal TEER improvement and AMPK activation but also determining whether these effects are sustained over time without incurring cellular toxicity (Miyagishima et al., 2021; Hyttinen et al., 2011). Long‐term studies are needed to assess whether repeated dosing maintains barrier integrity or if adaptive processes eventually diminish the protective effects of AICAR.

Additionally, it will be necessary to delve deeper into the isoform‐specific effects of AMPK in the RPE. Evidence suggests that the α2 isoform in particular plays a critical role in mediating responses to oxidative stress and that its activation may differ qualitatively from that of the α1 subunit (Qin & De Vries, 2008; Qin, 2012). Understanding these nuances will help design therapeutic strategies that maximize the beneficial aspects of AMPK activation while minimizing potential adverse outcomes, such as impaired phagocytosis or unwanted alterations in cellular metabolism (Qin & De Vries, 2008).

In parallel, investigating the interplay between AICAR‐induced AMPK activation and autophagic pathways in the RPE will shed light on how tightly coupled these processes are in the context of dry AMD. Since autophagy is vital for clearing oxidatively damaged cellular components and supporting cell survival, it will be important to determine whether prolonged AMPK activation fosters a beneficial autophagic flux or rather leads to autophagy dysregulation over time (Hyttinen et al., 2011; Qin, 2012). Strategies that involve monitoring autophagic markers along with TEER measurements and junction protein expression could provide a comprehensive picture of cellular health in AICAR‐treated RPE cells.

Given the concerns regarding potential systemic effects, advanced drug delivery systems such as localized intraocular injections or biodegradable implants could be considered for delivering AICAR directly to the retina, thereby limiting systemic exposure and enhancing local efficacy (Viglino et al., 2019; Sid et al., 2014). Preclinical animal models that mimic the pathological features of dry AMD will be indispensable in evaluating the pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, and therapeutic windows of AICAR in an in vivo setting. Such studies would also be instrumental in identifying potential adverse effects and assessing the long‐term safety profile of AICAR when administered via ocular routes.

Another key consideration is the potential for combination therapy. Since some reports indicate that AMPK activation by AICAR might impair certain RPE functions such as phagocytosis if excessively activated, combining AICAR with other agents that either modulate AMPK isoform activity or protect against excessive autophagic stimulation may provide a synergistic benefit (Qin & De Vries, 2008; Villarroel et al., 2011). For instance, the protective effect of fenofibric acid in preventing AICAR‐induced permeability increases has been documented, suggesting that a combination approach may overcome the limitations of monotherapy (Villarroel et al., 2011).

Finally, translational studies will need to integrate biomarker analyses capable of monitoring AMPK activation status, autophagy markers, and tight junction protein localization in the RPE. These biomarkers could guide dose optimization and serve as early indicators of therapeutic response in clinical trials (Villarroel et al., 2011; Qin & De Vries, 2008). Genetic or epigenetic factors that predispose individuals to variations in RPE responsiveness may also be identified, thereby enabling personalized medicine approaches for patients with dry AMD.

In conclusion, while there remain numerous challenges and uncertainties, AICAR represents a highly promising candidate for repurposing as a barrier‐protective agent in dry AMD. Its capacity to robustly activate AMPK, improve cellular energy homeostasis, promote autophagy, and restore tight junction integrity in RPE cells offers a multifaceted mode of action that addresses several key pathological features of dry AMD. With targeted preclinical studies that unpack the complexity of AMPK signaling in retinal tissues and strategic clinical trial designs that mitigate potential adverse effects, AICAR could conceivably fill a significant therapeutic gap in the current treatment landscape for dry AMD (Hyttinen et al., 2011; Qin, 2012; Villarroel et al., 2011).

Overall Conclusions:  
AICAR’s well‐documented ability to mimic AMP—thereby inducing AMPK activation—and its established role in regulating cellular energy balance and autophagy place it squarely in the category of compounds with high therapeutic potential for metabolic disorders. Emerging evidence in retinal models, particularly those simulating the oxidative stress and barrier dysfunction typical of dry AMD, further reinforces the rationale behind its repurposing. Nonetheless, while preclinical data are encouraging, careful consideration must be given to the dualistic nature of AMPK signaling in the RPE, as well as the potential systemic risks associated with systemic exposure. Despite these challenges, the convergence of multiple lines of evidence—from improved TEER measurements and specific molecular activation to supportive mechanistic studies—strongly advocates for further research into AICAR as a viable therapeutic strategy for dry AMD (Hyttinen et al., 2011; Villarroel et al., 2011).

In summary, the repurposing of AICAR for dry AMD is supported by both its robust mechanistic underpinnings and its promising preclinical efficacy data, placing it among the most attractive candidates for modulating RPE barrier integrity in this debilitating disease. Future studies that focus on fine‐tuning its dosage, delivery, and integrated combination therapies hold the key to unlocking its full therapeutic potential and advancing it from the bench to the clinic (Hyttinen et al., 2011; Qin & De Vries, 2008; Qin, 2012).

Taking into account the strengths of its well‐defined mechanism, the reproducible experimental data demonstrating barrier rescue in RPE cells, and the feasibility of its pharmacological modulation, AICAR represents a compelling candidate for further development in the treatment of dry AMD. With continued advances in targeted drug delivery and combination therapeutics, the prospects for leveraging AICAR’s metabolic and protective effects in the retina are promising, warranting its inclusion in the strategic pipeline for combating age‐related macular degeneration (Hyttinen et al., 2011; Qin & De Vries, 2008).

This comprehensive evaluation underscores that while challenges remain—including potential dose‐dependent effects, off‐target actions, and the need for precise modulation of AMPK isoforms—the overall body of evidence provides a solid rationale for advancing AICAR in preclinical and ultimately clinical studies focused on dry AMD. Its capacity to restore RPE barrier integrity under oxidative stress, coupled with its well‐established pharmacodynamic properties, offers a therapeutic strategy that could mitigate the progression of retinal degeneration and improve visual outcomes for patients suffering from this currently untreatable condition (Villarroel et al., 2011).
