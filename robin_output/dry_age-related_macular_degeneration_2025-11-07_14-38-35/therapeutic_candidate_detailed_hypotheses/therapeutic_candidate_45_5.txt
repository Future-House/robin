Proposal for 5-Azacytidine (Sigma A2385)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
5‐Azacytidine is a synthetically produced nucleoside analogue of cytidine originally derived by chemical modification of the natural ribonucleoside. In 5‐azacytidine the normal carbon atom at the 5‐position of the pyrimidine ring is replaced with a nitrogen atom, a modification that critically alters its biochemical properties. This compound is classified as an azanucleoside and has been widely studied as a DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) inhibitor. Its discovery and subsequent synthesis have enabled researchers to interrogate the role of DNA methylation in gene regulation. In its typical biological usage, after cellular uptake mediated by nucleoside transporters, 5‐azacytidine is phosphorylated by cellular kinases into its active triphosphate form (5‐aza‐CTP) and can become incorporated into RNA as well as into DNA, although only around 10–20% is converted to the deoxyribonucleotide form prior to DNA incorporation. This unique feature is a double‐edged sword: on one hand, it allows modulation of cytosine methylation patterns within genomic DNA by trapping DNMTs; on the other, its incorporation into RNA can perturb protein synthesis and contributes to its cytotoxic profile. Chemically, its structure and mode of synthesis place it within a well‐characterized class of antimetabolite agents whose utility extends beyond oncology into other fields where epigenetic modifications are implicated (Christman, 2002; Jasielec et al., 2014).

Therapeutic History:  
Historically, 5‐azacytidine and its deoxy analogue 5‐aza‐2′‐deoxycytidine (decitabine) were first developed as antimetabolite chemotherapy agents for hematologic malignancies. Their clinical utility in treating conditions such as myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) is well established, with DNA demethylation and tumor suppressor gene reactivation being central to their antineoplastic efficacy. Over the years, the successful repurposing of these nucleoside analogues has led to extensive studies investigating their potential effects beyond oncology. In cancer cells, the irreversible binding of DNMT enzymes to DNA following incorporation of 5‐azacytidine has been shown to produce passive demethylation during cell division and consequent gene reactivation (Christman, 2002; Seelan et al., 2018). In more recent years, researchers have begun to consider the roles of aberrant DNA methylation in non‐cancer diseases, including ocular pathologies like age‐related macular degeneration (AMD). Reviews in ocular epigenetics have noted that epigenetic dysregulation, including hypermethylation of promoters for antioxidant enzymes and other protective genes, may contribute to RPE dysfunction in AMD (Li et al., 2020). For instance, studies in cultured RPE cells and AMD cybrid models have observed tolerability of DNMT inhibitors, as exemplified by experiments where polarized RPE monolayers treated with 5‐aza‐2′‐deoxycytidine showed no significant disruption in barrier markers such as ZO‐1 or cytokeratin, and even demonstrated gene reactivation effects such as upregulation of clusterin (Li et al., 2020). Although 5‐azacytidine itself has not been as widely evaluated in ocular models as decitabine, the extensive biochemical literature surrounding its DNMT inhibitory properties forms a strong rationale for its potential repurposing toward dry AMD via epigenetic modulation.

Mechanism of Action:  
At the molecular level, 5‐azacytidine exerts its pharmacologic actions primarily through inhibition of DNMT enzymes. Under normal physiological conditions, DNMT enzymes bind to cytosine residues in CpG dinucleotides and catalyze the transfer of a methyl group from S‐adenosyl-L‐methionine (SAM) to the 5‐position carbon of cytosine, a process that is essential for the maintenance of epigenetic patterns and gene silencing. However, the substitution of nitrogen at the C5 position in 5‐azacytidine disrupts this process because the reaction mechanism relies on a β‐elimination step that cannot occur when a nitrogen is present instead of a carbon. Consequently, when 5‐azacytidine is incorporated into the DNA of replicating cells, DNMT enzymes – particularly the maintenance methyltransferase DNMT1 – are unable to complete the methylation reaction and become covalently trapped at the site of incorporation (Christman, 2002; Seelan et al., 2018). This irreversible trapping inhibits the recycling of DNMTs and leads to their proteasomal degradation, thus reducing the cellular pool of active DNMT enzymes. In subsequent rounds of DNA replication, the absence of sufficient DNMT activity results in passive demethylation of newly synthesized DNA strands that fail to be remethylated. This epigenetic reprogramming can lead to reactivation of genes with previously hypermethylated and silenced promoters (Cheishvili et al., 2015; Palii et al., 2008). It is important to note that 5‐azacytidine is also incorporated into RNA, where it may inhibit RNA methylation and alter translation; however, the therapeutic emphasis in the context of reactivating silenced genes typically focuses on its DNA‐mediated effects. In the specific context of the proposed therapeutic strategy, oxidative stress in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells is hypothesized to promote the aberrant recruitment of DNMT1 and DNMT3A to the promoters of tight junction genes such as OCLN and TJP1, thereby silencing them via hypermethylation. The administration of 5‐azacytidine is expected to reverse this process by demethylating these promoter regions through the above‐described DNMT trapping mechanism, ultimately leading to the reactivation of occludin and ZO‐1 expression (Christman, 2002; Jasielec et al., 2014; Mukhtar, 2020). Although the majority of experimental evidence in the literature focuses on gene reactivation in oncologic contexts, the fundamental biochemical interactions remain the same in any dividing cell type where DNA replication permits incorporation of the analogue. Additionally, although RPE cells in vivo are relatively quiescent compared to rapidly dividing cancer cells, in vitro models such as ARPE-19, particularly under oxidative challenge, can exhibit increased proliferation and epigenetic plasticity, thereby providing an opportunity to evaluate the mechanism in the context of barrier dysfunction (Li et al., 2020; Sarkisjan et al., 2017).

Expected Effect:  
The central hypothesis of the proposed therapeutic strategy is that under conditions of oxidative stress, DNMT enzymes are aberrantly recruited to the promoters of genes critical for tight junction integrity – specifically occludin (OCLN) and ZO-1 (TJP1) – resulting in their hypermethylation and subsequent transcriptional repression. This diminished expression of tight junction proteins compromises the barrier function of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), leading to decreased transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) and increased FITC-dextran permeability. It is expected that a treatment with 1 µM 5‐azacytidine will inhibit DNMT activity in these stressed RPE cells, specifically by demethylating the promoters of OCLN and TJP1. As the promoter regions are demethylated, transcription of OCLN and TJP1 is anticipated to resume, thereby restoring the synthesis and proper cellular localization of occludin and the ZO-1 protein. This re-expression is predicted to facilitate the re‐assembly of tight junction complexes at the plasma membrane of RPE cells, culminating in a recovery of barrier properties measurable as an increase in TEER and a reduction in paracellular permeability as evidenced by decreased FITC-dextran flux (Christman, 2002; Palii et al., 2008; Li et al., 2020). There is supporting evidence in related experimental studies wherein treatment of polarized RPE monolayers with DNMT inhibitors—albeit using 5-aza-2′-deoxycytidine in some cases—did not disturb baseline tight junction markers such as ZO-1 and cytokeratin, and even induced the expression of epigenetically silenced protective genes like clusterin (Li et al., 2020). Although direct demonstration of OCLN and TJP1 promoter demethylation by 5‐azacytidine in RPE under oxidative stress is not yet available, analogous results in other epithelial contexts provide a strong mechanistic rationale. Moreover, the dual activity of the drug – both its canonical demethylating effect and its potential to perturb RNA methylation – may synergize to enhance transcriptional reactivation if balanced appropriately with a low, non‐cytotoxic concentration such as 1 µM (Seelan et al., 2018; Cheishvili et al., 2015).

Overall Evaluation:  
The prospect of repurposing 5‐azacytidine for dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) represents a novel and intriguing approach that leverages well‐established principles in epigenetic regulation. On the strength side, the compound’s mechanism of action is thoroughly documented across multiple biomedical studies and is centered on the irreversible trapping and degradation of DNMT enzymes, leading to passive loss of DNA methylation in replicating cells (Christman, 2002; Seelan et al., 2018). This mechanism has been exploited with success in oncology to reactivate silenced tumor suppressor genes, suggesting that similar reactivation of critical genes in non‐cancerous cells might also be achievable. The specific hypothesis that oxidative stress in RPE leads to the epigenetic silencing of tight junction genes such as OCLN and TJP1 is plausible given the known role of DNMT enzymes in mediating gene repression under stress conditions, and the literature supports the concept that DNA methylation is a dynamic and reversible process that can be modulated pharmacologically (Li et al., 2020). Preliminary in vitro findings in RPE monolayer models with DNMT inhibitors, particularly the tolerability of these compounds and their capacity to upregulate epigenetically silenced genes (as seen with clusterin expression), bolster the argument that DNMT inhibition in RPE is not inherently deleterious and may indeed rectify dysfunction in barrier integrity (Li et al., 2020).

Nonetheless, several weaknesses and challenges must be recognized. First, 5‐azacytidine is known to incorporate into RNA as well as DNA, which introduces additional layers of complexity in its mode of action. The off‐target effects on RNA methylation can lead to perturbations in protein synthesis and may result in unintended consequences, especially given the highly specialized function of RPE cells in the retina (Jasielec et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2010). Second, the S-phase dependency of the drug’s mechanism—requiring active DNA replication for incorporation—poses a potential limitation when considering that in vivo RPE cells are largely quiescent. While in vitro ARPE-19 cells under oxidative stress may show increased proliferative activity, the translation of these findings to an in vivo dry AMD setting remains uncertain. Third, the global demethylation induced by 5‐azacytidine does not confer gene specificity; hence, there is a risk that while reactivating tight junction genes, the drug might also inadvertently activate promoters of deleterious genes or provoke inflammatory responses. This lack of precision could compromise retinal homeostasis or even exacerbate pathological processes if not carefully controlled (Mukhtar, 2020; Cheishvili et al., 2015). Fourth, toxicity is a major concern. While lower doses (such as 1 µM) are proposed to achieve a favorable balance between hypomethylation and cytotoxicity, the narrow therapeutic window observed in oncology suggests that careful titration and extensive in vitro and in vivo safety studies are essential before progressing to clinical evaluation in AMD (Christman, 2002; Daifuku, 2025).

Additional challenges include the need for direct experimental evidence to support the specific hypothesis relating to tight junction reactivation. Although the general effects of DNMT inhibitors in reactivating silenced genes are well documented, there is currently insufficient evidence demonstrating that 5-azacytidine directly demethylates the promoters of OCLN and TJP1 in RPE cells subjected to oxidative stress. Moreover, evaluations of barrier function—such as detailed TEER measurements or FITC-dextran permeability assays—have been more commonly reported in the context of other DNMT inhibitors such as 5-aza-2′-deoxycytidine rather than 5-azacytidine per se (Li et al., 2020). Therefore, a critical gap remains in validating whether the proposed epigenetic precision approach can indeed restore tight junction integrity in a physiologically relevant model of dry AMD.

Despite these concerns, the overall evaluation is cautiously optimistic. The robust and well‐understood mechanism of 5‐azacytidine in inducing DNA demethylation provides a solid theoretical basis for the proposed therapeutic strategy. Given that similar epigenetic interventions have successfully reactivated gene expression in other cellular contexts, and considering that preliminary data in RPE systems suggest tolerability and potential gene reactivation, it is reasonable to propose that further investigation is warranted. Initial preclinical studies should focus on establishing the methylation status of the OCLN and TJP1 promoters in RPE cells under oxidative stress and then determine whether 5‐azacytidine treatment can effectively reverse these modifications. Comprehensive analyses including methylation-specific polymerase chain reaction (MSP), chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays for DNMT occupancy, and detailed transcriptional profiling would be essential to confirm target engagement. Parallel functional assays, such as TEER measurements and FITC-dextran permeability assays, must be undertaken to correlate molecular events with phenotypic restoration of barrier function (Mukhtar, 2020; Daifuku, 2025).

In addition, it would be prudent to perform dose–response studies to delineate the optimal concentration of 5‐azacytidine that maximizes promoter demethylation and gene reactivation while minimizing off-target cytotoxicity. Considering that RPE cells in vitro may exhibit different proliferative rates compared to their in vivo counterparts, alternative experimental models, such as induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived RPE cells from AMD patients, may provide additional insights into the therapeutic window and efficacy of 5‐azacytidine in a clinically relevant context. Finally, given that the global nature of epigenetic reprogramming may lead to unintended activation of other cellular pathways, combination strategies employing lower doses of 5‐azacytidine alongside more targeted epigenetic modulators—such as selective HDAC inhibitors—could be explored to enhance the specificity of gene reactivation and improve overall retinal safety (Li et al., 2020; Nashine & Kenney, 2020).

In summary, 5‐azacytidine represents a promising candidate for repurposing in the treatment of dry AMD through a novel epigenetic precision approach aimed at reversing the aberrant methylation of tight junction gene promoters. Its well‐characterized mechanism of DNMT inhibition and widespread use in oncology offer a strong biological rationale. However, significant challenges remain, including its non‐selective incorporation into nucleic acids, potential off‐target effects, reliance on cellular proliferation for optimal activity, and a narrow therapeutic index that necessitates rigorous dose optimization. The feasibility of restoring tight junction integrity in RPE cells via demethylation of OCLN and TJP1 promoters is theoretically sound yet awaits empirical confirmation in preclinical AMD models. Overall, while the candidate has substantial mechanistic strengths that support its potential role in restoring RPE barrier function under oxidative stress conditions, its development as a treatment for dry AMD will require detailed molecular and functional validations, careful safety profiling, and perhaps the exploration of combination therapies to harness its benefits while mitigating risks (Christman, 2002; Jasielec et al., 2014; Li et al., 2020; Daifuku, 2025).
