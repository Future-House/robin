Proposal for 4-Octyl Itaconate (4-OI; Sigma SML2192)

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
4‐Octyl Itaconate (4‐OI) is a cell‐permeable derivative of itaconate, a naturally occurring immunometabolite generated in activated macrophages during inflammatory responses. 4‐OI has been synthesized by chemically modifying itaconate to improve its lipophilicity and enhance its cellular uptake, which in turn facilitates its electrophilic reactivity. This improved cell permeability enables 4‐OI to effectively modulate intracellular targets such as Keap1. As a member of the electrophilic compounds class, 4‐OI belongs to a group of therapeutics that are designed to exploit the redox-sensing molecular machinery of cells. Historically, compounds in this class have been used to induce antioxidant and cytoprotective responses by activating the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway. In its commercial form (Sigma SML2192), 4‐OI offers a reproducible and standardized reagent for preclinical studies and drug development, making it an attractive candidate for repurposing in conditions where oxidative stress plays a central pathophysiological role, such as dry age-related macular degeneration (AMD) (Camelo, 2025, pp. 4–6; Fu et al., 2021, pp. 3–6).

The therapeutic candidate is particularly intriguing given the significant role of oxidative stress and inflammation in the pathogenesis of dry AMD, especially within the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). The RPE is highly susceptible to oxidative damage due to its high metabolic demand, continuous exposure to light, and abundance of polyunsaturated fatty acids. Dysregulation of the redox balance in these cells has been directly tied to barrier dysfunction, including loss of transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) and increased permeability to molecules such as FITC-dextran. By design, 4‐OI aims to exploit its electrophilic nature to alkylate reactive cysteine residues on the Keap1 protein, thereby liberating Nrf2 and triggering its stabilization and nuclear translocation. Once in the nucleus, Nrf2 upregulates various antioxidant genes and possibly genes that maintain tight junction integrity, thus promoting cytoprotection and barrier restoration. This profile makes 4‐OI a promising candidate in the context of dry AMD, where restoration of RPE barrier function is a critical therapeutic goal (Camelo, 2025, pp. 4–6; Cai et al., 2021, pp. 5–6).

Therapeutic History:  
The use of itaconate derivatives, including 4‐OI, has been widely investigated across several biomedical fields, particularly in studies focused on modulating the cellular response to oxidative stress and inflammation. Preclinical studies in various cell types have demonstrated that 4‐OI can potently activate the Nrf2 pathway, leading to increased transcription of phase II detoxification enzymes and antioxidant proteins. For instance, in human retinal pigment epithelium cells exposed to angiotensin II (Ang II), 4‐OI was shown to significantly reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels while concomitantly enhancing superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity. Such findings provide concrete evidence of its ROS-scavenging potential and anti-inflammatory efficacy (Fu et al., 2021, pp. 3–6).

Outside the ocular context, 4‐OI has been evaluated in models of renal ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI), where it was observed to reduce tubular injury scores, lower inflammatory cytokine levels, and improve overall kidney function through activation of the Nrf2 pathway. Similar protective effects have been recorded in lung transplantation models under cold ischemia/reperfusion conditions, wherein 4‐OI treatment improved epithelial cell viability and donor lung function (Gao et al., 2025, pp. 1–2; Li et al., 2023, pp. 3–5, 9–11). The mechanism underlying these benefits consistently centers on the electrophilic activation of the Nrf2 pathway via alkylation of Keap1 cysteines.

Regarding ocular diseases, while the direct use of 4‐OI in dry AMD has yet to be fully evaluated in clinical settings, several studies have established the general importance of the Keap1/Nrf2 axis in maintaining retinal health. Agents that stimulate this pathway, including dimethyl fumarate, quercetin, and various itaconate derivatives, have shown promising results in protecting RPE cells from oxidative and inflammatory insults (Camelo, 2025, pp. 4–7). Preliminary investigations using 4‐OI in RPE cell models have indicated improvements in antioxidant responses and cell viability, suggesting that compounds of this class may offer therapeutic benefits similar to those seen in other tissues challenged by oxidative stress (Fu et al., 2021, pp. 6–8).

In addition, the therapeutic history of similar electrophilic Nrf2 activators highlights their dual role both in mitigating inflammatory responses and in bolstering barrier functions via tight junction stabilization. This is particularly relevant when considering the pathogenesis of dry AMD, which is marked by compromised RPE barrier integrity and chronic oxidative injury (Cai et al., 2021, pp. 5–6; Falcão et al., 2025, p. 20). Thus, although the direct clinical application of 4‐OI in AMD is still in its early stages, the compound has a well-documented history in preclinical models of oxidative injury and barrier dysfunction, which supports its repurposing for dry AMD.

Mechanism of Action:  
The central mechanism by which 4‐OI exerts its therapeutic effects is through its electrophilic modification of Keap1, a regulatory protein that normally sequesters Nrf2 in the cytoplasm and promotes its proteasomal degradation. Under basal conditions, Keap1 binds to Nrf2 and facilitates its ubiquitination, thereby maintaining low intracellular levels of Nrf2. 4‐OI covalently modifies key cysteine residues on Keap1, which disrupts the Keap1/Nrf2 interaction and prevents the ubiquitination and subsequent degradation of Nrf2. This post-translational modification allows Nrf2 to accumulate in the cytoplasm and eventually translocate into the nucleus (Camelo, 2025, pp. 4–6; Fu et al., 2021, pp. 3–6).

Once in the nucleus, Nrf2 heterodimerizes with small Maf proteins and binds to the antioxidant response element (ARE) located in the promoter region of various cytoprotective genes. This binding leads to the transcriptional activation of genes encoding a host of antioxidant enzymes and protective proteins, including heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), NAD(P)H quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1), and various glutathione biosynthetic enzymes. Through this coordinated induction of antioxidant defenses, cells can effectively neutralize ROS, restore redox homeostasis, and mitigate the harmful effects of chronic oxidative stress (Fu et al., 2021, pp. 3–6; Camelo, 2025, pp. 6–7).

In addition to its antioxidant role, Nrf2 activation has been implicated in the regulation of inflammatory signaling pathways. By promoting the expression of anti-inflammatory mediators and by interfering with the nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) pathway—through competition for transcriptional co-activators—Nrf2 activation can lead to decreased secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8 (Camelo, 2025, pp. 4–7). It is worth noting that the effects of 4‐OI on inflammatory cytokine production can be dose-dependent; while low concentrations typically suppress cytokine transcription and inflammation, higher doses have been associated with unintended pro-inflammatory outcomes, such as enhanced IL-1β release and inflammatory apoptosis in certain cell types (Muri et al., 2020, pp. 2–3, 5–7). Such dual effects necessitate careful titration of the dose to ensure that the therapeutic window is maximized for protective outcomes while minimizing potential cytotoxicity.

Furthermore, evidence from studies on endothelial cells and Caco-2 monolayers indicates that similar electrophilic Nrf2 activators can protect and restore barrier function, as measured by transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER). In these models, activation of Nrf2 has been linked to the upregulation of tight junction proteins and the maintenance of paracellular integrity (Cai et al., 2021, pp. 5–6; Gao et al., 2025, pp. 1–2). Although direct studies of TEER in RPE cells treated with 4‐OI are limited, the underlying molecular logic suggests a similar beneficial impact on RPE barrier function. This is particularly relevant given the hypothesis that 4‐OI treatment will restore TEER and decrease FITC-dextran permeability in oxidative stress or A2E plus blue-light injury models by both reducing oxidative stress and enhancing the expression of tight junction proteins that are regulated by Nrf2 (Camelo, 2025, pp. 6–7; Fu et al., 2021, pp. 3–6).

Additionally, the specificity of 4‐OI for Keap1’s cysteine residues, along with its favorable cell permeability, positions the compound as a modulatory switch that can trigger a host of downstream protective pathways. This multifaceted mechanism of action, encompassing direct antioxidant effects, anti-inflammatory activity, and preservation of barrier function, underpins the potential clinical utility of 4‐OI in conditions marked by oxidative damage and barrier dysfunction, such as dry AMD (Camelo, 2025, pp. 4–6; Fu et al., 2021, pp. 3–6).

Expected Effect:  
The proposed hypothesis for employing 4‐OI in dry AMD is that by alkylating Keap1 cysteines in RPE, it will stabilize and induce the nuclear accumulation of Nrf2, thereby upregulating antioxidant defenses and tight junction proteins. The expected outcome in an in vitro model is a restoration of TEER and a decrease in FITC-dextran permeability under conditions of oxidative stress or when RPE cells are challenged with A2E and blue-light exposure. Precedent for this predicted effect is found in studies where 4‐OI’s electrophilic activation of Nrf2 has led to robust antioxidant responses, including the upregulation of genes encoding detoxifying enzymes and the suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Camelo, 2025, pp. 6–7; Fu et al., 2021, pp. 3–6).

Specifically, the anticipated activation of Nrf2 in RPE cells is expected to result in increased expression of key cytoprotective genes such as HO-1 and NQO1, which play crucial roles in combating ROS-mediated cellular damage. In addition, Nrf2 activation has been linked to the expression of genes associated with maintaining tight junction integrity—an essential aspect of the RPE’s barrier function (Camelo, 2025, pp. 4–6; Cai et al., 2021, pp. 5–6). Given that reduced TEER and increased FITC-dextran permeability are hallmarks of RPE barrier disruption in dry AMD, the modulation of these parameters by 4‐OI is a central goal of the proposed intervention.

Experimental data from studies in other cell types, such as endothelial cells challenged with endotoxin, have shown that electrophilic Nrf2 activators can rescue TEER to levels exceeding 95%, suggesting that similar outcomes may be achievable in RPE cells (Camelo, 2025, pp. 4–6; Cai et al., 2021, pp. 5–6). In parallel, research using 4‐OI in human primary RPE models has demonstrated a reduction in ROS production, decreased inflammation, and improved cellular viability in the context of oxidative stress induced by agents like angiotensin II (Fu et al., 2021, pp. 3–6, 7–8). These findings lend credence to the expectation that 4‐OI will counteract A2E plus blue-light-induced oxidative damage in RPE cells, thereby reestablishing normal barrier properties.

Moreover, given that oxidative stress and inflammation are intimately intertwined in the progression of dry AMD, the multitargeted action of 4‐OI is expected to yield a comprehensive protective effect. By dampening inflammatory cytokine release (including IL-6, IL-8, and MCP-1) and reducing oxidative biomarkers such as malondialdehyde, 4‐OI should mitigate the deleterious microenvironment that contributes to RPE dysfunction (Camelo, 2025, pp. 6–7; Fu et al., 2021, pp. 3–6). The net result would be a restoration of cellular homeostasis, improved barrier integrity, and a reduction in the secondary effects of chronic oxidative injury, which collectively translate into a potential therapeutic benefit for dry AMD.

Finally, the anticipated outcomes are supported by the observation that Nrf2 is expressed in RPE cells and is critical for their survival under stress conditions (Camelo, 2025, pp. 4–6; Cai et al., 2021, pp. 2–3). Activation of Nrf2 by 4‐OI, therefore, is expected to counteract the pathophysiological mechanisms that precipitate RPE degeneration in dry AMD. These effects could be quantified both by measuring restored TEER values as a proxy for tight junction integrity and by assessing decreased FITC-dextran permeability as an indicator of improved barrier function (Cai et al., 2021, pp. 5–6; Fu et al., 2021, pp. 3–6).

Overall Evaluation:  
The overall evaluation of 4‐Octyl Itaconate as a therapeutic candidate for dry AMD is decidedly positive, though not without certain challenges that must be addressed in subsequent investigations. One of the most significant strengths of 4‐OI is its strong mechanistic rationale: by targeting Keap1 via electrophilic alkylation, 4‐OI effectively liberates Nrf2, triggering a cascade of antioxidant and cytoprotective responses that are crucial for maintaining RPE health. This mode of action is consistent with a broad body of preclinical evidence across several tissue models, including renal, pulmonary, and endothelial systems (Gao et al., 2025, pp. 1–2; Li et al., 2023, pp. 3–5, 9–11). The ability of 4‐OI to restore barrier function, as evidenced by its reported rescue of TEER in endotoxin-challenged endothelial cells, provides an additional layer of validation for its potential use in RPE models, where barrier dysfunction is a hallmark of dry AMD (Camelo, 2025, pp. 4–6; Cai et al., 2021, pp. 5–6).

From a therapeutic history perspective, the repurposing of 4‐OI is supported by its documented efficacy in reducing oxidative stress and inflammation in multiple preclinical studies. Its effects have been observed in various experimental systems that mimic the cellular and molecular events underpinning dry AMD, including the activation of the Nrf2 pathway, downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and protection against ROS-induced apoptosis (Fu et al., 2021, pp. 3–6, 7–8). Although direct clinical evidence in dry AMD models is limited, the robust preclinical data from related systems and the well-established role of the Keap1/Nrf2 pathway in retinal health lend substantial support to the hypothesis that 4‐OI could be effective in ameliorating RPE degeneration.

Mechanistically, 4‐OI offers several advantages. Its specificity for modifying Keap1 and thereby stabilizing Nrf2 is well-documented, and its integration into cellular signaling networks that govern both oxidative stress responses and inflammatory processes is compelling. The upregulation of phase II detoxification enzymes and tight junction proteins following Nrf2 activation suggests that 4‐OI could address multiple pathogenic features of dry AMD simultaneously. However, a note of caution is warranted: studies have indicated that 4‐OI’s pharmacological profile is dose-dependent, and high concentrations may lead to paradoxical effects, such as increased IL-1β release and pro-inflammatory apoptosis. These findings underscore the importance of careful dose optimization and thorough toxicological evaluation in further preclinical work (Muri et al., 2020, pp. 2–3, 5–7).

Another strength lies in the compound’s favorable safety profile in murine models. Studies in renal IRI and lung transplantation models have demonstrated that 4‐OI is not only efficacious but also well-tolerated, with no significant adverse effects observed at therapeutic doses (Gao et al., 2025, pp. 1–2; Li et al., 2023, pp. 3–5). This bodes well for its potential translation to ocular applications, where safety is a paramount concern given the delicate nature of retinal tissues. Additionally, the capacity of 4‐OI to achieve near-complete restoration of TEER in other epithelial models suggests that similar barrier-protective effects might be replicated in RPE cells, a hypothesis that is reinforced by the known importance of Nrf2 in maintaining tight junction integrity (Camelo, 2025, pp. 4–6; Cai et al., 2021, pp. 5–6).

Despite these strengths, there are some notable limitations. Direct evidence of 4‐OI’s efficacy in RPE-specific models of dry AMD remains sparse, and most of the supporting data are extrapolated from studies in other cell types or in non-ocular systems. As such, while the mechanistic rationale is strong, further in vitro and in vivo studies specifically focused on the retinal context are essential to confirm that the anticipated effects—namely, enhancement of TEER and reduction of FITC-dextran permeability—can be robustly achieved in RPE cells under oxidative stress or A2E plus blue-light injury conditions (Fu et al., 2021, pp. 3–6; Fu et al., 2021, pp. 6–7).

Moreover, the dual activity of 4‐OI, wherein low doses are beneficial while high doses might provoke inflammatory responses, presents a potential challenge for clinical translation. This necessitates precise pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic studies to define the optimal dosing regimen that maximizes therapeutic benefits while circumventing adverse effects. Such dose–response studies will be critical not only in cell-based assays but also in animal models that reliably reproduce the pathology of dry AMD (Muri et al., 2020, pp. 2–3, 5–7).

Another consideration is the complexity of dry AMD as a multifactorial disease. While oxidative stress and inflammation are key drivers, other pathological processes such as complement activation, cellular senescence, and genetic predispositions also play significant roles in disease progression. As a result, a monotherapeutic approach solely based on Nrf2 activation may need to be complemented by additional interventions targeting these other pathways. Nonetheless, the central role of oxidative stress in RPE degeneration makes the Nrf2 pathway a critical node, and therapeutics like 4‐OI that modulate this pathway could form a valuable component of combination therapies for dry AMD (Cai et al., 2021, pp. 2–3; Camelo, 2025, pp. 4–6).

In summary, 4‐Octyl Itaconate presents a compelling therapeutic candidate for dry age-related macular degeneration based on its well-characterized mechanism of inducing Nrf2 activation through electrophilic alkylation of Keap1, its established preclinical efficacy in reducing oxidative stress and inflammation in multiple tissues, and its documented ability to restore barrier function as evidenced by improvements in TEER in other cellular models. The compound’s synthetic design ensures its effective cellular delivery, and its use in non-ocular models provides a strong preclinical foundation for its repurposing in AMD. However, the translation of these effects to the RPE and the ocular environment requires further targeted research. Critical next steps include dose optimization studies to avoid pro-inflammatory effects, rigorous validation in RPE cell culture models under oxidative challenge (such as A2E plus blue-light injury), and subsequent in vivo evaluations in animal models of dry AMD.

Overall, the repurposing of 4‐OI as a novel Nrf2-centered approach to reinforce RPE junctions and mitigate oxidative stress in dry AMD is promising. Its strengths include a solid mechanistic rationale, demonstrated efficacy in modulating key cellular stress pathways, and evidence of barrier protective effects in analogous cell systems. Conversely, its weaknesses center on the current paucity of direct RPE or ocular data, the potential for dose-dependent adverse effects, and the inherent complexity of dry AMD pathophysiology requiring possibly multimodal therapeutic interventions. With these considerations in mind, 4‐Octyl Itaconate merits further investigation as part of a comprehensive drug development strategy for dry AMD, with the goal of establishing robust preclinical evidence to support its advancement toward clinical trials (Camelo, 2025, pp. 4–7; Cai et al., 2021, pp. 5–6; Fu et al., 2021, pp. 3–6).
