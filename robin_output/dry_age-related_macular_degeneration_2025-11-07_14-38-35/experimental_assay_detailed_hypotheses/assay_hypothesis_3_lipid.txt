Assay Overview  
The lipid-peroxidation assay uses the ratiometric probe BODIPY-C11 in cultured retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells. Upon oxidation of its polyunsaturated butadienyl segment, BODIPY-C11 shifts its fluorescence emission from red (~590 nm) toward green (~510 nm). In practice, human RPE cells (e.g. ARPE-19 or primary human iPSC-derived RPE) are pre‐loaded with the probe, then challenged acutely with a pro‐oxidant such as tert-butyl hydroperoxide (t-BHP). Live‐cell confocal or high‐content imaging (or plate‐reader assays) quantify the green∶red fluorescence ratio as a direct measure of lipid peroxidation. Drug candidates—especially antioxidants or redox modulators—are co-incubated or pre-treated to assess their ability to attenuate the BODIPY-C11 oxidation signal.  

Biomedical Evidence  
Oxidative damage to RPE lipids is central in dry AMD pathogenesis. Clinically, RPE cells over decades accumulate photo‐oxidative and mitochondrial ROS, leading to peroxidized phospholipids, malondialdehyde (MDA), and 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) adducts observed in Bruch’s membrane and drusen. Genome‐wide and biochemical studies (AREDS trials, complement genetics) implicate chronic oxidative stress as a driving force for RPE dysfunction, complement activation, and local inflammation. Histopathology of human donor eyes with geographic atrophy shows elevated lipid peroxidation markers in RPE and subretinal deposits, correlating with RPE atrophy and photoreceptor loss. Preclinical models (e.g. Nrf2-knockout mice) further demonstrate that impaired antioxidant defenses in RPE accelerate AMD‐like pathology.  

Previous Use  
First described in RPE by Sehgal et al. (Invest Ophth Vis Sci 2014), the BODIPY-C11/t-BHP system has been employed to:  
• Screen small‐molecule antioxidants (e.g. Nrf2 activators, phase II enzyme inducers) for cytoprotective potential.  
• Dissect mitochondrial versus NADPH oxidase–derived ROS by combining BODIPY-C11 with mitochondrial probes.  
• Validate genetic targets (e.g. overexpression of superoxide dismutase 2 or glutathione peroxidase 4) that modulate lipid peroxidation in RPE.  
Key discoveries include the demonstration that Nrf2 pathway activation robustly lowers lipid peroxidation in RPE and that certain lipid kinases regulate basal ROS handling, insights that have guided early‐stage AMD therapeutic research.  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths:  
• Disease relevance: Models a central pathogenic event—lipid peroxidation in RPE—linked to drusen formation and RPE atrophy.  
• Quantitative, high‐content readout: Ratiometric BODIPY-C11 signals minimize well-to-well variability and enable real‐time live‐cell monitoring.  
• Throughput: Adaptable to 96- or 384-well formats for small‐molecule libraries or genetic screens.  
• Versatility: Can be combined with mitochondrial or lysosomal probes to dissect subcellular ROS sources.  

Weaknesses:  
• Acute insult model: t-BHP generates non-physiological peroxide bursts, lacking the chronic, low‐grade oxidative stress seen in AMD.  
• Simplified biology: Does not recapitulate drusen biology, complement activation, RPE–photoreceptor interactions, or inflammatory cell recruitment.  
• Cell‐line limitations: ARPE-19 may differ substantially from polarized, pigmented primary or iPSC-derived RPE in antioxidant capacity and lipid metabolism.  
• End‐point specificity: BODIPY-C11 reports lipid oxidation broadly but cannot distinguish specific peroxidation products (e.g. HNE vs MDA adducts) that have distinct downstream signaling effects.  

Conclusion  
The BODIPY-C11/t-BHP lipid peroxidation assay provides a robust, quantitative platform to evaluate antioxidant and redox-modulating compounds in RPE cells and is well aligned with a key mechanistic axis in dry AMD. However, translating hits from this acute oxidative-stress model to in vivo efficacy will require complementary assays that capture chronic stress, complement dysregulation, and RPE–photoreceptor physiology.
