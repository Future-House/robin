Assay Overview  
• Disease aspect modeled: chronic oxidative‐stress–induced mitochondrial dysfunction in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), a central feature of dry age‐related macular degeneration (AMD). Lipofuscin bisretinoid A2E accumulates in RPE lysosomes with age; upon blue‐light exposure it photooxidizes, generates ROS, and triggers mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) collapse.  
• Readouts: ΔΨm is quantified via fluorescent potentiometric dyes—JC-1 (ratiometric green/aggregate red emission) or TMRE/TMRM (single‐channel accumulation)—by microplate reader, high‐content imager or flow cytometry. A drug that preserves or restores dye accumulation indicates ΔΨm protection. Optional multiplex: measure cell viability (resazurin), ROS (DCFDA) or ATP levels in same wells.  
• Cell model: human ARPE-19 cells (widely used, though lower‐passage primary human RPE or iPSC-derived RPE can be substituted for improved physiological relevance). Cells are loaded with A2E (20–50 µM, 6–24 h), washed, then exposed to blue‐light (430–480 nm) for defined periods (e.g. 10–30 min) to elicit photooxidation and ΔΨm loss.  

Biomedical Evidence  
• Mitochondrial dysfunction is well established in dry AMD pathogenesis. Histological studies of postmortem AMD eyes show swollen mitochondria in RPE, decreased cristae density, and impaired oxidative phosphorylation (Feher et al., 2006; Jarrett et al., 2008).  
• Genetic and biomarker studies link AMD to oxidative‐stress pathways (e.g. polymorphisms in SOD2) and complement activation that amplify ROS‐mediated mitochondrial damage (Lambert et al., 2016 review).  
• Clinically, patients exhibit reduced macular pigment and lower ATP production in perifoveal retina (Wang et al., 2016), and early‐phase trials of elamipretide (a mitochondria‐targeted peptide) report improved visual function, underscoring mitochondria as a therapeutic target (Campochiaro et al., 2019).  
• A2E‐mediated photooxidation recapitulates key features of dry AMD: chronic lipofuscin accumulation, blue‐light sensitivity, ROS burst, and subsequent RPE atrophy (Sparrow et al., 2012). ΔΨm loss is an early event in this cascade and correlates with caspase activation and cell death.  

Previous Use  
• Lu et al. (Free Radic. Biol. Med. 2016) validated JC-1 in ARPE-19 + A2E/light to screen antioxidants; they identified compounds that preserved ΔΨm, reduced ROS, and protected barrier function.  
• Sparrow and colleagues have used TMRE-based assays in primary human RPE to demonstrate photooxidation kinetics of A2E and screen Nrf2 activators.  
• Mitochondrial protective agents (e.g. SS-31/elamipretide) were profiled in similar assays, revealing dose–response curves for ΔΨm rescue prior to moving into ex vivo and in vivo models.  
• High‐throughput adaptations on 384-well formats enable screening of small‐molecule libraries for novel mitochondrial stabilizers in RPE.  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
• Direct, quantitative readout of a hallmark AMD pathology (mitochondrial collapse).  
• Amenable to medium/high‐throughput screening; multiplexable with viability and ROS assays.  
• Uses human RPE lines; can be upgraded to iPSC‐RPE for patient‐specific studies.  
• Rapid assay window (hours), facilitating lead triage.  

Weaknesses  
• ARPE-19 immortalized line has lower mitochondrial reserve vs. native RPE; primary/iPSC‐RPE recommended for higher fidelity.  
• A2E photooxidation is only one oxidative‐stress source; dry AMD is multifactorial (complement dysregulation, lipid accumulation, inflammation).  
• ΔΨm preservation may not translate directly to long‐term cell survival or function (phagocytosis, polarized secretion).  
• Light‐exposure conditions must be tightly controlled to avoid phototoxic artifacts.  

Conclusion  
The mitochondrial membrane potential assay (JC-1/TMRE in A2E-loaded RPE) is a well‐validated, disease‐relevant in vitro platform for identifying and characterizing mitochondrial protective agents in dry AMD. It excels at medium‐throughput lead selection but should be integrated into a tiered strategy—including phagocytosis, inflammatory and complement‐activation assays—to fully capture the multifactorial nature of dry AMD.
