Assay Overview  
• Goal: Quantify autophagic flux in RPE under AMD‐relevant stress and rescue by candidate therapeutics.  
• Reporter: Stably transduced human RPE cells (e.g. ARPE-19, primary or iPSC-derived RPE) expressing mCherry-GFP-LC3 tandem construct (Mizushima et al., Nat Protoc 2010). GFP signal is quenched in the acidic autolysosome, while mCherry persists, allowing discrimination of autophagosomes (GFP+ mCherry+, “yellow”) versus autolysosomes (GFP– mCherry+, “red”).  
• Stressors: A2E or lipofuscin loading, blue-light irradiation, H₂O₂ or inflammatory cytokines to mimic dry AMD–associated oxidative and lipofuscin stress.  
• Readouts: High-content imaging or flow cytometry in 96- or 384-well format. Endpoints include number and size of yellow vs. red puncta per cell, red/yellow ratio as a flux metric, and total cell viability.  
• Throughput: Medium to high—amenable to multiwell screening of small-molecule or genetic libraries.  

Biomedical Evidence  
• Centrality of autophagy in RPE homeostasis: RPE phagocytose ~10% of photoreceptor outer segments (POS) daily. Autophagy and lysosomal pathways clear POS debris, damaged organelles and aggregated proteins (Kaarniranta et al., Prog Ret Eye Res 2011).  
• Age-related decline: Aging and AMD donor eyes exhibit decreased LC3-II levels, p62/SQSTM1 accumulation, lysosomal dysfunction, and enlarged lipofuscin granules (Mitter et al., J Biol Chem 2014; Cao et al., Invest Ophthalmol Vis Sci 2019).  
• Genetic and clinical links: Polymorphisms in autophagy genes (ATG5, ULK1) modulate AMD risk; decreased autophagic capacity correlates with drusen load and RPE atrophy in patients (Zhang et al., Hum Mol Genet 2015; Ferrington et al., Wiley Interdiscip Rev Dev Biol 2016).  
• Pharmacological modulation: mTOR inhibition (rapamycin) or TFEB activation restores autophagic flux, reduces lipofuscin, and preserves RPE function in animal models of dry AMD (Mitter et al., J Biol Chem 2014; Appelqvist et al., Autophagy 2013).  

Previous Use  
• Drug discovery in neurodegeneration and cancer: The mCherry-GFP-LC3 reporter has been widely used to screen autophagy modulators in neuronal and tumor cell lines (Mizushima et al., Nat Protoc 2010; Pankiv et al., J Biol Chem 2007).  
• RPE applications: Mitter et al. used this tandem reporter in ARPE-19 cells loaded with A2E to demonstrate that A2E impairs autophagic flux and that rapamycin or trehalose rescues flux and reduces cell death (J Biol Chem 2014).  
• Key discoveries: Identification of TFEB as a master regulator of RPE lysosomal biogenesis; demonstration that lysosomal alkalinization by chloroquine analogs exacerbates flux deficits, validating acidification as a therapeutic target (Wong et al., Autophagy 2016).  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
1. Direct, quantitative measure of autophagic flux rather than static LC3‐II accumulation.  
2. Plate‐based, scalable format suitable for mid‐ to high‐throughput screening.  
3. Physiologically relevant cell type when using primary or iPSC-derived RPE.  
4. Multi‐parametric readouts (flux, puncta morphology, viability) enable mechanistic profiling.  

Weaknesses  
1. Reporter overexpression may perturb basal autophagy; requires careful controls.  
2. ARPE-19 cells have limited fidelity to native RPE—primary or iPSC-RPE validation needed.  
3. 2D culture omits interactions with Bruch’s membrane and immune cells, limiting pathophysiological complexity.  
4. Autophagy modulation may yield context-dependent effects—overactivation can trigger autophagic cell death.  

Conclusion  
The mCherry-GFP-LC3 autophagic flux assay in RPE offers a robust, disease‐relevant platform for screening and mechanism‐of‐action studies of candidate dry AMD therapeutics targeting autophagy–lysosome pathways. Its predictive value will be enhanced by validation in primary or iPSC-derived RPE and complementary assays (e.g., POS phagocytosis, lysosomal pH measurement).
