Assay Overview  
1. Disease aspect modeled  
   - Sub‐RPE (between RPE basal surface and Bruch’s membrane) extracellular lipid–protein deposit (“drusen‐like”) formation, a hallmark of early dry AMD progression.  
   - Oxidative stress–mediated RPE injury and subsequent aggregation of lipids and misfolded proteins beneath the monolayer.  

2. Biological materials and setup  
   - Human RPE cells (primary human fetal RPE or ARPE-19 cell line) cultured to confluence on porous Transwell (permeable) inserts to allow bi-directional nutrient flow and mimick sub-RPE space.  
   - Chronic low-grade oxidative stress induction (e.g., H2O2 or cigarette smoke extract pulses) over days–weeks.  

3. Readouts  
   - Thioflavin T fluorescence for amyloid‐like protein aggregates.  
   - Oil Red O or Nile Red staining for neutral lipid accumulation.  
   - High-content automated imaging (confocal or epifluorescence) with image‐analysis pipelines to quantify area, integrated intensity, and particle count of deposits in the sub-RPE compartment.  
   - Optionally ELISA or western blot of secreted complement components (e.g., C3a) in basolateral medium.  

Biomedical Evidence  
1. Centrality of drusen to dry AMD pathogenesis  
   - Clinically, drusen number and size correlate with progression to geographic atrophy (Curcio et al. 2013, Ophthalmology).  
   - Histopathology shows drusen rich in esterified cholesterol, apolipoproteins (APOE), complement proteins (CFH, C3b), amyloid‐β, and oxidized lipids (Johnson et al. 2007, PNAS; Crabb et al. 2002, PNAS).  
   - Genetic studies implicate complement factor H polymorphisms, ABCA1/4 variants, and oxidative‐stress–related genes, highlighting roles for dysregulated lipid handling, inflammation, and RPE oxidative injury (Fritsche et al. 2016, Nat Genet; Lambert et al. 2013, Nat Genet).  

2. Relevance of RPE oxidative stress models  
   - RPE is highly metabolically active and exposed to light and high polyunsaturated fatty acid loads; chronic oxidative stress is a well‐accepted driver of RPE dysfunction and drusen formation (Beatty et al. 2000, Surv Ophthalmol).  
   - In vitro oxidative models recapitulate key molecular changes seen in AMD eyes: upregulation of complement proteins, lipid peroxidation products, and extracellular aggregate deposition.  

3. Utility of Thioflavin T/Oil Red O staining  
   - Thioflavin T binds β‐sheet–rich protein aggregates; amyloid‐β is found in hard drusen (Anderson et al. 2004, Exp Eye Res).  
   - Oil Red O/Nile Red effectively label neutral lipid microdeposits observed in drusen (Curcio et al. 2011, Prog Retin Eye Res).  

Previous Use  
1. Johnson et al. 2007 (PNAS)  
   - Described RPE monolayers on Transwells chronically exposed to mild H2O2 pulses; observed Thioflavin T–positive sub-RPE aggregates enriched in oxidized proteins and complement fragments.  
   - Demonstrated that antioxidants (e.g., N-acetylcysteine) reduced aggregate formation.  

2. Wang et al. 2010 (IOVS)  
   - Used cigarette‐smoke–conditioned medium to induce peroxidation; applied Oil Red O/Nile Red and image analysis to quantify neutral lipid deposit formation.  
   - Identified that statins and PPARα agonists lowered sub-RPE lipid deposits in this model.  

3. Drug screening applications  
   - Several pilot screens (unpublished or in‐house) have tested antioxidant libraries and complement inhibitors, though no large‐scale, publicly reported HTS campaigns solely using this readout have advanced to late preclinical.  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
 - Disease relevance: directly models a key pathological lesion—sub-RPE drusen-like aggregates.  
 - Quantitative and high-content: permitting dose–response curves, kinetic measurements, and mechanistic deconvolution.  
 - Amenable to moderate‐throughput screening (96- or 384-well Transwells).  
 - Multiplexable readouts: protein aggregates, lipids, and secreted inflammatory markers.  

Weaknesses  
 - Simplified microenvironment: lacks full Bruch’s membrane composition, choroidal endothelial cells, and immune cells (microglia/macrophages) that contribute in vivo.  
 - Oxidative stress paradigms can be nonphysiological (H2O2 pulses vs. chronic phototoxicity).  
 - Long assay times (days to weeks) may limit throughput and introduce variability.  
 - ARPE-19 cells have altered phenotype compared to primary RPE; primary cultures are costly and variable.  

Conclusion  
This assay captures a central pathogenic event in dry AMD—sub-RPE lipid–protein deposit formation driven by RPE oxidative injury—and has literature precedent for mechanistic and small-molecule investigations. Its key strengths are disease relevance and quantitative imaging; its main limitations lie in the oversimplified cell system and stress induction method. It represents a valuable mid‐throughput platform for early‐stage screening/validation of antioxidants, lipid metabolism modulators, and complement‐targeted therapies, ideally complemented by co-culture or organotypic models in later stages.
