Assay Overview  
The RPE–Endothelial Co-Culture Barrier Test models the outer blood–retina barrier by co-culturing human retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells on the apical side of a permeable Transwell insert and choroidal endothelial cells on the basolateral side (or in the lower chamber). Key readouts are:  
• Trans-epithelial/trans-endothelial electrical resistance (TEER), measured with an EVOM or similar voltohmmeter, to quantify barrier tightness in real time.  
• Paracellular permeability of FITC-dextran (4–70 kDa), assessed by sampling lower-chamber fluorescence, to gauge leakiness.  
• Optionally, cytokine and growth factor levels in apical/basal media (e.g., VEGF, PEDF, complement components) by ELISA or multiplex assay to capture RPE–endothelial cross-talk under basal or stress conditions (oxidative stress, complement activation, drusen extract exposure).  

Biomedical Evidence  
• Barrier Breakdown in Dry AMD: Clinical and histopathological studies (Bhutto et al. 2010; Leung et al. 2015) highlight early dysfunction of the outer blood–retina barrier—RPE tight junction loss, drusen-induced inflammation, choriocapillaris dropout—and link these to progression toward geographic atrophy.  
• RPE–Endothelial Signaling: RPE secretes factors (VEGF, PEDF, complement regulators) essential to choriocapillaris maintenance; endothelial cells deliver oxygen/nutrients and secrete anti-oxidants back to RPE. Disruption of this paracrine loop is central to RPE cell death and photoreceptor loss in dry AMD.  
• Pathogenic Triggers in Vitro: Oxidative stress (H2O2, blue light), complement dysregulation (C3a/C5a exposure), and drusen components (lipofuscin, ApoE) have been shown to impair TEER and elevate permeability in monolayer RPE models. A co-culture adds the endothelial dimension critical to disease physiology.  

Previous Use  
• Barrier-centric Screens: Similar Transwell co-cultures have been employed in proof-of-concept studies to test small-molecule Nrf2 activators, complement inhibitors, and anti-inflammatory agents for their ability to restore TEER and reduce FITC-dextran flux after oxidative or complement challenge (e.g., Sennlaub et al. 2014; Leung et al. 2015).  
• Mechanistic Insights: Bhutto et al. (2010) used RPE–endothelial co-cultures to show that RPE-derived VEGF is critical for endothelial survival, and that its dysregulation contributes to choriocapillaris atrophy. Leung et al. (2015) extended this by demonstrating that complement activation on RPE leads to secondary endothelial barrier disruption.  
• Lead Optimization: Early-stage drug discovery programs have adapted this assay to triage complement modulators and anti-oxidants based on their ability to preserve barrier metrics under stress, thereby linking molecular activity with tissue-relevant endpoints.  

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
• Physiological Relevance: Directly models outer blood–retina barrier and cell–cell cross-talk central to dry AMD pathogenesis.  
• Quantitative Readouts: TEER and FITC-dextran flux provide sensitive, high-throughput–compatible metrics of barrier integrity.  
• Flexibility: Can be combined with disease-relevant insults (drusen extracts, complement proteins, oxidative stress) and multiplexed on 24- or 96-well Transwells for moderate throughput.  

Weaknesses  
• Cell Source Variability: Primary human RPE and choroidal endothelial cells show donor heterogeneity and limited lifespan; iPSC-derived cells require extensive characterization.  
• Missing Immune Component: Lacks microglia/macrophages and systemic complement factors, which are key drivers of chronic inflammation in vivo.  
• Simplified Architecture: Does not replicate subretinal debris accumulation, Bruch’s membrane thickening, or photoreceptor interactions that contribute to geographic atrophy.  
• Readout Specificity: Changes in TEER may result from alterations in cell viability or morphology rather than true tight junction modulation, requiring orthogonal validation (immunostaining for ZO-1, claudin-19).  

Conclusion  
The RPE–Endothelial Co-Culture Barrier Test captures a core aspect of dry AMD—outer barrier breakdown driven by dysfunctional RPE–choroid signaling—and offers robust, quantitative assays for initial therapeutic screening. However, its predictive power for later-stage disease features (drusen accumulation, photoreceptor death, immune cell recruitment) is limited. Incorporating additional cell types (microglia), extracellular matrix components (aged Bruch’s membrane mimics), or complement source may enhance disease fidelity for advanced preclinical validation.
