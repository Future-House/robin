Assay Overview  
•  Disease aspect modeled: Chronic, low‐grade inflammation driven by drusen constituents in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), specifically activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome by cholesterol crystals or oxidized low‐density lipoprotein (oxLDL).  
•  Biological system: Human RPE cells (commonly ARPE-19 or primary/hiPSC-derived RPE).  
•  Stimuli: Preformed cholesterol crystals (50–200 µg/mL) or physiologically relevant concentrations of oxLDL (10–100 µg/mL).  
•  Readouts:  
   –  Caspase-1 enzymatic activity (e.g. FLICA fluorescent probe assay or Western blot for p20 caspase-1).  
   –  IL-1β (and optionally IL-18) release into culture supernatant (quantified by ELISA).  
   –  ASC speck formation (immunofluorescence) or NLRP3 oligomerization (cross-linking + immunoblot).  
   –  Cell viability/pyroptosis (e.g. LDH release or propidium iodide uptake).  
•  Workflow: 24–48 h pretreatment for “priming” (e.g. low-level TNFα or LPS), followed by 6–24 h drusen‐mimetic challenge; endpoints are harvested at 6–24 h after challenge.

Biomedical Evidence  
1.  Drusen composition & inflammation  
   –  Drusen, the hallmark extracellular deposits in dry AMD, contain cholesterol crystals and oxidized lipids known to act as damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). Human donor studies reveal cholesterol‐rich drusen and elevated IL-1β/IL-18 in AMD eyes (Anderson et al. 2002; Klein et al. 2008).  
2.  NLRP3 in AMD pathogenesis  
   –  Postmortem AMD retinas show upregulated NLRP3, ASC, and cleaved caspase-1 in RPE/choroid (Tarallo et al. 2012; Doyle et al. 2012).  
   –  Mouse models lacking NLRP3 or caspase-1 are protected from RPE degeneration in Alu RNA–induced and cholesterol crystal–induced AMD models (Tarallo et al. 2012; Mao et al. 2014).  
3.  Clinical correlations  
   –  Vitreous and serum levels of IL-1β and IL-18 correlate with AMD severity and progression (Tseng et al. 2013; Liao & Chen 2019), implicating inflammasome‐driven interleukins in disease activity.  
4.  Crosstalk with complement  
   –  Complement factor C1q and membrane attack complex can further prime NLRP3 activation, highlighting synergy between drusen‐driven complement dysregulation and inflammasome signaling (Kauppinen et al. 2016).

Previous Use  
•  Mao et al. 2014 established that cholesterol crystals induce NLRP3 activation in human RPE, recapitulating inflammasome‐dependent cell death seen in AMD models.  
•  Tarallo et al. 2012 used Alu RNA to trigger both priming and activation in RPE; subsequent caspase-1 assays were instrumental in linking Alu accumulation to inflammasome‐mediated degeneration.  
•  Employing this assay, several academic labs have screened small‐molecule NLRP3 inhibitors (e.g., in fluorescence‐based caspase‐1 assays) for their capacity to reduce IL-1β release from RPE (preclinical tool compounds, e.g. MCC950).  
•  Key discoveries:  
   –  Demonstration that two signals (priming + drusen‐mimetic) are required for robust inflammasome activation in RPE (Mao et al. 2014).  
   –  Identification of lysosomal rupture and cathepsin B release as upstream events in cholesterol crystal–driven NLRP3 activation (Duewell et al. 2010; extended to RPE).

Overall Evaluation  
Strengths  
•  Pathophysiological relevance: Directly models drusen‐associated DAMPs central to dry AMD.  
•  Functional endpoint: Quantitative, biochemical readouts (caspase-1, IL-1β) amenable to dose–response and high‐throughput adaptation.  
•  Flexibility: Can be coupled with co-culture (microglia/macrophages) or complement proteins to model multicellular interactions.  
•  Validated by in vivo genetics: Consistency between in vitro RPE responses and NLRP3 knockout phenotypes in animal models.

Weaknesses  
•  Cell model limitations: ARPE-19 may lack full RPE polarization/pigmentation; primary or iPSC-derived RPE are more resource‐intensive.  
•  Simplification of drusen: Real drusen are heterogeneous (lipids, proteins, complement, DNA/RNA); assay focuses on cholesterol/oxLDL only.  
•  Missing chronicity: Short‐term stimulation may not capture cumulative, low‐grade inflammation of AMD’s decades‐long progression.  
•  Crosstalk omission: Does not inherently model complement activation, extracellular matrix changes, or photoreceptor–RPE interactions that also drive pathology.  
•  Two-signal requirement: Requires an artificial priming step (e.g. LPS/TNFα) that may not fully mimic in situ RPE priming.

Conclusion  
Overall, the drusen component–driven NLRP3 activation assay offers a robust, disease‐relevant platform for initial screening of anti‐inflammasome therapeutics in dry AMD. To enhance translational value, it should be deployed in well-characterized primary or iPSC-RPE systems, and integrated with complementary assays capturing chronic oxidative stress, complement dysregulation, and multicellular interactions.
